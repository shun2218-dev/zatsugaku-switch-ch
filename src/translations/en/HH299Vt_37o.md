### Cold going in, cold coming out

When you get into a pool, the water feels cold. After swimming for a while, you get used to it. But when you climb out, you feel cold all over again.

A physics blog from the University of Waikato in New Zealand calls this experience "the swimming pool paradox." You're cold going in, and you're cold coming out. That hardly seems fair.

These two kinds of cold actually come from different mechanisms. Let's look at them one at a time.

### The chill in the water: how easily heat moves

First, the chill you feel when you get into the water.

According to the University of Waikato explanation, this is due to **thermal conduction**. Thermal conduction is the transfer of heat between things that are touching each other.

Water conducts heat far better than air does. So when you're surrounded by water, heat leaves your body faster than it would if you were surrounded by air at the same temperature. The explanation notes that water can feel cold when you get in even when it's warmer than the air.

We go into more detail on how materials that conduct heat well feel colder to the touch in "[Why does metal feel cold and wood feel warm?](/trivia/Xpgc8HdEB44/)"

### The chill after you get out: heat of vaporization

So what causes the cold after you get out of the water? That's the **heat of vaporization** we introduced in the video.

The dictionary defines the heat of vaporization as the amount of heat a liquid absorbs from outside when it becomes a gas. The absorbed heat isn't used to raise the temperature; it's used to turn the liquid into a gas.

An explanation from Osaka Kyoiku University describes this using the example of getting out of the bath. A liquid needs heat to evaporate. That heat is taken from whatever the liquid is touching. When your body is wet, the droplets on its surface evaporate while drawing away your body heat, and that's why you feel cold.

The University of Waikato explanation says the same thing. When you come out of the water, your body is wet. For that water to evaporate, energy is needed, and that energy comes from your own body.

### Turning water into gas takes a lot of heat

Just how large is the heat of vaporization?

According to the dictionary, turning 1 gram of water at 100°C into steam at the same temperature takes about 539 calories of heat.

The water on your skin isn't at 100°C. Even so, it still absorbs heat as it evaporates. Every time water on your skin evaporates, it carries heat away from your body.

### The same way sweat cools you down

In fact, our bodies use this mechanism all the time. It's called sweat.

A heatstroke document from Japan's Ministry of the Environment explains that when you sweat, the sweat evaporates and takes heat from the surface of the body, which lowers your body temperature as a result. When it's hot out, the heat of vaporization from evaporating sweat is mainly what brings body temperature down.

When you climb out of a pool, your body is in much the same state as if you were sweating all over. The mechanism that cools you on a hot day works strongly on a wet body, and you feel it as cold.

### Wind and humidity change how cold you get

In the video, we mentioned that wind makes you even colder.

The University of Waikato explanation notes that on hot days, especially when the humidity is low and there's a breeze, the water on your body evaporates quickly. The faster it evaporates, the more heat is taken away in a short time.

The Ministry of the Environment document describes the opposite case. When humidity is high or the wind is weak, sweat has a harder time evaporating, and the cooling effect of the heat of vaporization becomes smaller. That's why sweating on a muggy day doesn't cool you off.

Wind does one more thing. The same document lists "convection," which becomes more efficient with wind, as one of the ways the body releases heat. Wind doesn't just speed up evaporation; it also carries away the heat around your body.

We also cover the link between wind and evaporation in "[Why does laundry dry faster on windy days?](/trivia/4KEmTOiWH_M/)" and "[Why does the breeze from a fan feel cool?](/trivia/y8p4n1ioVgc/)"

### Drying off right away is the best fix

The Osaka Kyoiku University explanation opens with something kids often hear: "Dry off quickly or you'll catch a cold." If you stay wet, your body keeps getting colder.

The cause of the chill was water evaporating on your skin. If you wipe the water off with a towel, there's less water left to evaporate. And the heat taken from your body drops accordingly.

Wrapping yourself in a towel at the poolside makes perfect sense. Wipe off the water and block the wind. Those two steps go a long way toward easing the cold after you get out.
