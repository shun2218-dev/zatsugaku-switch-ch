### Earwax comes in two types

There are two types of earwax: the dry, flaky **dry type** and the sticky **wet type**.

When families talk about earwax, they sometimes find they're split: "Ours is like powder," "Ours is kind of moist." This isn't about how you clean your ears. It's a trait you're born with.

This has been known for a long time. According to an announcement from the Japan Science and Technology Agency (JST), earwax type is "one of the most easily recognizable traits, with one of the simplest patterns of inheritance." You can tell which type someone has at a glance, and the way it passes from parent to child is simple.

### It's decided by one letter in one gene

So which gene decides it? That was figured out in 2006, when a research team including Nagasaki University identified it as a gene called **ABCC11**.

A gene is a blueprint written in four kinds of letters (bases). At one particular spot in the ABCC11 gene, some people have G (guanine) and others have A (adenine). This kind of one-letter difference is called a **single nucleotide polymorphism** (SNP, an individual difference of just one letter).

People inherit one copy of each gene from their father and one from their mother, so there are three possible combinations:

- AA (both A): dry type
- GA (one G): wet type
- GG (both G): wet type

If you have even one G, you get the wet type. That means even if both parents have wet earwax, if they're both GA, their child can end up AA with dry earwax.

The research team confirmed this by actually examining the earwax of 126 people. All 88 people with dry earwax were AA. Of the 38 people with wet earwax, 37 were GG or GA.

### Even the one exception had a reason

Were there people with wet earwax who were AA, or the reverse?

According to JST's announcement, only one person with wet earwax was AA. In an earlier survey where people self-reported their earwax type, quite a few people's answers didn't match. So the team redid the study, this time checking people's earwax directly.

The paper also found a small number of people who were missing part of the ABCC11 gene, separate from this single spot. "Decided by one letter" is broadly correct, but in rare cases other changes are involved.

### What's different inside the ear

Earwax is a mix of secretions from **ceruminous glands** (a kind of apocrine gland, which are relatives of sweat glands) and **sebaceous glands** (glands that produce oil) in the skin of the ear canal. Shed skin and dust get mixed in too.

The ABCC11 gene is the blueprint for a "pump" protein that carries substances from inside a cell to the outside. When the research team looked into it, they found that the pump built from the A version of the blueprint was very weak at moving substances out.

A 2017 review article on earwax explains that this difference in the pump changes secretion from the apocrine glands, which is what splits earwax into the two types. In the video, we described it as "lots of oil means wet, little oil means dry." What's behind that is this difference in how secretion works.

### The dry type is common in East Asia

The ratio of dry to wet types varies a lot by region.

According to JST's announcement, 70 to 80% of people in Japan have the dry type. Meanwhile, nearly all people in Europe and Africa have the wet type.

According to the paper, the proportion of people carrying the A version is highest in the world among people in China and Korea. It gets lower the farther you go south, as well as east and west, from there. Some Indigenous peoples of North and South America also have the dry type.

The research team believes humans originally had wet earwax, and that the dry type spread from a small number of ancestors. Something as everyday as earwax can even offer clues about how humans migrated.

### Both types protect your ears

Neither type is better than the other.

According to the 2017 review, earwax moisturizes, cleans, and protects the skin of the ear canal. It also helps keep bacteria in check and keeps out water, insects, and dust.

The MSD Manual Consumer Version advises against trying to remove earwax yourself with cotton swabs, hairpins, or similar objects. Doing so can push the earwax deeper and risks damaging the eardrum.

If your ear feels blocked or you're having trouble hearing, it's safest to have the earwax removed by an ear, nose, and throat (ENT) doctor. The same manual also notes that some people, such as those with very sticky earwax, may need regular cleaning by a doctor.
