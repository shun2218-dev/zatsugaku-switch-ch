### Ocean waves come in two kinds: "wind waves" and "swell"

The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) explains ocean waves by dividing them into two broad types.

- **Wind waves**: Waves raised by the wind blowing at that spot, still growing as the wind pushes them. They're irregular and pointed, and when the wind is strong, whitecaps form.
- **Swell**: The waves left over once they're no longer being built up by the wind, either because they've traveled to where the wind isn't blowing or because the wind has died down. They're regular and rounded, with long crests stretching out side by side.

The waves in the video that grow before a typhoon arrives are this second type: swell.

### What builds waves: wind strength, duration, and distance

According to NOAA (the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration), the size of waves depends on how strong the wind is, how long it keeps blowing, and how much open water it blows across in the same direction (the **fetch**). The stronger the wind and the longer it blows, the bigger the waves.

The JMA says that as long as the wind is moving faster than the waves, the waves keep getting pushed and keep growing. According to the JMA, near the center of a typhoon, waves can exceed 10 meters (about 33 feet). That's what the video means by "the typhoon's strong winds keep pushing on the sea surface."

### "Doyo-nami": waves that reach the coast before the typhoon

The JMA names "doyo-nami" as the classic example of swell. These are waves born around a typhoon thousands of kilometers to the south that travel all the way to Japan's coast. For generations, people in Japan have used this name for the high waves that pound Pacific-facing beaches from summer into fall, as a warning to watch out for them. (The name comes from "doyo," a traditional seasonal period around midsummer.)

According to the JMA, doyo-nami travel very fast, sometimes at more than 50 km/h. When a typhoon south of Japan has its path blocked by the Pacific High and creeps slowly northward over the southern ocean, the swell can reach the coast several days before the typhoon does. The video's line, "these waves can travel at more than 50 km/h, and when a typhoon moves slowly, they spread ahead of the storm itself," matches this explanation.

NOAA also notes that fully developed ocean waves outrun the storm that created them. As swell travels long distances, its wavelength gets longer and its height gets lower.

### Why it gets "suddenly" higher near the shore

Swell that has come from far away should have gotten lower, so there's a reason it suddenly grows tall at the coast.

According to the JMA, swell has a longer wavelength and period than wind waves, so in shallow places like breakwaters, rocky shores, and beaches, it's affected by the seabed and tends to grow taller (this is called **shoaling**). Once the water gets shallower than a certain depth, wave height increases sharply. NOAA explains that swell approaching the shore can grow to as much as about 1.5 times its offshore height before it breaks and crashes in.

Another thing worth knowing is how much wave heights vary. The "wave height" in weather forecasts is the **significant wave height**, which is the average height of the highest one-third of waves. According to the JMA, if you watch 100 waves (roughly 10 to 20 minutes), the tallest will be about 1.5 times the significant wave height, and over 1,000 waves (roughly 2 to 3 hours), it can be close to twice that. In other words, waves taller than the forecast number can show up every so often without warning.

### Even when it's sunny, stay away from the shore

The JMA warns that even when a typhoon is far out at sea well south of Japan, swell can travel all the way to the Pacific coast, and doyo-nami can roll in even in calm weather.

When a typhoon or a strengthening low-pressure system is approaching and the waves are high, accidents in which people fishing, surfing, or just going to look at the sea are swept away by high waves happen almost every year. The JMA urges people not to go near the coast unnecessarily when high wave warnings or advisories are in effect. Even if the sky is clear, the sea may already be feeling the typhoon's effects.
