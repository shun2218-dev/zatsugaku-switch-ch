### Doctors call it the "red reflex"

The red-eye effect from the video has a medical name: the **red reflex**.

According to the American Academy of Ophthalmology, the flash lights up the retina, which is rich in blood vessels, and that reflection travels back to the camera, so the eye appears red. When the video says "the blood vessels inside the eye show up in the picture," this is exactly what it means.

The key point is that **red is a good sign**. If a child is looking straight at the lens and both eyes glow red in the same way, it's likely that nothing is blocking light from reaching the retina and the eyes are healthy.

### What you might notice when the eyes don't show red

The Academy encourages people to make use of flash photos because of the opposite cases. A white, yellow, or black reflection may be a sign of an eye condition.

- **White reflection**: In many cases it's nothing abnormal; the child may simply have been looking off to the side. But if the white covers most of the pupil, possible causes listed include cataracts, retinal detachment, eye infections, and **retinoblastoma**, a rare eye cancer in children
- **Yellow reflection**: Possibly **Coats disease**, in which blood vessels in the retina become twisted and leak. It's more common in boys under 10 and usually affects one eye. In photos, it's said to be hard to tell apart from retinoblastoma
- **Different reflections in each eye**: If one eye is red but the other is dark or shows nothing, it could be strabismus (misaligned eyes). The earlier treatment starts, the more effective it is
- **The most common cause**: Refractive errors such as nearsightedness, farsightedness, and astigmatism, which can usually be corrected with glasses

That's a list of scary-sounding conditions, but it's worth keeping in mind that the Academy itself writes that a white reflection is often nothing abnormal. There's no need to worry excessively; the idea is simply to get it checked if you notice something.

### Sometimes you should turn "red-eye reduction" off

The red-eye reduction feature mentioned at the end of the video is handy for taking nice-looking photos. But if your goal is checking someone's eyes, it actually gets in the way.

The American Academy of Ophthalmology lists four conditions for taking photos suited to this kind of check:

1. The child is looking straight at the camera lens
2. The flash is turned ON
3. The background is fairly dark
4. **Red-eye reduction is turned OFF**

Red-eye reduction suppresses red eye by shining light beforehand to make the pupils smaller. In other words, it erases the very reflection you're trying to look at.

### What to do if something looks off

The Academy's advice is clear: if you notice an unusual reflection, **bring that photo** to your child's pediatrician or a pediatric ophthalmologist. There's no need to wait for symptoms to appear.

They also explain why. Young children don't have trouble as long as one eye sees normally, so they won't complain about vision problems on their own. Pediatricians are recommended to check the red reflex with an ophthalmoscope at every checkup.

### How this differs from cats' eyes glowing green

It's the same "reflection from the back of the eye," yet cats and dogs glow green or yellow while people show up red. The difference is whether there's a reflective layer.

According to a 2004 review in comparative morphology, primates (including humans) lack a reflective layer called the tapetum. In cats and dogs, the tapetum bounces light back, which produces its color. In the human eye, light simply hits the blood-vessel-rich retina and comes back. That's why the color is the red of blood.

It's not that human eyes "don't glow"; it's that without a reflective layer, the color of blood shows through as it is.
