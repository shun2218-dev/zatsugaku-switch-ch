### The original purpose: stopping coin shaving

The Japan Mint's coin Q&A sums up the history neatly.

> Coin ridges were originally added "to prevent the outer edges from being shaved off, in the case of coins made of precious metals such as gold and silver" or "to show that the coin was the highest denomination at the time."

Gold and silver coins were valuable because of the metal itself. So if you shaved a tiny bit off the edge, you could keep that metal. Keep spending the shaved coin, and the difference was pure profit.

If the edge has ridges carved into it, you can tell at a glance that it's been shaved. If the ridges are broken, nobody trusts that coin anymore. The idea wasn't to make shaving "impossible" but to make it "obvious."

The other reason was "to show that it's the highest denomination." In other words, an identifying mark on the most valuable coin.

### There was a time when 10-yen coins had ridges

That "mark of the highest denomination" reason left behind an interesting bit of history.

> Very occasionally you may come across a 10-yen coin with ridges. These are 10-yen coins issued between Showa 26 (1951) and Showa 33 (1958). When the 10-yen coin was introduced in Showa 26 (1951) as the highest denomination at the time, it was given ridges. However, a 100-yen coin (silver) was issued in Showa 32 (1957), and because it was difficult to tell apart from this 100-yen coin, the design was changed to one without ridges in Showa 34 (1959).

These are the so-called "giza-ju" ("ridged tens").

In 1951, the 10-yen coin debuted as the highest denomination of its day and got ridges as a mark of that. But in 1957 the 100-yen silver coin came out, and the two became hard to tell apart. So in 1959, the ridges were dropped from the 10-yen coin.

In short, **the ridges that once marked the highest denomination got in the way of telling coins apart, so they were removed.** This was the turning point where the purpose of ridges shifted from "showing value" to "distinguishing from other coins."

### Today's purposes: telling coins apart and preventing counterfeits

> Today, they are added "to distinguish them from other coins" and "as a counterfeiting countermeasure."

The metal in modern coins isn't worth their face value, so there's no incentive to shave them. That narrowed the purpose down to two things.

The Mint gives the same explanation for holes.

> The reason some coins have holes (officially written with the character 孔) is, like the ridges, "to distinguish them from other types of coins" and "as a counterfeiting countermeasure," but another major reason is "to save raw materials."

The holes in the 5-yen and 50-yen coins also help people tell them apart by touch.

### The number of ridges is kept secret

Here's a small detail that makes a lot of sense.

> Since one of the reasons ridges are added to coins is "as a counterfeiting countermeasure," the Japan Mint does not disclose the number of ridges.

The ridge count is kept secret to prevent counterfeiting. If you don't know the exact number, it's harder to make ridges that match the real thing.

By the way, the Mint doesn't disclose its production costs either.

> We do not disclose the production cost of coins, in order to maintain public confidence in currency and because it is thought that doing so could encourage counterfeiting.

### The 500-yen coin's diagonal ridges were a world first

Now for the modern technology.

> One of the reasons ridges are added to coins is "as a counterfeiting countermeasure." The diagonal reeding on the 500-yen coin is one such anti-counterfeiting technology; angling the ridges diagonally improves resistance to counterfeiting. In addition, the 500-yen coin was the world's first mass-produced coin to feature diagonal reeding.

**A world first.**

Why are diagonal ridges harder to fake? Vertical ridges can be made by creating a die and stamping the coin out. But diagonal ridges can't be made the same way. The manufacturing process itself becomes harder.

### And then, "irregular diagonal reeding"

There's an even more advanced technology, too.

> On the 500-yen coin commemorating the 60th anniversary of the enforcement of the Local Autonomy Act, some of the diagonal ridges were given a shape different from the others, providing even greater resistance to counterfeiting. This irregular diagonal reeding is a technology developed independently by the Japan Mint.

The idea is to **change the shape of just some** of the diagonal ridges. This is "irregular diagonal reeding," and the Mint describes it as a technology it developed on its own.

The new 500-yen coin issued in November 2021 uses this irregular diagonal reeding. If you look at the edge, the ridges are shaped differently in just four spots: top, bottom, left and right.

### Three stages of edge ridges

To sum up, here's how the purpose of the ridges has changed.

| Era | Purpose |
| --- | --- |
| The age of precious-metal coins | Prevent shaving of the outer edge |
| Modern era (the giza-ju years) | Show that a coin is the highest denomination |
| Today | Distinguish from other coins / prevent counterfeiting |

What began as a defense when coins were valuable for their metal became an identifying mark, and today it's the arena for a technological race against counterfeiters. Those tiny grooves on the edge carry the story of how the meaning of money has changed.
