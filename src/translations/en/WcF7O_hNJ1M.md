### The three stages before a smell reaches you

Broadly speaking, there are three stages before you actually smell something.

1. Odor substances are released from their source into the air
2. The air carries those substances to your nose
3. Your nose detects them

The reason smells seem fainter on windy winter days is thought to be that cold and wind play a part in every one of these stages. The video covered stages 1 and 2, but let's go through them in order, including stage 3, "how sensitive your nose is."

### Higher temperatures make smells easier to release

First, the stage where substances come out of the odor source.

A research group led by Sagae placed flowers of a petunia relative (*Petunia axillaris*) at 20°C, 25°C, 30°C, and 35°C (68°F, 77°F, 86°F, and 95°F) and measured how much scent the flowers gave off.

They found that as the temperature rose, the amount of scent compounds stored inside the flowers went down. Even so, from 20°C to 30°C, the amount of scent released from the flowers into the air **went up**. According to the paper, this is because higher temperatures made the scent compounds evaporate more easily, and that increase outweighed the drop in the amount stored inside.

On the other hand, scent emission peaked at 30°C and dropped at 35°C. That's because a flower's scent depends not only on how easily the compounds evaporate, but also on how much of them the flower produces.

The video's explanation that "the warmer it is, the more easily smells are released" holds true for evaporation of the substances themselves. But for smells made by living things, like plant fragrances, it isn't necessarily true that "the hotter it gets, the stronger the smell."

### Your nose's sensitivity may also change with the air

This wasn't covered in the video, but the nose itself, the part that receives smells, may also be affected by the surrounding air.

Kuehn and colleagues had 75 healthy people sit in a chamber where air pressure and humidity could be adjusted, and tested how faint a smell they could still detect. They found that **people could detect fainter smells in humid air than in relatively dry air**. They also found that under low air pressure, the ability to detect faint smells was lower than under high pressure.

However, in a study by Philpott and colleagues that tested 10 people 10 times each over 10 weeks, differences in the exam room's temperature and humidity had no clear effect on how concentrated a smell needed to be for people to detect it.

Whether dry winter air lowers the sensitivity of our noses is something studies disagree on, so it can't be said for sure yet.

### Wind stirs up smells and dilutes them

Next, the stage where odor substances are carried to your nose.

The Queensland Government in Australia explains, regarding air pollutants, that when strong, turbulent winds blow, pollutants disperse quickly and their concentrations drop. Conversely, when there's no wind and the air isn't moving, pollutants can't disperse and their concentrations build up.

Odor substances also drift through the air, so the same reasoning applies. On days with strong wind, odor substances scatter quickly too, and they tend to be diluted by the time they reach your nose.

Put the other way around, even on a cold day, if there's no wind and the air is stagnant, smells won't get diluted as easily. Rather than "smells are fainter in winter," it seems more accurate to say that smells tend to seem fainter on days when "cold" and "strong wind" come together.

### Three reasons working together

To sum up, possible reasons smells seem fainter on windy winter days are:

- The cold tends to reduce the amount of substances released from odor sources
- Strong wind scatters and dilutes the substances in the air
- Dry air may also lower the sensitivity of your nose (though studies disagree)

Just as the video says "is thought to," it's probably not any single cause, but several conditions overlapping that make smells "seem fainter."
