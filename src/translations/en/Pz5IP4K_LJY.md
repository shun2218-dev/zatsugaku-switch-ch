### There are only three pigments

The Forestry and Forest Products Research Institute's explanation starts by narrowing down the number of pigments.

> The change in color of a plant's green leaves is called autumn coloring. In most cases, leaves turn red or yellow. This is because the main pigments plants have are three: chlorophyll, carotenoids, and anthocyanins. They are green, yellow, and red, respectively.

Green, yellow, and red. Combinations of just these three are enough to explain the colors of the mountains in autumn.

Two of them are already in the leaf starting in spring.

> Chloroplasts, which carry out photosynthesis, contain chlorophyll and carotenoids.

Both green chlorophyll and yellow carotenoids are present in green summer leaves. But there's overwhelmingly more chlorophyll, so the yellow can't be seen.

### Yellow is "subtraction," red is "addition"

This is the crucial difference. First, yellow.

> Yellowing is when chlorophyll breaks down in old leaves and the yellow of carotenoids, which had been hidden by the green, appears.

The green simply drains away. The yellow was there from the start. Ginkgo leaves turn yellow not because they make a new color, but as a result of clearing out the green.

So what about red?

> Reddening is when an abscission layer forms in places such as the leaf stalk of an aging leaf, blocking the movement of substances between the leaf and the branch, and anthocyanins are made from the products of photosynthesis that have built up in the leaf.

This isn't subtraction. A partition called the "abscission layer" forms between the leaf and the branch, cutting off the flow of materials. Using the products of photosynthesis left stranded in the leaf, the leaf **newly synthesizes** a red pigment called anthocyanin. In other words, it's addition.

Both get lumped together as "fall colors," but yellow is what's left after chlorophyll disappears, while red is a color made after autumn arrives. The processes are completely different.

The explanation also gives typical examples.

> The classic example of yellowing is the ginkgo, and of reddening, the Japanese maple. Persimmon trees develop a mottled pattern of green, red, and yellow.

The persimmon's mottling means that within a single leaf, "parts where green remains," "parts where yellow has appeared," and "parts that have made red" are all progressing at the same time.

### Changing color is part of getting ready to drop

The timing of the color change has meaning.

> These color changes generally happen before the leaves fall. Trees that shed all their leaves at once have a season of red (or yellow) leaves. It comes in autumn or at the start of the dry season.

Autumn coloring happens at the point of a leaf's "last job before it's done." The forming of the abscission layer is itself preparation for leaf drop. Because a partition forms to cut the leaf off, substances have nowhere to go and become anthocyanin. Autumn coloring is part of the same chain of events as leaf drop.

As the phrase "in autumn or at the start of the dry season" suggests, autumn isn't the only trigger. Trees that shed their leaves in the dry season also change color before dropping them.

### Even evergreens change color, if you look closely

What about trees that don't shed their leaves all at once?

> Evergreen trees usually don't drop their leaves all at once, so it isn't noticeable, but in trees such as mokkoku, you can find red or yellow leaves somewhere in the crown all year round.

Evergreens replace their leaves too. It's just that the timing is scattered, so it never becomes a seasonal sight. If you come across a mokkoku (Japanese ternstroemia), there should be red or yellow leaves mixed in somewhere on the tree.

### Red spring leaves may be sunscreen

Finally, the explanation adds a surprising note.

> There are also plants, such as Japanese photinia, whose new leaves turn red. This red is also the color of anthocyanins and is thought to play roles such as protecting young leaves from ultraviolet light.

Japanese photinia, often used for hedges, puts out bright red new shoots in spring. That's the same anthocyanin as in autumn leaves, but its job is the exact opposite. Rather than the color of a leaf at its end, it's thought to be sunscreen protecting newborn leaves from UV light.

This also offers a hint about what autumn coloring means. The fact that leaves about to fall go out of their way to make red pigment could suggest it's not just a dumping ground for waste but may actually serve some purpose. As the Forestry and Forest Products Research Institute's explanation says "is thought to," the meaning of autumn coloring itself is still being researched.
