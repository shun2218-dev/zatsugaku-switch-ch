### Only "some" plants close their leaves at night

The movement of leaves folding up or standing upright at night is called **nyctinasty** (sometimes called "sleep movement"). It's most common in the legume family, with classic examples including the silk tree (mimosa tree), the sensitive plant, and clover. Outside the legumes, you can also see it in wood sorrels and in Calathea and Maranta, which are popular houseplants.

That said, **not every houseplant closes its leaves**. Plenty of plants, like pothos and monstera, look almost the same at night as they do during the day. The video's title says "houseplants" broadly, but it's more accurate to read it as "plants of the type that close their leaves."

### Water moving back and forth inside the pulvinus

Many plants that show nyctinasty have a slightly swollen section at the base of each leaf called the **pulvinus**. It's packed with "motor cells" that can swell and shrink.

When potassium ions and chloride ions flow out of these cells, water follows them out, and the cells shrink. When ions and water flow back in, the cells swell. By switching which side of the pulvinus swells and which side shrinks (top versus bottom), the plant raises or lowers its leaves. In 2018, a group including researchers from Tohoku University announced that they had found the "passageways" (ion channels) responsible for moving these ions in the rain tree (Samanea saman).

Some plants don't have a pulvinus at all, and instead move their leaves through differences in how fast the upper and lower surfaces of the leaf grow.

### The signal comes from an internal clock

The timing of leaves opening and closing isn't decided just by the surroundings getting dark. A **roughly 24-hour rhythm inside the plant (its internal clock)** switches the motor cells' activity according to the time of day. That's why, even if you keep the plant in a room that's dark all the time, its leaves will keep opening and closing at about the same time every day for a while.

In fact, the very idea of an internal clock in living things began in the 18th century with observations of this leaf movement. The motion of plant leaves sparked a major discovery in biology.

### "Why do they close?" is still a mystery

The video said it helps them "avoid losing heat and being eaten by insects," but these are just some of the leading hypotheses so far. Researchers have also proposed ideas like these:

- Reducing heat lost to the cold night sky (Darwin's theory)
- Making it easier for water on the leaf surface to run off
- Preventing moonlight from throwing off the plant's sense of "how long the night is"
- Folding up the leaves so nocturnal insects have a harder time eating them

However, there's at least one report that, when actually measured, the effect on leaf temperature was tiny, less than 1°C (about 1.8°F), and none of these theories is conclusive yet.

Also, although we describe it as "sleeping" or "resting," it isn't the same as sleep in animals. Moving the leaves requires cells to spend energy transporting ions, so you could even say it's the plant diligently "working" every night.
