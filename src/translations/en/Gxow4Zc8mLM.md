### The star of the smell isn’t the soil, it’s microbes

Let’s start by sorting out the names. Japan’s National Diet Library’s Collaborative Reference Database has an entry on exactly this question.

> It was found that “the smell when rain starts to fall” is called “petrichor,” and “the smell after rain” is called “geosmin.”

Geosmin is a substance made by microbes such as actinomycetes that live in the soil. In other words, the smell comes not from the soil itself but from living things in the soil. How faint it can be and still catch the human nose is covered in [another article](/trivia/SpZP_ivxm6U/).

The question here is a different one. Geosmin is in the soil on sunny days, too. So why do we smell it so strongly **only after rain**?

### Raindrops “launch” the smell into the air

In 2015, an MIT research team captured that moment with high-speed cameras. Here’s how MIT News described it:

> Using high-speed cameras, the researchers observed that when a raindrop hits a porous surface, it traps tiny air bubbles at the point of contact. As in a glass of champagne, the bubbles then shoot upward, ultimately bursting from the drop in a fizz of aerosols.

In other words: when a raindrop hits a porous surface, it traps small air bubbles where it makes contact. Like in a glass of champagne, the bubbles race upward and finally burst out of the drop, spraying a fizz of tiny particles.

The paper’s figure caption sums up this process in three stages:

> Aerosol generation after drop impingement on porous media is a three-step process, consisting of bubble formation, bubble growth, and bubble bursting.

Bubbles form, bubbles grow, bubbles burst. Those are the three steps.

The researchers also point out what the scattered particles carry:

> The researchers suspect that in natural environments, aerosols may carry aromatic elements, along with bacteria and viruses stored in soil.

In nature, these particles may carry scent compounds stored in the soil, and even bacteria and viruses along with them.

So the after-rain smell isn’t something that just “drifts up from the soil on its own.” Each raindrop is flinging scent-laden particles up into the air.

### A gentle drizzle smells stronger than a downpour

This is a finding that matches everyday experience. The team ran experiments changing the intensity of the rain.

> In separate experiments, the team deposited single drops of water on each surface, simulating various intensities of rainfall by adjusting the height from which the drops were released. The higher the droplet’s release, the faster its ultimate speed.

They recreated different rain intensities by changing the drop height, and here’s what they found:

> From their experiments, the team observed that more aerosols were produced in light and moderate rain, while far fewer aerosols were released during heavy rain.

Light and moderate rain produced more particles, while heavy rain produced far fewer.

If a drop falls too fast, the water spreads out across the surface before it has a chance to trap and grow bubbles. Producing the particles that carry the smell takes just the right speed.

Come to think of it, few of us remember noticing a strong “earthy smell” after a typhoon-level downpour. That scent rises after a summer evening shower or a soft, passing drizzle.

### And then the wind carries it

MIT News wraps it up in one sentence:

> These aerosols may be released during light or moderate rainfall, and then spread via gusts of wind.

They’re released during light or moderate rain, then spread by gusts of wind.

The team also came up with a formula to predict how much is released. They say it can be calculated from two things: “the velocity of the raindrop and the permeability of the contact surface.” That also means it’s unlikely to happen on surfaces that don’t let water through, like asphalt, and happens readily on surfaces with gaps, like dry soil or fallen leaves.

So we can list the conditions for a strong smell: after a stretch of dry days, rain of moderate intensity falls on a place with soil or fallen leaves, and a breeze passes through. On days when all four line up, the smell reaches us at its richest.

Youngsoo Joung, who led the research, described the significance of the discovery like this:

> Until now, people didn’t know that aerosols could be generated from raindrops on soil.

Until 2015, no one knew that raindrops could produce particles from soil. It’s a pretty fun story that for a smell everyone knows, only the way it travels remained a mystery for so long.
