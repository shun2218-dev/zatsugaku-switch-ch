### Sunlight is measured as two separate types

The Japan Meteorological Agency observes the sunlight reaching the ground by clearly dividing it into two parts.

- **Direct solar radiation**: "solar radiation that reaches the ground directly from the sun's photosphere"
- **Diffuse solar radiation**: "solar radiation that is scattered and reflected by atmospheric components and arrives from all directions of the sky"

Add the two together and you get the total amount of light the ground receives (global solar radiation).

The shadow question can be restated as a question about the **ratio** between these two. On sunny days, direct radiation dominates, so light comes from one direction. On cloudy days, direct radiation drops to nearly zero and only diffuse radiation remains. By definition, diffuse radiation arrives "from all directions of the sky," so there's simply no direction for a shadow to fall.

So what the video described as light that "scatters in all directions" actually has a proper name and is carefully measured.

### Do shadows "disappear" or just "blur"?

Strictly speaking, shadows don't drop to zero on cloudy days.

How sharp a shadow's edge looks depends on the apparent size of the light source. The smaller and more point-like the source, the sharper the edge; the larger and more spread out the source, the blurrier the edge. On cloudy days, **the entire sky becomes the light source**, so the blurring at the edges becomes extreme and the outline can no longer be made out. That's what's really going on.

In fact, even on cloudy days a faint darker area remains right at your feet and where objects touch the ground. Thinking of it not as "no shadow" but as "a shadow spread out until it's extremely faint" matches what you actually see.

### No shadow, but you still get sunburned

This is the most practically important part. The Japan Meteorological Agency's page "Clouds and Ultraviolet Radiation" says:

> Even when clouds cover almost the entire sky, the UV Index is about 80–90% of clear-sky levels under thin clouds, while under overcast skies it is about 60% of clear-sky levels.

It adds that "when it is raining, it drops to about 30% of clear-sky levels." In other words, on a cloudy day, even when direct radiation is blocked so much that no shadows form, UV still reaches you at 60% of clear-sky levels.

There's also an exception worth keeping in mind. The same page notes that "depending on cloud conditions, if you are in direct sunlight, the UV Index can be higher than on a clear day even when there are relatively many clouds." When clouds are scattered around, the light they scatter toward you adds to the total.

These figures are based on UV Index observations made in Sapporo, Tsukuba, Kagoshima, and Naha from 1997 to 2010 (for Kagoshima, from 1997 to March 2005).
