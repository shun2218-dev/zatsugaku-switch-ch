### Hard surfaces bounce sound back

City sounds don't just travel straight from their source to your ears. They hit hard, smooth surfaces like roads, building walls, and windows, bounce off, and reach you after reflecting again and again.

The general hum of a city is full of these bounced-back sounds. It's also why your voice echoes so much in a tunnel or an empty room: hard surfaces reflect sound.

### Fresh snow is "full of gaps"

Freshly fallen snow is made of snow crystals loosely piled up, with lots of air trapped between the grains. Ice itself is hard, but most of a layer of fresh snow is actually empty space.

When sound waves get into these gaps, the air inside is pushed back and forth. Air vibrating in those narrow spaces rubs against the surrounding ice grains, and that friction turns part of the sound's energy into heat. The deeper the sound travels into the gaps, the weaker it gets, so less of it bounces back.

This is the same mechanism that lets glass wool in soundproof-room walls, or a thick, plush carpet, absorb sound. [A wool sweater keeps you warm thanks to the air between its fibers](/trivia/MKfS0AIVDhM/), and in sound-absorbing materials, too, the "air in the gaps" plays the starring role.

### Measured in Yamagata 70 years ago

The fact that snow absorbs sound has actually been measured.

In 1954, Minoru Takada and colleagues at the Faculty of Engineering, Yamagata University, published a paper titled "On the Sound Absorption Coefficient of Snow and Sound Propagation over a Snow Surface" in the Journal of the Acoustical Society of Japan. They measured how much sound snow absorbs, and also studied how sound travels over snow under various conditions: soft fresh snow, old hardened snow, frozen snow, and while snow was falling.

The results were compared with bare ground. According to the paper's abstract, **over freshly fallen snow, the snow absorbed quite a lot of sound, so sound became considerably weaker beyond about 200 meters from the source**.

### Packed or frozen snow loses the quiet

The same paper showed another important point. **Over hardened or frozen snow, sound weakened about as much as it did with no snow at all.**

When the gaps get crushed or sealed by ice, there's nowhere for sound to go in. The surface becomes hard and starts reflecting sound.

So the "quiet of a snowy day" is strongest on the morning right after a snowfall. Once people and cars pack the snow down, or the surface freezes over the following days, the city's usual echoes come back.

On snowy days there are also often fewer people out and fewer cars on the road, so it seems likely that having fewer sources of noise adds to the quiet as well.

### Inside snow, voices barely get through

Snow's ability to weaken sound is even stronger inside the snow itself.

Ken-Ichi Sakakibara and colleagues at the Health Sciences University of Hokkaido studied whether searchers' voices could reach someone buried in an avalanche. According to their report for a government-funded research grant (KAKENHI), in packed snow, sound was weakened by about 50 decibels at a depth of 30 centimeters, and it was "nearly impossible" for conversation above the snow surface to reach a buried person.

On the other hand, when the snow surface itself was made to vibrate, by tapping it with a ski pole or stomping on it, sound reached a depth of 30 centimeters at a level 20 decibels above the quietest sound a person can hear. The researchers concluded that to let a buried person know searchers are there, directly vibrating the snow surface works better than calling out.

The same property of snow that hushes a city can be life-or-death knowledge in the mountains.
