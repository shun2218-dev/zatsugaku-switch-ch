### Fog is defined as "visibility under 1 km"

Let's start with the Japan Meteorological Agency's forecast terminology.

> Fog: A state in which visibility is less than 1 km due to tiny suspended water droplets.

Visibility under 1 km. Anything thinner has a different name.

> Mist: A state in which visibility is at least 1 km but less than 10 km due to tiny suspended water droplets or moist fine particles.

River fog covers only a small area, so whether it gets recorded as "fog" in weather observations depends on the location. But the reason it looks white is the same: fine water droplets floating in the air scatter light.

### Water cools slowly, air cools quickly

The key condition for river fog is the difference in how water and air cool down.

The JMA defines the process by which the ground cools at night:

> Radiative cooling: A drop in temperature as heat from the ground surface is lost through radiation.

On clear nights with little wind, the ground and air shed heat out into space and cool down. But water doesn't cool easily. Water has a high specific heat, and in a river, more water keeps flowing in from upstream. If the river is deep, it takes a long time for the whole thing to cool.

So by early morning, here's the situation:

- **Air**: Chilled way down by radiative cooling
- **River water**: Still holding on to the previous day's temperature

Just above the surface, water vapor leaves the warm water. But the moment that vapor meets the cold air, it has nowhere to go.

Here's the JMA's definition:

> Dew point temperature: The temperature at which relative humidity reaches 100% when the temperature is lowered while keeping water vapor pressure constant. The temperature at which water vapor in the air condenses to form dew.

The vapor rising off the water immediately drops below its dew point in the cold air and turns back into droplets. Those droplets become fog.

### That's why it forms "in the shape of the river"

River fog forms a band along the river because the source of the water vapor is limited to the river itself.

Vapor is supplied only directly above the water, so condensation happens only there. Move a little away, and there's no vapor supply, so no fog forms.

Seen from above, the river's shape shows up as a white ribbon. When fog traces the course of a river through a valley, it's a weather phenomenon literally drawing the landscape.

### The seasons and times when conditions line up

Here are the conditions under which river fog is commonly seen:

| Condition | Reason |
| --- | --- |
| Clear nights | Without clouds, radiative cooling works effectively |
| Light winds | Strong winds mix the air and erase the temperature difference |
| Fall through winter | The season when air temperature drops far below water temperature |
| Dawn | The coldest time of day |
| Downstream of dams or lakes | Water with a steady, higher temperature flows in |

River fog is common below dams because the released water comes from deep in the reservoir and stays warm even in winter. The bigger the gap between water and air temperature, the thicker the fog.

On the flip side, once the sun rises and the air warms up, the fog disappears. As the air temperature approaches the water temperature, the vapor stops condensing. Morning river fog vanishes quickly because this temperature gap narrows.

### The same setup as "bathtub steam"

For an everyday example, it's just like taking a bath on a cold day.

Steam rises from a hot bath because the vapor coming off the water meets the cold bathroom air and turns back into droplets. If you warm up the bathroom first, the steam doesn't appear.

River fog is the same phenomenon, with the river as the bathtub and the winter morning air as the cold bathroom. The scale is completely different, but what's happening is the same. Whenever you have "warm water surface + cold air," something white will always rise.

[The white steam rising from roads after a summer downpour](/trivia/mNKiiwcG2Ic/) involves the same combination of a hot surface and cooler air. The direction is reversed (in river fog the water is warm; on roads the pavement is hot), but they share one thing: water vapor cooling and turning back into droplets.
