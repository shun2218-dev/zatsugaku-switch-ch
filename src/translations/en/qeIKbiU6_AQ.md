### The smell comes from bacterial byproducts

The smell of indoor-dried laundry isn't the smell of detergent or tap water. It's a substance made by bacteria.

Research by Kao identifies a fatty acid called 4-methyl-3-hexenoic acid (4M3H) as a main component of the musty "damp laundry" smell. It also reports that bacteria of the genus Moraxella are heavily involved in producing this compound.

Moraxella bacteria are found widely around us, including on human skin and in the mouth. They're nothing unusual.

### Bacteria grow on nutrients left in your laundry

This is where an explanation from the Japan Soap and Detergent Association comes in.

> Laundry carries dirt and odors such as skin oils, sweat, food spills, mud, and cosmetics.

Skin oils and sweat. These become food for bacteria.

> To thoroughly remove these various kinds of dirt in a single wash, it's important to combine four things in the right balance: (1) the washing machine (mechanical action), (2) water, (3) the laundry, and (4) detergent (surfactants).

When those four get out of balance, dirt is left behind. The leftover skin oils become food for bacteria.

> However, when the machine is overloaded, there's also more dirt, so there aren't enough surfactant molecules. As a result, not enough micelles form in the water, and cleaning power drops.

Overloading the washer works in favor of creating the cause of the smell.

### Slow drying gives bacteria more time to work

This is the heart of the indoor-drying problem.

It takes time for bacteria to multiply and produce odor compounds. The longer laundry stays wet and warm, the longer the bacteria can stay active.

- **Drying outdoors (sunny, breezy)**: dries in 1–3 hours
- **Drying indoors (no air movement, fairly humid)**: can take half a day or more

That difference determines how much smell you get.

So the number one fix is "dry it fast." The fact that [laundry dries faster on windy days](/trivia/4KEmTOiWH_M/) translates directly into odor prevention.

- Blow air on it with a fan or air circulator
- Use a dehumidifier
- Hang items with space between them
- Use a bathroom dryer (a ventilation-and-heating system common in Japanese bathrooms)

Simply raising the room temperature can sometimes backfire. Bacteria are more active when it's warm, so "warm and humid" is the worst possible condition.

### Why rewashing doesn't get rid of it

Sometimes, even after you rewash clothes that picked up that indoor-drying smell, the smell comes back.

Kao's research has made a discovery that helps explain why. In a 2022 announcement, it reported finding clumps of bacteria (**biofilms**) stuck to towel fibers. Moraxella bacteria, which are commonly found on clothing with that damp-laundry smell, were reportedly detected at a high rate.

A biofilm is a state in which bacteria gather inside a film-like structure they produce themselves. It's the same kind of thing as dental plaque or the slime in a drain.

When bacteria are covered by this film, it's hard for detergent and water to reach them. So a normal wash doesn't fully remove them, and the remaining bacteria produce the smell again the next time the fabric gets wet.

A 2024 announcement reported that odor compounds remaining on towels after washing are linked to the amount of biofilm, with an especially strong correlation for fatty acids.

### Fixing it means reducing the bacteria

Once a biofilm has formed, a regular wash isn't enough.

Methods generally considered effective include:

**Use an oxygen bleach.** Dissolve sodium percarbonate in hot water and soak. Bleach breaks down odor compounds and also acts on bacteria.

**Raise the temperature.** Above a certain temperature, bacteria can't multiply. But you need to stay within your clothes' heat tolerance.

**Clean the washing machine drum.** Biofilms form on the back side of the drum too. If bacteria are being supplied from there, treating only the clothes won't help.

**Avoid leftover bathwater and long soaks.** (In Japan, many people reuse leftover bathwater for laundry.) Any time spent sitting wet becomes time for bacteria to be active.

### Prevention is the surer bet

It's easier to keep a biofilm from forming than to remove one that's already there.

1. **Get the skin oils fully out** (don't overload the washer, use the right amount of detergent, use warm water if needed)
2. **Shorten the time laundry stays wet** (don't leave it sitting in the washer, hang it right away)
3. **Dry it fast** (air flow, dehumidifying, spacing)

These three steps each deny bacteria one of the things they need: "nutrients," "time," or "water." The idea isn't to add something, but to cut back on what the bacteria need.

By the way, some point out that drying laundry in the sun in hopes of UV sterilization has limited effect. The fast drying itself is thought to make the bigger difference.
