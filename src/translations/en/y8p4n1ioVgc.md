### Body heat escapes in three ways

As the video explains, a fan doesn't cool the air in the room. The reason it still feels cool is that it helps heat escape from your body.

According to a report by Japan's Ministry of the Environment, body heat mainly escapes through these three routes:

- **Convection**: Heat moving from your skin to the surrounding air. When the air is cooler than your skin, the bigger the temperature difference and the stronger the wind, the more heat escapes.
- **Thermal radiation**: Heat leaving your skin as invisible light (infrared) toward surrounding walls and other surfaces.
- **Evaporation**: Heat being carried away as sweat and other moisture on your skin turns into water vapor. The same report notes that if you sweat 150 g in an hour and that sweat evaporates efficiently, you can release roughly 100 watts' worth of heat.

Of these, a fan's breeze helps with "convection" and "evaporation."

### Wind strips away the "warm, humid air layer"

Without any breeze, the air right around your skin tends to stay put, warmed by your body heat and dampened by sweat and water vapor from your skin. That shrinks both the temperature difference and the humidity difference between your skin and the surrounding air, making it harder for heat to escape.

A fan's breeze keeps blowing this air away and replacing it with room air. The Ministry of the Environment report also explains that the amount of heat lost through convection depends on "the temperature difference between skin and air" and "the wind conditions," and that sweat evaporates better "the lower the humidity and the stronger the wind."

The video summed it up as "a fan helps sweat evaporate and lowers how hot you feel," but to be precise, it's best to think of it as **helping heat escape through convection, not just sweat evaporation**. When you're barely sweating and a breeze still feels cool, that's mainly thanks to convection.

Also, the video's statement that "skin is constantly evaporating moisture" is correct. Even when you aren't sweating, moisture evaporates from your skin and breath without you noticing (this is called insensible water loss). The report also states that the heat lost through evaporation includes both sweat and this insensible water loss.

### Be careful when the air is close to body temperature

Heat escapes through convection only when the air is cooler than your skin. The Ministry of the Environment report also notes that when the surrounding temperature approaches skin temperature or rises above it, sweat evaporation becomes the main way to release heat. If the air is hotter than your skin, the more wind you get, the more heat you actually absorb from that hot air.

For this reason, guidelines have been set for the temperatures at which it's okay to rely on a fan alone.

- The U.S. Centers for Disease Control and Prevention (CDC) advises using a fan only when the room temperature is below 90°F (about 32°C), cautioning that above that, a fan can raise your body temperature.
- According to a 2021 study, the World Health Organization (WHO) recommends limiting fan use to temperatures below 35°C.

However, these threshold temperatures are debated among researchers. A research team including the University of Sydney used a model that calculates how heat flows into and out of the body and reported that the range in which fans are helpful is wider than current guidelines suggest (2015). In addition, a 2021 study using weather data from 108 cities around the world proposed temperature thresholds below which fans don't backfire: under 39°C for healthy young adults and under 38°C for healthy people aged 65 and over. Keep in mind that the results also vary with humidity.

On the other hand, some findings suggest older people should be cautious. A U.S. research team ran an experiment in which healthy people aged 61 to 71 spent time in a 42°C room while the humidity was raised from 30% to 70%. On the days they had a fan blowing on them (at about 4 m per second), both their heart rate and core body temperature tended to rise more. In young people, a breeze increases the amount of sweat, but in the older participants in this experiment it did not. The researchers think that because the ability to sweat declines with age, fans may become less effective. That said, 42°C is a very extreme condition.

### How to use a fan to prevent heatstroke

Japan's Ministry of the Environment and Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare recommend that on hot days indoors, you control the temperature with an air conditioner and **use a fan or air circulator along with it**. It's a division of labor: the air conditioner cools the air in the room, and the fan delivers that air to your body.

- Don't hold back because you're worried about the electricity bill; use the air conditioner appropriately, including at night and while you sleep (Ministry of the Environment).
- Check the room temperature often, and also refer to the heat index known as WBGT (Wet Bulb Globe Temperature) (Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare).

If heatstroke is suspected, such as with dizziness or lightheadedness when standing, people are advised to move to a cool place, loosen their clothing, cool the neck, armpits, and groin, and take in fluids and salt. If the person can't drink water on their own or doesn't respond when spoken to, don't hesitate to call an ambulance (119 in Japan). If you're concerned about your health, please consult a medical professional.
