### Air that hits a mountain has nowhere to go but up

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas explains that when a flow of air runs into a mountain or hill, it gets lifted upward. To borrow the UK Met Office's phrasing, the air "can't go into the hill," so it's forced up along the slope.

That's exactly what the video means when it says, "When wind hits a mountain, the air gets pushed up along the slope."

### Air cools as it rises because it expands

The higher you go, the lower the surrounding air pressure. Air that gets lifted expands, and because it uses its own heat to do so, its temperature drops. This is called cooling by **adiabatic expansion** (expanding without exchanging heat with its surroundings).

There's a rule of thumb for how fast it cools. According to Hareru Land, the Japan Meteorological Agency's website for kids, air without clouds cools by about 1°C for every 100 meters it rises. When it cools enough to reach its **dew point** (the temperature at which water vapor starts turning into water droplets), the vapor becomes tiny droplets and shows up as cloud.

Once a cloud has formed, the cooling slows to about 0.5°C per 100 meters (this varies with temperature and pressure). That's because water vapor releases heat when it turns into water, warming the surrounding air.

### Whether clouds form depends on how moist the air is

The WMO International Cloud Atlas says clouds form on the windward slope "if the airflow is sufficiently moist." If the air is dry, it won't cool all the way to its dew point even when lifted, and no cloud forms. What kind of cloud you get depends on how humid the air is and how easily it moves up and down (atmospheric stability).

Sometimes clouds thicken near the summit and blanket the ridge, or rain or snow starts falling or gets heavier. The Met Office also notes that this is why hills tend to get more rain than other places.

A clear example in Japan is the winter snow on the Sea of Japan side of the country. According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, cold northwesterly winds from the Asian continent pick up moisture and form snow clouds as they cross the Sea of Japan, and snowfall is heaviest in the high mountains and mountain areas where those snow clouds run into.

### Why the leeward side tends to be clear

After crossing the mountain, the air now flows down the slope. The Met Office explains that descending air warms up and the cloud droplets evaporate. It's the reverse of the climb: the air moves down into higher pressure and gets compressed, so its temperature rises.

The Japan Meteorological Agency also explains that in winter, on the Pacific side of Japan, the air that has already dropped its snow blows down over the mountains, so there's hardly any snow, and the weather is clear and dry. Air that has shed its moisture as rain or snow on the windward side has less water left to form clouds in the first place. That's the mechanism behind the video's line, "the other side tends to be clear."

The air coming down the mountain can even end up as a warmer, drier wind than it was before it started climbing. This is called the foehn effect (→ [article on wind that crosses mountains](/trivia/DqbwBmVIRls/)).

### Clouds can form on the leeward side too

That said, the leeward side isn't always clear.

According to the Met Office, air that has crossed a mountain can ripple up and down on the leeward side (**mountain waves**). Where the air rises within the wave, it cools and clouds form; where it sinks, it warms and the droplets evaporate. This is how smooth, lens-shaped "lenticular clouds" form. The WMO also notes that on windy days, clouds that form near a summit can stream off to the leeward side like a flag (banner clouds).

It's best to think of "clouds on the windward side, clear on the leeward side" as one of the common patterns you see around mountains.
