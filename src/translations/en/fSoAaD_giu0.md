### "No water" doesn't mean "a small amount of water"

First, let's clear up a common misunderstanding. Microbes can't multiply not because the **amount** of water is small.

A glossary entry from the Japan Society of Refrigerating and Air Conditioning Engineers explains water by dividing it into two types.

> Water consists of free water, whose molecules can move around freely and which microorganisms can use, and bound water, which is linked by hydrogen bonds to other components in the food (such as proteins and carbohydrates), has its molecular motion restricted, and is not used by microorganisms.

**Free water** is water that can move freely and that microbes can use. **Bound water** is water that's attached to other components and can't move.

Even if a food contains water, microbes can't use it if it's all bound water. The measure of this proportion is water activity.

> In 1957, Scott proposed water activity as an indicator of the proportion of free water in food and showed the relationship between water activity and the growth limits of microorganisms.

The definition is clear-cut, too.

> Water Activity (Aw) = P/Po
> Po: the equilibrium vapor pressure when pure water is placed in a sealed container and allowed to evaporate under constant temperature conditions
> P: the equilibrium vapor pressure of the test food under the same conditions

And here's what it means in practice:

> Because water activity indicates the growth limit (lower limit) of food microorganisms, in Japan it is used in the manufacturing standards under the Food Sanitation Act for pressure-and-heat-sterilized foods in containers and packaging, soft drinks, fish paste products, and meat products.

It's an indicator used in legal standards. This isn't just a matter of gut feeling.

### The three foods simply use different methods

Honey, rice, and salt. These three reach the same goal by different routes.

**Honey: tying up water with sugar**

A consumer Q&A from Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries describes what's in honey like this:

> Of glucose and fructose, the main components of honey,

Because there's so much sugar, water molecules are surrounded by sugar and can't move. The extremely high sugar concentration is also why crystals form.

> Therefore, the more glucose a honey contains, the more easily it crystallizes.

It's so concentrated that sugar that can't stay dissolved turns into crystals. In other words, there's almost no free water.

**Rice: drying to reduce water**

Here's the Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries' explanation of the JAS (Japanese Agricultural Standard) for milled rice:

> Moisture content affects storability and the growth of mold and the like when milled rice is stored at room temperature.

The standard is 15.0% or less. The remaining water is bound water attached to starch and proteins, which microbes can't use.

**Salt: no water to begin with**

Salt doesn't contain water. And salt itself strongly attracts water. Salting works as a preservation method because salt pulls free water out of food.

### Canned food alone uses a completely different method

This is the interesting part that ties the video together. Canned food works on a different principle from these three.

Here's the explanation from the Japan Canners Association:

> Canned foods, bottled foods, and retort pouch foods are sealed so that air, water, bacteria, and the like absolutely cannot get in, and their contents are fully heat-sterilized, so there is no risk of them causing food poisoning. Of course, no food additives such as sterilizing agents or preservatives are used.

The contents of a can are full of moisture. Whether it's simmered fish or fruit, there's plenty of free water. It's exactly the kind of environment where microbes can multiply.

So why doesn't it spoil? **Because there are no microbes.**

> For sterilizing canned foods (seafood, agricultural products, meat, prepared dishes, etc.), some bottled foods, and retort pouch foods, high-temperature, high-pressure sterilization at 120°C or higher for 4 minutes or longer is generally performed using a "retort" (high-pressure kettle).

In other words, there are two approaches to preservation.

| Method | Idea | Examples |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Take away the environment | Microbes may be present but can't multiply | Honey, rice, salt, dried fish, jam |
| Eliminate the microbes | The environment allows growth, but there's nothing to grow | Canned food, retort pouches, bottled food |

### And these two approaches are connected in the law

What's even more interesting is that these two approaches are combined within the regulations. Take a look at the Food Sanitation Act standard cited in the Japan Society of Refrigerating and Air Conditioning Engineers' glossary entry.

> "For pressure-and-heat-sterilized foods in containers and packaging with a pH exceeding 4.6 and a water activity exceeding 0.94, the method shall be heating so that the temperature at the center reaches 120°C for 4 minutes, or a method with equal or greater effectiveness."

The sterilization conditions for canned and retort foods are **split by water activity value**. The reason is spelled out as well.

> This indicates that when the water activity is at or below 0.94 (or pH 4.6), the lower limit for the growth of Clostridium botulinum, botulinum need not be considered a target of sterilization.

If the water activity is 0.94 or below, botulinum can't multiply, so such strict heating isn't necessary. If the water activity exceeds 0.94, 120°C for 4 minutes is required.

The "take away the environment" method and the "eliminate the microbes" method aren't separate techniques. Their relationship is that **how much of the environment you've taken away determines how much you need to eliminate**.

### Nearly every food preservation method is an application of this

Looking at things from this angle, everyday preservation methods sort themselves out neatly.

- **Dried fish, dried noodles, kiriboshi daikon (dried shredded daikon radish)** → Reduce free water by drying (same as rice)
- **Jam, yokan (a sweet bean jelly), condensed milk** → Tie up water with sugar (same as honey)
- **Shiokara (salted fermented seafood), salted wakame seaweed, miso** → Draw out water with salt (same as salt)
- **Canned food, retort pouches, bottled food** → Seal and heat-sterilize
- **Freezing** → Freeze the water so it can't move
- **Pickling in vinegar** → Lower the pH so microbes can't survive

Pickling in vinegar is the only one based on acidity rather than water activity, but that too showed up in the Food Sanitation Act standard above as "pH 4.6." It's quite satisfying how traditional preserved foods connect directly to the indicators of modern food safety.
