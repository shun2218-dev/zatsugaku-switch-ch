### What the rules require

Under Law 2 of the Laws of the Game (IFAB), a soccer ball must:

- Be spherical.
- Be made of suitable material.
- Have a circumference (the distance around it) of 68–70 cm.
- Weigh 410–450 g at the start of the match.
- Have an air pressure of 0.6–1.1 atmospheres (600–1,100 g per square cm) at sea level.

On top of that, balls used in official matches of FIFA, the confederations, and national associations must **have passed the tests of the FIFA Quality Programme and carry its mark**. In other words, the fact that a ball "rolls and bounces properly" isn't just down to the manufacturer's design—it's verified by testing.

### What FIFA's tests check

According to FIFA, a ball must pass seven tests: weight, circumference, sphericity, rebound, water absorption, pressure loss, and shape and size retention. Here are the standards for the strictest level, "FIFA QUALITY PRO" (size 5 ball), taken from the 2018 testing manual FIFA has made public. Tests are carried out in a room at 20°C (68°F) and 65% humidity, with the ball inflated to about 0.8 atmospheres (0.8 bar).

- Roundness (sphericity): A machine measures the radius at 4,500 points on the ball's surface, and the maximum deviation from the average radius must be within 1.5%. For a size 5 ball, that works out to a radius deviation of roughly 1.6 mm or less.
- Circumference: 68.5–69.5 cm (a narrower range than the rules' 68–70 cm).
- Weight: 420–445 g.
- Rebound: Dropped from a height of 2 m onto a steel plate, the ball must bounce 135–155 cm. Even when chilled to 5°C (41°F), it must bounce at least 125 cm.
- Water absorption: After being squeezed 250 times underwater, the ball's weight must increase by no more than 10%.
- Pressure loss: After 72 hours, the air pressure must drop by no more than 20%.

Note that these are the 2018 figures, and the standards can change when the manual is revised.

### Does it keep its shape after being fired 2,000 times?

The test closest to the video's claim that the ball "keeps its shape through thousands of kicks" is the shape and size retention test. A machine fires the ball at a steel plate at 50 km/h (about 31 mph) **2,000 times**. Afterward, the change in circumference must be within 1.5 cm, the roundness within 1.5%, and the change in air pressure within about 0.1 atmospheres (0.1 bar), with no damage to the seams or valve.

The reason a ball springs right back into shape after getting dented is that the air compressed inside pushes back from within. Material from the manufacturer Molten explains that a ball consists of an outer cover, a cushioning layer beneath it, and a bladder that holds the air. The video described the inner layer as something that "keeps the air pressure even," but it's more accurate to think of the bladder's job as holding the air in so it doesn't escape. Even so, air does gradually leak out, which is why the tests also measure "how well it resists losing air."

### A few notes on the valve and panels

The video said the valve (the part you inflate the ball through) "lets air pass in only one direction." Normally it stays sealed so air can't escape on its own, but you can let air out by inserting a needle. In FIFA's tests, too, air pressure is measured with a gauge attached to a needle, and air is let out to adjust it.

Also, "pentagon and hexagon panels stitched together" describes the classic black-and-white ball. Today's match balls come in all sorts of panel counts and shapes—"Trionda," the official ball of the 2026 World Cup, is made of just **four panels**. Some balls are bonded together with heat instead of stitched (FIFA's tests even record whether a ball is stitched or bonded), and Molten says its thermal bonding improves the ball's "sphericity." The claim that "the panel structure spreads the force of impacts evenly" could not be confirmed in official sources.

### The "does it roll straight?" test is actually for futsal balls only

Let's also address the "roll true" part of the title. FIFA's 2018 manual includes a "balance" test, in which a ball is rolled down a ramp and its deviation from a straight line is measured. However, this test applies **only to futsal balls** (futsal is a small-sided indoor version of soccer) and isn't done on regular soccer balls. To be precise, how well a soccer ball rolls is mainly checked indirectly through tests of roundness, weight, rebound, and so on.
