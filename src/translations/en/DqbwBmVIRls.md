### The Japan Meteorological Agency's definition describes two mechanisms

The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) defines the foehn effect, as a term used in weather forecasts, like this:

- A phenomenon in which moist air drops rain as it crosses a mountain, then flows down the mountain and becomes dry and warmer
- Or, a phenomenon in which a mass of air with high "potential temperature" from aloft descends on the leeward side of a mountain and becomes dry and warmer

**Potential temperature** is a rough measure of how warm a parcel of air would be if you brought it down to ground level. Air high up may be cold, but if it comes down to the ground, it gets compressed and warms up. Air with a high potential temperature is air that would be hot once it came down.

The video mainly covers the first mechanism, and at the end it touches on the second by saying that the effect "can also happen when air from high up flows down without producing any rain." JMA's kids' website, "Harerun Land," calls the first one a "wet foehn" and the second one a "dry foehn."

### Following a wet foehn by the numbers

Let's use the example from Harerun Land: wind crossing a 2,000-meter mountain, with a temperature of 25°C at the foot.

- **Going up (before clouds form)**: Air without clouds cools by about 1°C for every 100 meters it rises. Say it reaches 15°C at 1,000 meters, and clouds form there.
- **Going up (after clouds form)**: Once clouds have formed, the air cools by only about 0.5°C per 100 meters (this varies with temperature and pressure). At the summit, it's 10°C.
- **Coming down**: Air that has lost its water as rain warms by about 1°C for every 100 meters it descends. By the time it's back down to its original height, it's 30°C.

Air that was 25°C before climbing the mountain is 30°C after crossing it.

### The real reason it gets hotter is on the way "up"

The video says, "When that now-dry air flows down the opposite slope, it gets compressed and its temperature climbs quickly." It's true that the air warms on the way down, but that alone wouldn't make it hotter than where it started. According to Harerun Land, air that crosses a mountain without forming clouds warms on the way down by exactly as much as it cooled on the way up, and ends up back at 25°C.

The key to ending up hotter is that clouds form partway up. When water vapor turns into water, it warms the surrounding air, so the air cools more gently as it climbs. On top of that, water that falls as rain or snow can't cross the mountain, so there's less evaporation cooling the air on the way down. In other words, **the air doesn't cool much going up but warms fully coming down**, so on balance it ends up warmer.

### Why is the wind dry?

In a wet foehn, the air that dropped rain or snow on the windward side comes down having lost that much water vapor. In a dry foehn, air that was already dry high up comes down. Either way, the wind that reaches the foot of the mountain carries little moisture. That's why JMA's definition says both mechanisms make the air "dry and warmer."

### Sometimes the rainless "dry foehn" is the main player

Textbooks usually show the wet foehn diagram, but in Japan that doesn't always seem to be the whole story.

A research group at the University of Tsukuba looked at 198 foehn events on the Toyama Plain (on Japan's Sea of Japan coast) from 2006 to 2015. They found that the main mechanism was not a wet foehn warmed by rain on the windward side, but **a dynamic mechanism in which descending air is compressed and warms up**, in other words, a dry foehn. The researchers themselves say they had previously assumed the wet foehn was the main one.

The same study found that just over two-thirds of the foehn events occurred when a low-pressure system was passing over the Sea of Japan, and about one-fifth occurred under high pressure. Foehn events with a typhoon near Japan made up only about 1 in 20, but every case of dangerous heat above 35°C happened during these typhoon events. Where the video says it "can also happen," in some places it may be better to think of it as "actually more common."
