### Winter air is much drier than summer air

Harerun Land, the Japan Meteorological Agency's website for kids, says that whether the air looks clear depends on how much water vapor, dust, and similar matter it contains. It explains that in winter, temperatures are lower than in summer and convective activity (the movement in which air warmed by the ground rises and the air gets stirred up) is weaker, so there's less water vapor and dust, and the sky looks clearer than in summer. The video's line that "winter brings cold, dry air with little water vapor" matches this exactly.

How big is the difference? Let's look at the Japan Meteorological Agency's climate normals for Tokyo (averages for 1991–2020). "Vapor pressure," a rough measure of how much water vapor is in the air, is 4.5 hPa in January and 26.2 hPa in August. **The air in Tokyo in winter holds only about one-sixth as much water vapor as in midsummer.**

### Clear winter skies are mainly a Pacific-side thing

The dry winter air is carried in by the northwesterly seasonal winds blowing from the Asian continent. According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, this cold wind forms snow clouds as it crosses the Sea of Japan and drops snow on the Sea of Japan side of the country. After that, the air crosses the mountains and blows down the other side, so the Pacific side gets almost no snow and stays sunny and dry.

Tokyo's climate normals show this too: January gets 192.6 hours of sunshine, more than August (174.2 hours), even though August days are longer. In other words, the "high, clear winter sky" is mostly a sight on the Pacific side. On the Sea of Japan side, the same winter brings many cloudy and snowy days.

### What makes the sky whitish isn't water vapor, but "particles"

The video said that "summer air holds lots of water vapor, and light bumps into those particles and scatters," but a small clarification is in order. Water vapor itself is an invisible gas, not particles. What actually makes the sky hazy and whitish are the **tiny water droplets and fine dust particles that have absorbed moisture** floating in the air. In the Japan Meteorological Agency's terminology, reduced visibility caused by small water droplets or moist particles is called "mist," and reduced visibility caused by dry, fine particles is called "haze."

The blue color of the sky comes from air molecules strongly scattering blue light (→ [Why is the sky blue?](/trivia/tYn7fs759Hs/)). Dust and droplets, on the other hand, are about the same size as the wavelength of light or larger, so they scatter light without being very selective about color. A research presentation by Gedzelman of the City College of New York also states that the sky is "deepest blue when the air is clean and dry," and explains that a hazy sky is brighter but less blue.

In winter there are fewer of these particles, so less whitish light gets mixed in, and the blue of the sky looks deeper. It's easiest to understand the video's line that "light can travel straight through more easily" as referring to distant mountains and buildings looking sharp (that is, better visibility). The blue of the sky itself isn't light that reached us in a straight line, but the color of light scattered by air molecules.

Also, according to the UK's Met Office, the sky looks whiter closer to the horizon because light passes through a longer stretch of air and gets scattered many times, mixing in white light. On a clear winter day, try comparing the deep blue directly overhead with the paler color near the horizon.

### "Looking high" is a matter of perception

At the start of the video, we said "it isn't an optical illusion; it happens because what's in the air changes." As we've seen, the sky looking "clear" can be explained by what's in the air. But as for looking "high," the sky itself doesn't actually get any higher; it's more accurate to think of it as the **impression** we get from seeing a deep blue sky without haze. We couldn't find any research that has looked closely at why people feel a clear sky is "high."

Incidentally, in Japan it may be autumn, more than winter, that people most often describe as having a "high sky." The Japanese dictionary Digital Daijisen defines the saying "Ten takaku uma koyuru aki" ("In autumn the sky is high and horses grow fat") as an expression for the pleasant autumn season, when the sky is clear and sunny and horses gain appetite and fatten up. The reason the sky clears up in autumn is much the same as in winter (→ [6 "whys" you'll notice looking up at the autumn sky](/trivia/VnULqptS-aM/)).
