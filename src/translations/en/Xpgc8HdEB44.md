### Everything left at room temperature is the same temperature

Let's confirm the premise first. A metal table and a wooden table that have sat in the same room for a long time are at **the same temperature**, because they've come into balance with the room.

But when you touch them, the metal feels cold and the wood doesn't. What you're feeling isn't temperature.

Human skin has no sensor that measures temperature. What it has are sensors that detect how fast heat is leaving. So things that pull heat out of your hand quickly feel "cold."

### Thermal conductivity differs between materials by three orders of magnitude

The number that describes how fast heat travels is thermal conductivity. Material from Osaka Kyoiku University defines it:

> Thermal conductivity к [W/mK] is the amount of heat that flows per second through 1 m² of a plate 1 m thick when there is a 1°C temperature difference between its two faces.

Here are some key values from a table in the same material (units: W/m·K).

| Material | Temperature | Thermal conductivity |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Silver | 0°C | 428 |
| Copper | 0°C | 403 |
| Aluminum | 0°C | 236 |
| Iron | 0°C | 83.5 |
| Ice | 0°C | 2.2 |
| Water | 10°C | 0.582 |
| Glass | Room temperature | 0.55–0.75 |
| Dry wood | 18–25°C | 0.15–0.25 |
| Dry air | 0°C | 0.0241 |

Copper at 403 versus wood at 0.15–0.25: a difference of **more than 2,000 times**.

If you touch copper and wood at the same room temperature at once, the copper pulls heat out of your hand 2,000 times faster. That's why copper feels cold and wood doesn't.

### Metals are special because of free electrons

Why are metals alone so fast? The Osaka Kyoiku University material explains:

> Thermal conductivity increases in the order of gases, liquids, and solids. Metals have especially high thermal conductivity because, in addition to the collisions between molecules discussed under heat conduction, there are collisions between free electrons in the metal.

In ordinary materials, heat moves along as the vibration of molecules passes to their neighbors. It's like a bucket brigade.

On top of that, metals have electrons that can move freely through the crystal (free electrons). These electrons carry heat, which is overwhelmingly faster than the molecular bucket brigade.

The same free electrons are why metals conduct electricity well. The tendency that "things that conduct electricity well also conduct heat well" exists because the carriers are the same.

### Air is the slowest of all

Look at the bottom of the table. Dry air is 0.0241, about a tenth of even wood.

This is the principle behind insulation. [Wool sweaters are warm](/trivia/MKfS0AIVDhM/) and [thermos bottles keep things hot](/trivia/7tvkVb1PPwg/) because they take advantage of air's extremely low thermal conductivity.

Styrofoam, down feathers, fiberglass insulation, double-pane windows: they all use the same strategy of trapping a layer of still air. It's not the material itself that insulates, but the air the material holds.

### That's why "coldness" depends on the material

Compare this with your own experience, and the order lines up neatly.

Feels coldest: **metal** (doorknobs, spoons, horizontal bars)
Next coldest: **stone, tile, glass** (around 0.55–1)
Hardly feels cold: **wood** (0.15–0.25)
Barely cold at all: **fabric, foam materials** (which hold a lot of air)

Walk barefoot on a winter morning, and a tile floor feels cold while a wooden floor doesn't feel so cold. The floors are at the same room temperature; the difference is thermal conductivity.

### The same thing happens on the hot side

It works in reverse, too.

In a sauna, you can sit on the wooden bench, but you can't touch the metal parts. Both are the same temperature (80°C or 90°C). But metal pours heat into your hand all at once, so it burns you.

For the same reason, you can't hold an aluminum baking sheet straight out of the oven bare-handed, but you can hold a wooden cutting board at the same temperature.

**Even at the same temperature, only the metal is dangerous.** Whether hot or cold, what's at work is thermal conductivity.

### Wood's value comes as a range

The wood value in the table is given as a range, "0.15–0.25," because it varies with moisture content and density.

Wood is a collection of cells, holding air gaps inside. When it absorbs water, those air gaps are replaced by water. Water's thermal conductivity is 0.582, more than 20 times that of air, so damp wood conducts heat more easily than dry wood.

That difference is why a wet wooden cutting board feels colder than a dry one. [Why a wet towel feels cold](/trivia/Y2tQt9IL2ZY/) is part of the same logic.
