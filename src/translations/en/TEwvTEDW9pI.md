### The sparkles are tiny reflections of the sun

Look at water on a windy day, and you'll see points of light blinking on and off.

According to an explanation by Dr. Joseph Shaw, a researcher at the U.S. National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration (NOAA), each of these points of light is the sun undergoing **specular reflection** (light bouncing off in a fixed direction, like from a mirror) on the water. In English, this is called **sunglint**. A spot lights up when the slope of the water there is at just the right angle to send sunlight back to the viewer's eyes.

Many points of light appear and vanish, and from a distance they blend together into what looks like a smooth path of light.

### On flat water, only one spot shines

Dr. Shaw writes that if the water were perfectly flat, there would be only one bright spot.

Calm water works just like a mirror. A document from the Meteorological Satellite Center of the Japan Meteorological Agency also states that "calm water plays the same role as a mirror." The sun reflected in a mirror appears in only one place. According to NASA, if the sun were directly overhead and the water perfectly still, you would see a round reflection that looks exactly like the sun in the sky.

We explain why water becomes mirror-like when there's no wind in more detail in [our article on pond surfaces](/trivia/OGQaWRGDVsw/).

### Wind creates countless "little mirrors"

According to Dr. Shaw, even the slightest breeze raises ripples on the water. Then light from the sun, the moon, and even streetlights starts bouncing back from many places on the surface.

Each ripple's surface tilts in a slightly different direction. It's like having lots of little mirrors lined up, all pointing every which way. Only the surfaces that happen to be tilted so they send sunlight toward your eyes appear to shine.

Because the waves keep moving, when one surface tilts away from your line of sight, its light disappears, and when another surface reaches just the right tilt, that one lights up. Dr. Shaw also explains that as the rippled surface moves, each individual glint moves too. That's what the flickering, shifting sparkle really is.

### The shape of the glitter depends on the sun's height

The shape of the area where the sparkles gather changes with how high the sun is.

According to Dr. Shaw, it's round when the sun is directly overhead and elongated when it's near sunrise or sunset. NASA also describes seeing a long, elliptical band of light in the evening when the sun was low.

In the video, we said that "the water in the direction of the sun sparkles." To be precise, when the sun is low, it forms a path of light running from you toward the sun, and when the sun is high, it spreads out in a round patch roughly beneath the sun. Moonlight can create a similar path of light, too.

### Stronger wind makes the glitter wider and dimmer

In the video, we said that "how it looks changes with the strength of the wind." Here's more on how it changes.

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency's Meteorological Satellite Center, sunglint is small on calm water with no waves, and large and dim on rough water whipped up by strong wind. The more steeply the waves tilt, the farther away a surface can be and still reflect sunlight to your eyes, so the shining area spreads out. On the other hand, because the light is scattered over a wider area, it gets dimmer overall.

NASA also shares a photo taken in San Francisco in which the path of light over the choppy water near the shore was much wider than over the calmer water farther out.

In 1954, two American researchers, Cox and Munk, photographed the ocean's glitter from an airplane and studied how the waves were tilted. According to their paper, the magnitude of the wave slopes (the mean square) increased **in proportion** to wind speed. At a wind speed of 14 meters per second, it reached a magnitude corresponding to a 16-degree tilt. In other words, the spread of the glitter tells you how rough the sea surface is.

According to Dr. Shaw, the roughness of the water also changes not only with wind but with the temperature difference between the water and the air.

### An "ocean mirror" visible from space

Sunglint can also be seen from satellites.

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, where sunglint appears in weather satellite images changes with the season and time of day. And from the size and brightness of the sunglint, you can estimate whether the sea surface in that area is calm.

According to NASA, sunglint gets in the way when studying ocean color or phytoplankton, so it's removed from the data. But it can also be useful. Oil dampens ripples on the water and smooths it out, so areas where oil has spread shine differently from their surroundings. That makes sunglint a clue for spotting oil spills at sea.

The sparkle on the water is countless tiny mirrors on the surface flashing for just the instant they connect the sun and your eyes. By looking at how the light spreads, you can even read the day's wind and the state of the water.
