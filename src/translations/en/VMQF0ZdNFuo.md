### 20 km/h, up to 30 times a second

Woodpeckers peck at trees for several reasons. According to a researcher's explainer, they do it to strip off tough bark while looking for food, to dig nest holes in trunks, and to make noise that calls to others or announces their territory.

That explainer says woodpeckers typically strike the tree with their beaks at about 20 kilometers per hour (around 12 mph). When they drum to make sound, they can reach up to 30 strikes per second.

The video said "around 20 times a second," but the figure in the researcher's explainer is "up to 30 times a second." Either way, it's too fast for the eye to follow.

### The "cushion in the head" theory

With pounding that hard, why doesn't the brain get hurt?

For a long time, the widely accepted theory was that the skull absorbs the shock. According to the explainer, 3D scans of woodpecker heads revealed bone between the beak and the brain that is full of tiny spaces, like a sponge. This bone was thought to soften impacts, and it even inspired designs for things like helmets.

However, this theory had never been confirmed by experiment. As early as 1976, one researcher had already raised doubts, pointing out that if the beak absorbed the impact, the bird would have to hit even harder.

### Putting it to the test with high-speed cameras

In 2022, a research team including the University of Antwerp in Belgium tested this theory.

They studied three species of woodpecker. They filmed the birds pecking with high-speed cameras and tracked two points on the beak along with the position of the eye. The eye serves as a marker that is thought to move together with the part of the head that holds the brain. This let them compare, across more than 100 strikes, how abruptly each point stopped at the moment of impact.

If there were a cushion in the head, the brain area should have stopped more gradually than the beak. But the results showed no difference in any of the birds. The impact was not being softened between the beak and the brain.

The team concluded that a woodpecker's head is not a shock-absorbing device but works like a "stiff hammer."

### A cushion would actually be a disadvantage

So why don't they have a cushion?

The team checked this with calculations, too. If the head absorbed the shock, then even when striking at the same speed, the beak would sink less deeply into the wood.

Absorbing the shock would slightly reduce the force on the brain. But it would be a waste of energy, because the bird could cut the force on its brain by the same amount simply by pecking more gently. For a bird that makes its living by digging into wood, a head that doesn't absorb shock is a sensible design.

### Why is a small brain safer?

If there's no cushion, how is the brain protected?

In the team's data, woodpecker brains came to a sudden stop at up to about 400 g. Here, g is a unit where 1 equals the acceleration caused by Earth's gravity. The threshold for a human **concussion** (a temporary disruption of brain function caused by a strong force to the head) is estimated at around 135 g, so this is far above it. That's what the video meant by "for a human, an impact that strong could injure the brain."

The answer lies in the difference in brain size. According to the explainer, the pressure inside the brain when it stops suddenly is proportional to how sharply it stops and to the length of the brain. A smaller brain means lower pressure, even when stopping just as sharply.

A woodpecker's brain is about one-seventh the length of a human's. So the limit that corresponds to 135 g in humans works out to seven times higher in woodpeckers, about 1,000 g. That makes 400 g less dangerous than it looks. There is still plenty of margin.

An American researcher had already pointed out this idea in 2006. According to the explainer, in addition to the small brain, that researcher also cited the very short duration of each impact and the orientation of the brain within the skull as reasons.

The video said "the impact stays below the threshold for a concussion." More precisely, how sharply the brain stops does exceed the human threshold. But because the brain is small, the pressure inside it doesn't get high enough to cause a concussion.

### Why there are no giant woodpeckers

This relationship also answers another puzzle.

The explainer writes that the relationship between brain length and pressure can explain why there are no giant woodpeckers capable of digging much deeper holes than today's woodpeckers. A bigger body and brain would allow harder strikes, but the pressure on the brain would also grow.

The team also reported that woodpeckers face a different challenge: much like a driven nail, the beak often gets stuck in the wood. When they filmed and studied black woodpeckers, they found the birds moved their upper and lower beaks in an alternating, staggered motion to pull free in about 0.05 seconds.

At the end of the explainer, the researcher reflects that the "cushion in the head" theory is an example of an idea that became widely believed without scientific evidence. A story told on zoo signs and in books turned out not to hold up once it was actually measured.
