### There were many theories about what stripes do

A zebra's black and white stripes have puzzled people for a long time. A 2018 paper notes that there are **as many as 18 theories** about what the stripes are for.

Broadly speaking, they fall into these five groups:

- Helping zebras blend into woods and tall grass so they're harder to spot (**camouflage**)
- Making the stripes merge when the herd moves, throwing off the aim of predators like lions
- Releasing body heat to keep cool
- Serving as a signal for zebras to recognize each other
- Warding off bloodsucking insects

In 2014, Tim Caro and his colleagues at the University of California, Davis, ran a study comparing all five at the same time.

### Overlaying the maps: where horseflies are common, stripes match up

Caro's team looked at zebras, horses, and donkeys and their relatives, comparing how strongly striped each species was with the environment where it lives: how much forest there was, predators like lions, temperature, herd size, and how many bloodsucking flies there were.

The results were clear. The amount of striping was strongly linked to regions with environments where **horseflies** (large flies that suck the blood of horses and other animals) are active. The number of stripes on the belly was linked to regions where tsetse flies live.

On the other hand, none of the other four (camouflage, avoiding predators, regulating body temperature, and social signals) showed a consistent link. Based on this, the paper wrote that the answer to the stripe mystery is "within reach."

### Zebras are vulnerable to horseflies

Why would zebras need to avoid horseflies?

Caro's team found that zebra hair is shorter than that of other grazing animals living in the same regions, and that horseflies' mouthparts are much longer than the depth of a zebra's coat. That makes it easy for them to bite right through the hair.

According to a 2019 paper, African horseflies carry diseases that can kill zebras, such as **trypanosomiasis** (a disease caused by parasites) and African horse sickness. Not getting bitten is directly tied to survival.

### Horseflies get close, but can't land properly

So how do stripes keep horseflies off? In 2019, Caro's team observed horseflies flying around 3 zebras and 9 horses at a farm in Britain.

The number of times horseflies circled around, or briefly touched the body, didn't differ between zebras and horses. But the number of times they made a full landing was lower on the zebras.

Looking closely at video footage, the horseflies approached the zebras still flying fast and failed to slow down properly before reaching them. On horses they stayed for about 10 seconds on average, but in the few cases where they managed to land on a zebra, they stayed only about 1.2 seconds.

The team also ran an experiment in which horses wore three kinds of coats: black, white, and striped. With the striped coat, the number of times horseflies touched or landed dropped clearly. Yet the uncovered heads got visited by horseflies equally in every case. Even on the same horse, only the striped parts became harder to land on.

The paper concludes that stripes don't keep horseflies away from a distance; instead, **they keep horseflies that come close from landing properly**.

The video described stripes as "confusing bloodsucking flies and making them less likely to come near," but in this experiment, the number of times horseflies came close didn't actually change. More accurately, it's "even when they come close, they're more likely to fail at landing."

### The "stripes keep zebras cool" theory wasn't supported by experiments

There's also a theory that stripes cool the body. Black stripes absorb more of the sun's heat, so they get hotter than white stripes. A study that measured two live zebras in Kenya reported that during the day, the black stripes were 12–15°C hotter than the white stripes.

The idea is that this temperature difference creates small airflows over the stripes (**convection**, where warmed air rises and cooled air sinks), helping sweat dry faster and cooling the body.

Gábor Horváth and his colleagues in Hungary tested this experimentally. They covered metal barrels filled with water with zebra, horse, and cattle hides, as well as black, white, gray, and striped patterns, left them outdoors for four months, and kept measuring the temperature inside.

The striped barrels showed surface temperature differences just like real zebras. Even so, their internal temperatures showed no clear difference compared with the gray barrels. This was true even on hot days. The paper concluded that striped coats don't keep the body any cooler than gray coats.

In 2021, the same group examined the airflow over stripes using a special camera that captures fluctuations in the air. No flow of cooled air sinking over the white stripes formed, and the stripes' influence reached only 1–2 centimeters from the surface. Even a slight breeze makes that airflow disappear.

### Summary: bug defense is the leading answer; cooling looks unlikely

For now, the role of stripes with the strongest evidence is warding off horseflies and other bloodsucking flies. As for the theory that stripes cool the body, every experiment that has tested it has found no effect.

That said, exactly why horseflies fail to land on stripes, and how the stripes look to them, is still being studied. Rather than declaring "the answer is bug defense," it's probably best to think of it as "the most likely answer."
