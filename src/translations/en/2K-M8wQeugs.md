### The Japan Meteorological Agency's definition is practically the answer

Let's start with the definition. Here's the entry from the Japan Meteorological Agency's forecasting glossary:

> Turbulence: Turbulent flow in the atmosphere. Usually refers to disturbances in airflow that shake an aircraft in flight. It often occurs due to the influence of mountain waves, cumulonimbus clouds, and the like, and at high altitudes near the jet stream, clear-air turbulence (CAT) may also occur.

That lists three causes of shaking: mountain waves, cumulonimbus clouds, and the area near the jet stream. Of these, only cumulonimbus clouds are something you can actually see as a cloud.

In other words, the experience of "the plane shakes when it goes through clouds" is real, but the cause and effect are backwards. The clouds aren't shaking the plane. Rather, **clouds form in places where the air is moving violently enough to shake it**.

### A cumulonimbus is a cloud that makes "updrafts visible"

The Japan Meteorological Agency's explanation of cumulonimbus clouds captures the essence in a single sentence:

> A cumulonimbus is a cloud that has developed dramatically in the vertical direction due to strong updrafts.

A cloud stretching upward is proof that the air is moving violently up and down. The outline of a towering thunderhead is literally the shape of a column of rising air.

The same page also says, "The towering thunderheads often seen in summer are also cumulonimbus clouds." From the ground, they're just part of the summer scenery, but from an airplane's point of view, they're zones packed with strong up-and-down currents. Fly into an updraft and the plane gets lifted; fly into a downdraft and it drops. The shaking is strongest when the plane crosses the boundary between neighboring currents.

That's why planes avoid cumulonimbus clouds. Instead of flying through, they go around, climb over the top, or change altitude. When the captain announces, "We'll be diverting around some clouds," it isn't about the view—it's about physics.

### "Clear-air turbulence": shaking with no clouds at all

Going back to the Japan Meteorological Agency's definition, it mentions one more type:

> At high altitudes near the jet stream, clear-air turbulence (CAT) may also occur.

CAT stands for Clear-Air Turbulence. The plane suddenly shakes in a cloudless blue sky with no warning. This is the trickiest type, and when it strikes while the seatbelt sign is off, people get hurt.

The cause is the boundary where layers of air moving at different speeds slide past each other. The jet stream is a band of strong wind flowing high in the sky, and along its edges the difference in wind speed is large. Just as eddies form between a river's main current and the water near the bank, eddies also form at the boundaries between layers of air.

The same phenomenon shows up when you look at the stars. Here's an explanation from the Subaru Telescope:

> The twinkling of stars is caused by fluctuations in Earth's atmosphere.

The air disturbances that shake airplanes and the air disturbances that make stars twinkle are the same thing. One is felt as the aircraft's acceleration; the other is seen as flickering light.

### Mountains shake the air, too

The third cause, "mountain waves," is just what it sounds like. When wind hits a mountain range, the air on the downwind side undulates up and down after crossing the mountains. Like the ripples that linger after you drop a stone into water, these air waves can reach places far away from the mountains.

Since terrain doesn't move, the places and wind directions where mountain waves tend to form are fairly well known. Japan is very mountainous, and strong westerly winds blow in winter, so the conditions line up easily. Flights that cross the mountains from the Sea of Japan side to the Pacific side tend to be bumpy in winter because they're flying over these waves.

### Bottom line: clouds aren't the cause—they're a "marker"

To sum up:

- Planes shake because of turbulence, which means "turbulent flow in the atmosphere"
- A cumulonimbus is a cloud that stands as the result of strong updrafts, so turbulence is present there
- So clouds aren't the cause; they're a **visible marker** of turbulence
- There's also turbulence with no marker (clear-air turbulence and mountain waves), and that kind is trickier

"If you see clouds, it might get bumpy" is true. But "if you don't see clouds, it won't get bumpy" is not. That's exactly why it's worth keeping your seatbelt fastened.
