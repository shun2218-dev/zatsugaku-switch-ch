### How far "it's not just your imagination" holds up

Headaches or a heavy, sluggish feeling when the weather turns bad are sometimes called "weather pain" or "meteoropathy" (*tenki-tsu* and *kisho-byo* in Japanese). Jun Sato of Chubu University, known for his research on weather-related pain, reports that in an online survey, about 60% of respondents said they noticed pain caused by the weather. (He also notes that because it was an online survey, the respondents may not be a representative group.)

There are research examples, too. In one study, 28 migraine patients living near Utsunomiya, Japan, kept a headache diary for a year. Migraines were more likely when air pressure dropped by 5 hPa (hectopascals) or more from the day of the headache to the next day.

On the other hand, looking across the research as a whole, the results are mixed. A 2024 review paper says that air pressure, humidity, and wind may be related to migraine, but that recent studies don't agree with each other and no cause-and-effect relationship has been seen between weather and when migraines occur. How susceptible people are varies a lot from person to person, and weather is thought to be more likely to trigger a headache when it overlaps with other factors.

The video's line "it's not just your imagination" fits the research in the sense that **some people are sensitive to the weather**. But it doesn't happen to everyone, and the mechanism is still being worked out.

### The pressure change is smaller than you might think

As the video says, when a low-pressure system approaches, the force of the surrounding air pushing on your body (air pressure) gets weaker. But the change is small. Normal air pressure at ground level is about 1013 hPa, and when a low-pressure system or typhoon passes, the change is around 20 hPa in a day at most (sometimes more). That's only about 2%.

So why do some people still feel different? That's exactly what researchers are trying to figure out.

### "Blood vessels and the inner ear swell" hasn't been confirmed

The video said the pressure imbalance "makes blood vessels and the inner ear more likely to swell ever so slightly," but this isn't an explanation backed by solid evidence. Sato himself writes that for the idea that tissues and blood vessels swell under low pressure, "no clear evidence has been shown."

The "plugged-up ears" feeling also needs some sorting out. When your ears feel plugged during a plane's takeoff and landing or while diving, it's because a pressure difference builds up between the outside and the "middle ear," the space just behind the eardrum. Normally, air moves in and out through a tube connected to the back of the nose (the Eustachian tube), keeping the pressure on both sides of the eardrum equal, but when pressure changes suddenly, this adjustment can't always keep up. The "inner ear" mentioned in the video is a separate organ even deeper inside, which senses sound and the tilt of your body. Weather-related pressure changes are much slower and smaller than those on a plane, so it isn't clear whether the same mechanism explains them.

### The inner ear "pressure sensor" theory

The leading idea right now is that **the inner ear senses changes in air pressure and sends that signal to the brain**. The video presented this as something that "is thought to" happen, which is an appropriate way to put it.

The evidence comes from animal experiments by Sato and his colleagues:

- In rats with chronic pain, lowering the air pressure made pain behaviors stronger, and when the inner ear was destroyed, that change no longer appeared.
- When mice were placed in a chamber where the pressure could be adjusted, and the pressure was lowered by 40 hPa from 1013 hPa for 50 minutes, markers of nerve activity increased in part of the brain region that receives signals from the inner ear (the vestibular nucleus).

The paper says a similar mechanism may be at work in humans as well. Sato also notes that people with weather pain tend to feel dizziness more easily when it's induced artificially. However, studies that directly test how the human inner ear senses pressure changes on the scale of weather are still limited.

### Can we say "the autonomic nervous system is the reason"?

At the end, the video flatly stated that "this link between pressure changes and the autonomic nervous system is the reason," but this too is still **one leading hypothesis**. In animal experiments, Sato and his colleagues showed that the sympathetic nervous system (one part of the autonomic nervous system) is involved in how changes in pressure and temperature intensify pain, and they think not only low pressure but also cold and high humidity can act as triggers.

As for typhoon season and the rainy season, a survey Sato cited found that in Hokkaido, which has no rainy season, a smaller share of people reported weather pain than in other regions. That result suggests seasons and climate may play a role, but it, too, is an inference based on an online survey.

### If you're struggling

The link between weather and how you feel differs from person to person, and other conditions such as migraine or vertigo may be behind your symptoms. If headaches, dizziness, or ringing in your ears are hard to bear, keep coming back, or feel stronger than usual, don't try to judge it on your own—talk to a medical professional.
