### Light bends in air of different temperatures

When air warms up, it expands and becomes less dense (lighter). And when light passes through a boundary between air of different densities, its direction bends slightly (this is called refraction). The video's explanation that "light bends every time it passes through layers of air with different densities" is exactly right.

Normally, the air is at roughly the same temperature throughout, so light travels in a nearly straight line. But under the blazing sun, only the air right above the sun-heated ground warms up quickly, creating big unevenness in air temperature. When light coming from distant scenery passes through that air, it gets bent a little here and there, and its path is thrown off by the time it reaches your eyes.

### Not neat "layers," but air that's constantly mixing

The video described "layers with uneven temperatures and densities," but to put the reason for the wavering a bit more precisely, it's **because the air is constantly moving and mixing**.

Air warmed by the ground rises as an updraft. As it does, it churns together with the cooler air around it, so pockets of warm and cool air mix in small blobs that keep changing shape from moment to moment (turbulence). Since the way light bends changes each time, the scenery never settles and keeps wavering. The video's opening line, "because the air itself is moving," and its closing line, "the air rippling like a lens," capture this nicely.

If the warm and cool air stayed calm in neat layers, the scenery might be shifted or distorted, but it wouldn't keep wavering. On a hot road, you sometimes see what looks like a puddle of water on the pavement in the distance. This "road mirage" (called nigemizu, or "escaping water," in Japanese) happens when light is bent by the particular way temperatures are stacked, with the air near the ground especially hot, so the sky and scenery look reflected on the road surface. It's a type of mirage (an inferior mirage). Heat haze and road mirages belong to the same family, in that both involve light bending in air of different temperatures.

### Not just a summer-road phenomenon

In haiku, heat haze is a seasonal word for spring, but an encyclopedia notes that it is "not a phenomenon that occurs only in spring." It can happen in any season whenever sunlight heats the ground, and you can also see scenery waver above a campfire or a barbecue grill plate. All of these share the same mechanism: air strongly heated from below mixing with the air around it.

### Measuring "ground heat" by the strength of the shimmer

Heat haze is also used as a research tool. According to a report from the University of Tsukuba, a device called a "scintillometer" sends infrared light from a transmitter placed 100 meters or more away (up to about 5 kilometers for large models) and measures how much the strength of the light reaching the receiver fluctuates; in other words, the "strength of the heat haze." From those fluctuations, it can calculate how much heat the ground is using to warm the air, averaged over a wide area.

### Twinkling stars are part of the same family

The encyclopedia also says that the twinkling of stars (scintillation) has a cause similar to heat haze. As starlight passes through Earth's atmosphere, it's bent bit by bit by unevenness in temperature and density, so the star's brightness and position appear to flicker slightly. You could say heat haze happens "just above the ground" and star twinkling happens "in the atmosphere high up," making them related phenomena.
