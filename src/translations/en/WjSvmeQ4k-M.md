### Snow sounds come from grains rubbing together

When you walk on a snowy path, all sorts of sounds come from under your feet.

The Nakaya Ukichiro Museum of Snow and Ice in Kaga, Ishikawa Prefecture, explains this sound in an answer to a question from a child. Their answer: the sound you hear when you step on snow is thought to come from the grains of piled-up snow rubbing against one another. Depending on the size and hardness of those grains, the sound changes in all kinds of ways.

The museum also offers an everyday comparison. If you squeeze a bag of flour from your kitchen from the outside, it sometimes makes a squeaky sound. That's tiny particles of flour rubbing against each other, and according to the museum, it works the same way as the sound of snow.

### Different snow, different sounds

To sum up the museum's explanation, the sound changes depending on the condition of the snow.

- Freshly fallen, fluffy snow: not much sound
- Older snow that has turned into *zarame* (coarse, granular snow that looks like sugar crystals): a crunching sound
- Fine grains packed hard at very low temperatures: a squeak-squeak sound, quite high-pitched

Part of why fresh snow is so quiet is that snow absorbs sound. We cover that in "[Why does a town feel so quiet after fresh snow?](/trivia/hG0oFCSFSnM/)"

### On warm days, water makes the grains slide

So why does the sound change with temperature? The museum explains it by comparing snow to a sandy beach.

When you walk on a dry beach, the grains of sand rub against each other and make a sound. Wet sand doesn't make much sound. That's because the water on the surface of the sand grains makes them slide past each other easily.

The same thing happens with snow. When it's warm and the snow grains are slightly melted, there isn't much sound. When it gets cold and the grains freeze all the way to the surface, sound comes more easily.

When the temperature drops even further, the grains of piled-up snow get smaller, and their surfaces dry out completely. According to the museum, those small grains rubbing against each other produce a slightly higher squeak-squeak sound.

### The rough threshold: −10°C

Some sources give a number for where the line falls between sound and no sound.

A weather explainer page from the University of Wisconsin–Madison in the US says that when snow is warmer than about −10°C, it doesn't make a sound. The explanation is that the snow under your shoes melts a little and "flows." When it's colder than −10°C, the snow doesn't melt, and the ice crystals get crushed under your shoes, producing a creaking sound.

Montana State University Library has published a real recording of the sound in the Acoustic Atlas, its collection of natural sounds. It was recorded on packed-down snow on a day when the temperature was 13°F (about −10.6°C).

In the video, we said that "on slightly warmer days, around −5°C, the squeak is said to be less likely to happen." Within the sources we checked, we couldn't find any that gave −5°C itself as a figure. The University of Wisconsin uses −10°C as its rough threshold, and −5°C falls on the warmer side of that.

Also, it's not as if there's no sound at all on milder days. As the museum explains, granular *zarame* snow makes a different, crunching sound. What becomes less likely is that high-pitched squeak-squeak.

### Actually, nobody fully knows yet

The explanations so far differ slightly from source to source.

- Nakaya Ukichiro Museum of Snow and Ice: the sound of grains rubbing together. When there's water on the surface, they slide and the sound is less likely
- University of Wisconsin: in cold snow, ice crystals get crushed and make the sound

In a 2015 article, the science magazine Scientific American wrote that the squeak of snow is "barely studied and poorly understood."

In that article, Craig Carter, a materials scientist at the Massachusetts Institute of Technology, offered another idea. When piled-up snow sits in the cold for a while, very tiny ice bridges form between the grains. When you step on the snow, these bridges snap one after another, from the top down. That, he suggests, might be the squeaking sound.

Carter also said that freshly fallen dry snow just goes "poof" when you step on it and doesn't squeak. But he himself cautioned that this is still at the "idea" stage, and that he isn't strongly confident in it.

"Grains rubbing together," "grains getting crushed," "bridges snapping." No single one has been settled as the right answer, and maybe several are happening at the same time. What is certain is that whether there's water on the surface of the snow grains makes a big difference in the sound.

### Your footsteps are a little thermometer

The museum ends its answer with a suggestion for the child who asked the question: when snow piles up in winter, try listening for what sounds you hear as the temperature and the snow conditions change.

If your very first step out the front door in the morning goes "squeak," it's a pretty cold day. Even on the same road, as the temperature rises around midday, the sound gets lower and quieter.

Listen closely to your footsteps, and you can get a sense of how cold it is that day without even looking at a thermometer.
