### Morning fog is called "radiation fog"

The fog that shows up on clear, calm mornings has a name: **radiation fog**. It's fog that forms because the ground cools off during the night.

The Nagano Local Meteorological Office describes radiation fog, also called "basin fog," as the signature fog of autumn in Nagano Prefecture. According to its explanation, it tends to form on clear nights with light winds, when the area is covered by a migratory high-pressure system.

There's also an official rule for what counts as fog. According to the same office's page, "fog" is when tiny water droplets reduce visibility to less than 1 kilometer.

### On clear nights, heat escapes from the ground

During the day, the ground soaks up heat from the sun. At night, it gives that heat off toward the sky. This is called **radiative cooling**.

Here's how the Nagano Local Meteorological Office explains it. On clear nights with light winds, the ground releases heat into the atmosphere, so the temperature drops. The air touching the ground cools down too, until it can't hold any more water vapor. The extra water vapor then turns into tiny water droplets, and that becomes fog.

The U.S. National Weather Service (NWS) also explains that radiation fog forms when the air near the ground cools overnight. It notes that this type of fog is especially common in fall and winter.

Things are different on cloudy nights, because clouds make it harder for heat to escape. We cover this in more detail in "[Why do cloudy nights stay warmer?](/trivia/jro444JzT9c/)"

### When the wind is strong, fog can't form

The NWS writes that wind makes it harder for radiation fog to form. The places it lists as fog-prone are sheltered valleys with almost no wind, and areas near water.

So why does wind get in the way? According to an NWS guide for forecasters, strong winds mix the air near the ground with air from higher up. The air higher up is usually drier than the air near the ground. When that dry air mixes in, the air near the ground dries out too, and fog can't form.

In the video, we said the air near the ground "gets stirred together with the warmer air above, so it never fully cools." The reason the NWS guide gives is that the air above is "drier." Either way, the point is the same: strong winds dilute the cool, moist air near the ground.

### With no wind, you tend to get dew instead

So is it best to have no wind at all? Actually, not quite.

The same NWS guide says that "fog needs some mixing from the wind; without wind, you get dew instead of fog."

Material from Australia's Bureau of Meteorology explains the same thing. With no wind at all, the cooling may stay within just a few centimeters of the ground, and the result can be nothing more than a heavy dew. Dew is when water droplets form directly on cold grass or ground. That's different from fog, where droplets float in the air.

With a light breeze, the air chilled by the ground gets mixed upward a little. That makes the cold layer thicker, so the fog gets thicker too. The Bureau of Meteorology lists "light winds (usually 5 knots or less)" as an ideal condition for radiation fog, explaining that this breeze mixes the cooled air up to a few hundred feet above the ground. Five knots works out to about 2.6 meters per second (roughly 6 mph).

In other words, the sweet spot for fog is a wind that's "not too strong, but not zero."

### Fog grows from the bottom up

According to the NWS, radiation fog first forms at or just above the ground. As the air keeps cooling, the fog gets denser. The air above the fog layer cools as well, so the fog thickens upward through the night.

Radiation fog also tends to stay in one place. The Japan Meteorological Agency's Meteorological Satellite Center explains that radiation fog often forms along terrain like valleys and basins, and spreads out in the same spot over time.

Fog rising over rivers forms a little differently. We cover that in "[Why does river fog hang over rivers on cold mornings?](/trivia/S11MFGRYF2Y/)"

### Once the sun comes up, it vanishes

Radiation fog doesn't last long.

According to the Meteorological Satellite Center, once sunlight starts hitting it in the morning, radiation fog clears quickly. The Bureau of Meteorology also writes that radiation fog usually lifts a few hours after sunrise. The ground warms up, the air above it warms up, and the droplets turn back into water vapor.

That said, when the fog is very thick, or when clouds above the fog weaken the sunlight, it can take longer to clear.

That's why a road that's white with fog in the morning is often clear by the time the commute and school run are over.
