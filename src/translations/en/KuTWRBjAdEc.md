### Chapter 1: The Invisible Rules on the Pitch

**Offside exists to ban "goal-hanging"**

Offside is a rule that makes it a foul when a player receiving a pass is ahead of the second-to-last opponent. Since the goalkeeper is usually the farthest back, the reference point is effectively the last outfield player.

Without this rule, the attacking side could score just by keeping a player parked in front of goal. The very idea of defending would fall apart. So offside is a mechanism that makes the battle of wits between attack and defense possible.

→ [Why is offside decided by the "second-to-last" player?](/trivia/DAmkVLn-OAA/)

**The 11 m penalty distance is just 12 yards restated**

The 11 m penalty mark is also a number that comes from yards. Twelve yards is 10.9728 m, rounded to 11 m. It was set more than 100 years ago, balancing the limit of what a goalkeeper can react to against a distance from which the kicker can reliably aim.

Goalkeepers are said to have to react within about 0.3 seconds of the ball being kicked, and if they wait to see the kicker start moving, they're too late. So they end up betting on reading the kicker's preliminary movements and guessing.

→ [Why is the ball in a penalty shootout placed about 11 m from the goal?](/trivia/nvG99prTRtU/) and [Why is the shootout order a disadvantage for goalkeepers?](/trivia/SxdWIiT24pM/)

**Cards were born to cross the language barrier**

Here's the Japan Football Association's Q&A:

> The yellow card is shown by referees when they "caution" a player who has committed a serious foul or unsporting behavior, but until the 1960s, referees delivered "cautions" verbally. The yellow card was first introduced at the 1968 Mexico City Olympics.

Verbal warnings don't get through at international tournaments where players speak different languages. On top of that, spectators and the benches can't follow them, so a system like "sent off after a second card" can't really work. A color, on the other hand, is understood by anyone instantly.

Note that sources differ on the year of introduction: the Japanese encyclopedia Nihon Daihyakka Zensho gives the 1970 World Cup in Mexico, while Britannica says the idea came about in 1976.

→ [Why were yellow and red cards created?](/trivia/qpKSPk-d7KE/)

### Chapter 2: The 90 Minutes and the Players' Roles

**The 90 minutes started with one match in 1866**

The Japan Football Association's explanation of this is wonderfully specific:

> The Football Association (The FA) was founded in England in 1863, and the first rules of football were set, but even these did not specify the length of a match. In 1866, before a match between an FA side and a Sheffield side, it was decided that "play shall begin at 3 p.m. and end at 4:30 p.m.," and this is where "90 minutes" finally appears, though there was still no such thing as halftime.

**From 3 p.m. to 4:30 p.m.** The 90-minute length wasn't based on science; it was simply the gap between the times set for one particular match.

After that, it gradually became formalized:

> Then, in 1871, the FA Cup rules stated: "The duration of the match shall be one and a half hours. The interval at halftime shall not exceed five minutes, except where specially permitted by the referee."
> In 1897, it was codified in the Laws of the Game: "The duration of the match shall be 90 minutes unless otherwise mutually agreed. The interval at halftime shall not exceed five minutes except with the consent of the referee."

The 90 minutes that appeared in 1866 was written into the Laws of the Game in 1897. That took 31 years. Halftime, too, was originally just five minutes.

> Today, the rule is that "the half-time interval must not exceed 15 minutes."

→ [Why is soccer two 45-minute halves, 90 minutes in total?](/trivia/W6UgPMwFgFQ/)

**The captain's armband designates a single negotiator**

It's there to make clear who the representative is that can deal with the referee during a match. If it's unclear who the negotiator is, confusion results, so this makes responsibility clear.

The Japan Football Association also explains "Captains Only," which takes this idea further:

> This is the concept that, on the condition that they conduct themselves respectfully and appropriately during the match, only the captain may ask the referee questions about decisions and the like.
> It was formally approved by the International Football Association Board (IFAB) in 2024 and trialed at UEFA EURO 2024, organized by the Union of European Football Associations (UEFA).

→ [Why can only one player wear the captain's armband in soccer?](/trivia/TBNIBAF5fQQ/)

**"Volante" is Portuguese for steering wheel**

It took root in Japan (pronounced "boranchi") as the term for a midfielder who controls the flow of the game, like the one steering a car.

The name "soccer" itself also has its reasons in the history of the word. It was British student slang that shortened "Association" from the official name, Association Football. Even in Britain, the sport's homeland, it was in ordinary use until the mid-20th century, and from around the 1980s people began avoiding it, mistakenly believing it was American English.

→ [Why does the soccer term "volante" mean steering wheel?](/trivia/JA7TKBE1Jn8/) and [Why the same sport is called both "soccer" and "football"](/trivia/yu9ygwKK60o/)

### Chapter 3: Behind the Scenes at the Stadium

**The stripes on the grass come from mowing direction**

The grass isn't a different type. When you change the mower's direction on each row, the direction the grass blades lean changes, and so does the way light reflects. It's an optical illusion.

The stripes are also said to help players as markers for judging distance and speed.

→ [Why does the grass on soccer fields have stripes?](/trivia/g2aW06RhM3k/)

**Goalkeepers wear different colors so they can be identified**

Only goalkeepers have the special rule allowing them to use their hands. To make foul calls quickly, a visual distinction is essential.

→ [Why do only goalkeepers wear a different-colored uniform?](/trivia/7tIWEuAOSJM/)

**Pentagons and hexagons get flat pieces close to a sphere**

It's an arrangement for combining flat pieces into a shape as close to a sphere as possible. Twelve pentagons and 20 hexagons form a solid called a truncated icosahedron.

→ [Why do soccer balls have a pentagon and hexagon pattern?](/trivia/iZUFamkQQ0I/)

**Referees run 10 km to get a good viewing angle**

The accuracy of a call is directly tied to the referee's distance and angle from the play. That's why referees keep changing their position to follow the players' movements.

Referees assigned to J.League (Japan's professional soccer league) matches can't officiate unless they pass a fitness test: six 40 m sprints, each completed within 6 seconds, plus 40 interval runs.

→ [Why do soccer referees run about 10 km during a match?](/trivia/HA9S5zn0TjA/) and [How do soccer referees keep running for 90 minutes without getting tired?](/trivia/e4W-DWQRcEw/)

### What They All Have in Common

Line up all 12 facts, and they fall into two categories.

**Things that were designed**: offside, the penalty distance, the card colors, the captain's armband, the goalkeeper's colors, the ball's pattern. Each was intentionally created for the sake of "fairness" or "clarity."

**Things that accumulated**: the 90 minutes, "volante," the name "soccer," the stripes on the grass. Rather than being decided by someone, these are customs or coincidences that took hold.

The 90 minutes is the most telling example. The gap between start and end times set for a single match in 1866 became a rule over 31 years, and 160 years later it's the standard the whole world uses. It's a case of coincidence becoming institution.

Next time you watch a match, try asking whether what you're seeing was "designed" or "accumulated." It might look a little different.
