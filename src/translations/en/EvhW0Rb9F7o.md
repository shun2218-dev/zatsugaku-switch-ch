### How are whiskers different from ordinary hair?

Cat whiskers are thicker and longer than the hairs covering the rest of the body. But the difference goes deeper than looks.

A review paper (a paper summarizing earlier research) published in a veterinary journal in 1986 lists several features of whiskers. The follicles that whiskers grow from are much larger than those of body hair and are richly supplied with nerves. The follicles contain a sac-like tissue filled with blood. And each individual whisker corresponds to its own specific spot in the part of the brain that handles the senses.

According to this review, whiskers are also called "tactile hairs" in English. Nearly all mammals except humans have them.

### A "blood sac" and nerves at the root

Let's take a closer look at the base of a whisker.

The review describes the whisker follicle as a highly complex structure, richly supplied with both nerves and blood. Each follicle has one or more blood-filled sacs. Around those sacs are various kinds of **receptors**, which are the parts that pick up a stimulus and turn it into a nerve signal.

A 2002 study used a special microscope to examine the roots of rat and cat whiskers in three dimensions. It identified at least eight types of nerve endings with different shapes.

A 2025 paper on dog whiskers includes photos of whisker roots taken from a puppy's upper lip. The root, wrapped in its blood sac, could be seen with the naked eye and was about the size of a small grain of rice. The same paper also cites earlier research showing that whisker follicles in dogs and cats are very similar.

### Signaling both how far and how fast

So what kind of signals travel through the nerves when a whisker moves?

In a 1973 study, researchers moved the whiskers of anesthetized cats in controlled ways and recorded the nerve signals. They found four types of nerves, each with a different signaling pattern.

Two types kept firing for as long as the whisker stayed bent. The spacing between their signals changed depending on how far the whisker had moved. The other two types responded only at the moment the whisker moved. One responded only to fast movements, while the other also responded to slow ones.

In other words, whiskers don't just report that "something touched me." They also convey how far and how fast they moved. All of the nerves carrying these signals were of the fast-conducting type.

### Cats have whiskers on their front legs, too

When we think of whiskers, we picture the ones around the mouth. But hairs with the same structure grow in other places as well.

The 1973 study examined not only the whiskers around the mouth but also hairs on a cat's front wrists. The follicles of these hairs produced movement signals just like whiskers.

The review lists places where mammals have whiskers: around the mouth, above the eyes, on the cheeks, near the wrists of the front legs, and so on. Older studies reported that wrist whiskers are found in animals that use their front legs for climbing trees or grabbing things, but not in animals that use them only for walking.

### An experiment in a dark maze

The video said that whiskers help cats feel out their surroundings in the dark. There's an experiment that tested this.

In a 1986 study, cats ran through a maze, and researchers timed how long it took them to reach the goal. Even cats that could see performed worse in the dark. And when their whiskers were made unusable, their performance dropped even further. However, this drop happened only in the dark. In the light, losing the whiskers made no difference.

So in the light, cats rely on their eyes, and in the dark, the information from their whiskers starts to matter. To learn how cats' eyes work in the dark, see "[Why do cats' and dogs' eyes glow in the dark?](/trivia/5PQMhtb_khY/)"

### Standing in for the eyes, right up close

The review also introduces another idea about what cat whiskers do.

Cats are great at seeing things far away, but not so good at focusing on things up close. So whiskers may be standing in for the eyes in the space right around the face.

The review adds that whiskers probably also help protect the face and eyes from scratches. As support for this, it cites an experiment in which stimulating the area around a cat's whiskers caused the eyeball on the same side to retract.

The link to hunting has been studied, too. According to the review, when a cat catches a mouse, its eyes are key for spotting prey at a distance. But at the final moment of the bite, touch, including from the whiskers, becomes especially important.

### Do cats really measure gaps with their whiskers?

The video said that whiskers "are also thought to give cats clues about whether they can squeeze through a tight space."

Among the papers read for this article, none described an experiment directly confirming that cats measure the width of a gap with their whiskers. The review also notes that there's little research on how whiskers work in domestic animals, and that much remains to be studied.

Experiments have confirmed that whiskers sense objects around the face. The idea that they measure gap widths is best seen as an explanation inferred from that.

What's certain is that whiskers are an important sensory tool. The 2025 paper on dog whiskers concludes that, because whiskers are functional sensory organs, they shouldn't be trimmed for appearance's sake.
