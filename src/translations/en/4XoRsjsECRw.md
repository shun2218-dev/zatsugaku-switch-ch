### 1. Your recorded voice sounds like someone else

This is a story about something "missing." An explainer from the University of Tokyo sums it up.

> There are two pathways for sound: "air-conducted sound," where vibrations in the air reach the eardrum, and "bone-conducted sound," where vibrations travel through bone. The voice we normally hear ourselves speaking is a mix of sound arriving through both of these pathways. A recorded voice, on the other hand, contains only air-conducted sound, so it sounds different. The voice other people hear when we speak is also air-conducted sound, the same as the recording.

The voice you hear every day includes a component that reaches your inner ear through your skull. A recording doesn't capture that.

And that last sentence is the key. The voice other people hear is the same as the recording. So the strange one isn't the recording; it's the special version only you get to hear.

The properties of sound traveling through bone have also been measured. Research at the Japan Advanced Institute of Science and Technology has confirmed that, compared with air conduction, bone conduction attenuates high-frequency components. With fewer high tones, the proportion of low tones goes up, so your own voice sounds lower and fuller to you. That's why the recording sounds thin.

### 2. Food tastes bland when you have a cold

This is a story about things being "mixed." Here's an explainer from the Japan Society of Otorhinolaryngology-Head and Neck Surgery.

> The taste of food is created by a combination of the five basic tastes (sweet, salty, sour, bitter and umami) along with smell, texture, temperature, color and more.

The tongue handles just those five. The distinctive part that tells you "what flavor this is" comes from smell information. So when your nose shuts down,

> food becomes even harder to taste, and meals stop being enjoyable. This is called flavor disorder. Even when food doesn't taste good, in many cases testing shows there's nothing wrong with the sense of taste itself.

It even has a name, **flavor disorder**, and the tongue is working fine. The very feeling that "my sense of taste has gone wrong" is proof that the brain treats taste and smell as a single thing.

### 3. Eyes glowing red in photos

Of the five, this is the only one with a different structure.

Voice and taste were both stories about "the sense itself being out of step with reality." But with red-eye, nothing is wrong with your eyes. Flash light enters through pupils that have opened wide in the dark, reflects off the blood-vessel-rich layer at the back of the eye, and shows up red just as it is. The eye is reflecting light accurately.

It's startling because the photo captures **a place you can never see yourself**. It's not a trick of the senses; it's a matter of viewpoint.

### 4. Yawns are contagious

From here on, we're talking about behavior rather than senses. Here's research from the University of Nottingham.

> Experts at the University of Nottingham have published research that suggests the human propensity for contagious yawning is triggered automatically by primitive reflexes in the primary motor cortex — an area of the brain responsible for motor function.

It's triggered automatically by primitive reflexes in the primary motor cortex. And holding it in doesn't work.

> our ability to resist yawning when someone else near us yawns is limited. And our urge to yawn is increased if we are instructed to resist yawning. But, no matter how hard we try to stifle a yawn, it might change how we yawn but it won’t alter our propensity to yawn.

Being told to resist made the urge stronger, and no matter how hard people stifled a yawn, their "susceptibility" itself didn't change.

Note that the popular mirror-neuron explanation doesn't yet have solid direct evidence. The same announcement states, "The neural basis for echophenomena is unknown." On the other hand, the idea that "yawns spread more easily between people who are close" has been confirmed in a separate observational study, in the order of relatives → friends → acquaintances → strangers.

### 5. Craving sweets on days you're short on sleep

This, too, is about the body, not willpower. Here's e-health net from Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare.

> Research has shown that even in healthy people, just a few days of sleep deprivation, compared with days of sufficient sleep, reduces the secretion of leptin, a hormone that suppresses appetite, and conversely increases the secretion of ghrelin, a hormone that stimulates appetite, leading to increased appetite.

The brake (leptin) loosens and the gas pedal (ghrelin) gets pressed. Both move at the same time. What matters is the conditions: "even in healthy people" and "just a few days."

The same page notes that sugar processing gets worse, too.

> It has also become clear that the action of insulin secreted by the pancreas becomes less effective (insulin resistance), so blood sugar rises higher even after eating the same meal.

Not only do you crave sweets, but your blood sugar also rises more easily when you eat them. Sweets on a sleep-deprived day are a double disadvantage.

### Line up the five, and they split into two groups

The video wraps up by saying "at the root they're the same thing," but if we sort them out a bit, it looks like this.

**(A) The brain is mixing or filling in information**
- Your recorded voice (adding air-conducted and bone-conducted sound together)
- Taste with a cold (adding taste and smell together)

**(B) The brain and body decide actions and desires without our noticing**
- Contagious yawning (a motor cortex reflex)
- Sleep loss and sweets (hormone balance)

**(C) The sense is accurate; it's startling because it's "a place you can't see"**
- Red-eye

A and B share something in common: "you can't double-check your own senses or judgments yourself." Only C is a case where the sense itself is correct; you just can't get the viewpoint.

What all of them have in common is that the assumption "I know myself better than anyone" is more fragile than we think. The recording is what your voice really sounds like, the yawn you can't hold back is built in, and craving sweets is the result of hormones. The more something sounds familiar, the more likely the reason lies outside of you.
