### Windows fog up because the glass cools the air

In winter, indoor air holds a lot of water vapor from heating, people's breath, cooking, and so on. When this air touches glass chilled by the cold outside, the air right next to the glass gets cooled.

The Japan Meteorological Agency describes the temperature at which cooling air starts turning its water vapor into water droplets as the "dew point." If the glass surface is colder than this, water vapor in the air turns into droplets on the glass. That's condensation. A window looks white and foggy because it's covered edge to edge with extremely tiny droplets.

### Why small droplets don't fall

Freshly formed droplets don't run down right away, even on a vertical window. While they're small, they weigh very little, and the force holding them to the glass wins out.

According to classic research by physicist Yakov Frenkel, a droplet on a tilted surface stays in place until it passes a certain limit, because the angle it makes with the surface (the contact angle) changes between its front and back. Very small droplets may not fall even when the surface is vertical or upside down.

In the video, we said droplets "get caught on tiny bumps and bits of dirt on the glass surface and stay put." Slight irregularities on the glass do snag the edges of droplets and make them harder to move, so that explanation is broadly correct. But it's also worth knowing that even without any dirt, **small droplets can stay put on their own**.

As condensation continues, droplets slowly grow and merge with their neighbors when they touch. Once a droplet's weight overcomes the "clinging force," it starts to slide. The video's line, "they join and get heavier, until gravity wins and it starts to slide down," is exactly right.

### The trail left behind becomes a "pathway"

Frenkel noted that on surfaces water wets easily, a sliding droplet leaves an extremely thin trail of water behind it, moving along **as if paving its own road**. After a while, this thin trail breaks up into tiny droplets.

In experiments presented by a researcher at Université Paris Cité in France, drops sliding fairly quickly down a slope were observed leaving lines of tiny droplets behind them, with the next drop picking up those leftovers as it moved along.

The video's line, "once water has run down a path, the glass there stays a little wet, which makes it easier for the next droplet to take the same route," fits with these ideas. The next droplet connects with and absorbs the water left in the trail as it goes, so it's thought to be more likely to follow the same path.

The clear streaks you see on a foggy window where droplets have passed are also because the droplets soak up the tiny droplets along their path as they fall. After a while, new condensation forms on those streaks too, and they fog up again.

### Not "always the same trail"

The video said droplets "tend to follow similar trails every time," but that's a bit of an overstatement.

- Water left in a trail eventually evaporates or breaks up into tiny beads.
- Droplets are growing elsewhere on the window too, so new trails can start from there.
- According to Frenkel, on surfaces that repel water, sliding droplets don't leave a trail. Greasy smudges, fingerprints, or water-repellent coatings on the glass can change how trails are left behind.

So it's more accurate to think of it as droplets "tending to run down the same trails." If you look closely at a window, you should also see trails merging partway down and new ones appearing.
