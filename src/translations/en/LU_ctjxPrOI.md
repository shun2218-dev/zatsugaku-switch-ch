### Giraffes and humans both have seven neck bones

The bones of the neck are called the **cervical vertebrae**. They're the part of the spine running from just below the head down to the chest.

Humans have seven cervical vertebrae. And giraffes, with those long necks, also have seven. A 2021 paper likewise notes that giraffes retain "the seven cervical vertebrae typical of mammals."

The necks are completely different lengths, yet the number of bones is the same. And this isn't just about giraffes.

### Almost all mammals have "seven"

In 1999, Galis, a researcher at Leiden University in the Netherlands, wrote a paper titled "Why do almost all mammals have seven cervical vertebrae?" In it, she describes the number of cervical vertebrae in mammals as "remarkably constant." Mice, a kind of rodent, also have seven.

Only a handful of mammals are exceptions. According to a 2018 paper, the only mammals that depart from seven are **sloths and manatees**.

- Two-toed sloths: 5–8
- Three-toed sloths: 8–10
- Manatees: 6

In sloths, the number even varies within the same species. These exceptions make it all the more striking that other mammals uniformly have seven.

In birds and reptiles, on the other hand, the number of neck bones varies widely. The 2018 paper mentions that chickens have 14 and geese have 17.

### Why is it so hard to change from seven?

Galis explained the reason in terms of **development** (the process by which a body forms from a fertilized egg).

The arrangement of the body from front to back is determined by **Hox genes** (a group of genes that decide what gets built where in the body). The number of cervical vertebrae changes when the way these genes work changes.

But Hox genes are also involved in nerve development and in how cells multiply. According to Galis, changes that alter the number of cervical vertebrae are linked to a higher likelihood of neurological problems, childhood cancers, and stillbirth. She also notes that in some childhood cancers in humans, the rate of **cervical ribs** (a small rib forming on the seventh cervical vertebra) appears to be higher.

In other words, in mammals, trying to change the number of neck bones tends to cause problems in other important places too. As a result, individuals that deviated from seven had a harder time surviving, so the number stayed consistent. That's the idea. In birds and reptiles, the harm from this linkage is smaller, which is why the number can change more easily, according to this explanation.

However, this is one leading explanation, and research is still ongoing, including into why sloths were able to become exceptions.

### Giraffes made their bones "longer"

So how did giraffes get their long necks? The answer is by making each individual bone longer.

A 2015 paper measured giraffe and okapi (a short-necked close relative of the giraffe) bones at the American Museum of Natural History. In adult giraffes, the neck bones (measured along the central part of the bone) from the second to the sixth were roughly 28–31 cm long. The third bone was 29.5–31 cm.

In okapis, that same third bone was about 10 cm. Each giraffe neck bone is about three times as long as an okapi's.

The video said each bone is "nearly 30 centimeters." Since some of the measured bones were slightly over 30 cm, it's more accurate to say "around 30 centimeters."

### The first chest bone works like a "neck bone"

The giraffe's neck has one more trick.

In 2016, Megu Gunji and Hideki Endo of the University of Tokyo studied the giraffe's **first thoracic vertebra** (the first bone of the chest). This bone is shaped much like the seventh cervical vertebra of typical relatives in the cattle and deer group.

In experiments bending the neck of giraffe carcasses, the first thoracic vertebra moved up and down more than the other chest vertebrae. Chest vertebrae are connected to ribs and normally don't move much, but in giraffes, small modifications at the joints reduced the obstruction to movement. The attachment points for the muscles that bend the neck downward had also shifted backward.

Gunji and her colleague concluded that this bone acts as a **pivot point** for moving the neck, greatly expanding how far the end of the neck can reach. This is thought to help when eating leaves high up, or when males fight by swinging their necks at each other.

A German research group in 2021 also confirmed that the giraffe's first thoracic vertebra has neck-bone-like features.

The video said giraffes "mainly stretched each individual bone longer." The part beyond that "mainly" is this trick in the chest bone.

### Summary: Keep the number, change the shape

Giraffes got their long necks while sticking to the mammalian "rule of seven," by lengthening their bones and changing how a chest bone moves.

The number of bones is hard to change. So instead of the number, the shape and movement change. The giraffe's neck is a great example of how evolution works by "remodeling what's already there."
