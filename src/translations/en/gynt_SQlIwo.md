### Water and oil don't mix

Let's start with the basics. Water molecules carry an uneven electrical charge, so they attract one another. Oil molecules have very little charge imbalance, so water molecules pretty much ignore them.

That's why water and oil separate. If you scrub greasy grime with water, the water just beads up on the oil instead of carrying it away.

### Surfactants reach out to both sides

A surfactant molecule has two parts:

- **Hydrophilic group**: the part that gets along with water
- **Lipophilic (hydrophobic) group**: the part that gets along with oil

One end of a single molecule loves water, and the other loves oil. These molecules can line up along the boundary between water and oil (the "interface") and link the two together. The name "surfactant" is short for "surface-active agent": a substance that works at surfaces and interfaces.

Picture something shaped like a matchstick. The head points toward the water, and the stick points toward the oil.

### They form clusters called micelles

Once surfactant molecules in water pass a certain concentration, they gather together on their own. Here's how the Japan Soap and Detergent Association explains it:

> Detergents deliver strong cleaning power when the molecules of their main ingredient, surfactants, disperse in water and form clusters called "micelles."

A micelle is a sphere with the oil-loving parts facing inward and the water-loving parts facing outward. The oil-loving parts hide inside, while the water-loving parts touch the surrounding water. This is the most stable arrangement in water.

The inside of this sphere is like a little "pocket of oil" inside the water. That's why it can take in oil.

### Teaming up with mechanical force to lift dirt

The same explanation breaks down what happens in the laundry into four elements:

> To thoroughly remove all these different kinds of dirt in a single wash, it's important to combine four things in the right balance: (1) the washing machine (mechanical force), (2) water, (3) the laundry, and (4) the detergent (surfactants).

Detergent alone won't do the job. You need mechanical force.

> This mechanical force and the water spread water all the way into the gaps between the fibers of the laundry, pulling the dirt off and into the water.

Then it explains the role of micelles:

> When the force of the washing machine is added, these micelles surround the dirt on the fibers more effectively and pull it away.

Surfactants attach to the surface of the dirt and work their way in between it and the fibers, making it easier to lift off. Then mechanical force comes in and physically peels it away. It's a team effort.

### The second job: keeping dirt from coming back

This is the function people tend to overlook the most.

> They also surround the dirt that has come loose in the water, preventing "redeposition," where dirt reattaches to fibers.

The loosened dirt gets trapped inside micelles and stays suspended in the water. Without this, all that dirt you worked to remove would just stick somewhere else.

The second half of washing is the job of "holding dirt in the water." So detergent isn't so much a "dirt remover" as something that handles the whole process: lifting the dirt, keeping it afloat, and rinsing it away.

[Grease becomes harder to remove once it cools](/trivia/ophThNRMZf8/) because the oil gets thicker and harder for micelles to take in. Hot water cleans better partly because the oil softens, making it easier for micelles to do their job.

### Not enough detergent, not enough cleaning

The explanation also includes some practical advice:

> However, when the machine is overloaded, there's also more dirt, so there aren't enough surfactant molecules. As a result, not enough micelles form in the water, and cleaning power drops.

How much detergent you need depends on how much dirt there is. Cram in too much laundry, and there won't be enough molecules for the amount of dirt, so micelles can't form.

On the other hand, adding more detergent won't help if there isn't enough water.

> If there isn't enough water, the laundry and water don't mix well, causing all sorts of problems: dirt doesn't come out as well, cleaning is uneven, and strong mechanical force can damage the fibers.

Which brings us back to the idea of balancing those four elements.

### The same molecules at work in many places

Surfactants aren't just for detergent.

- **Shampoo and body wash**: remove skin oils
- **Dish soap**: removes grease
- **Mayonnaise**: lecithin in egg yolk acts as a surfactant, blending oil and vinegar (emulsification)
- **Milk**: proteins keep fat dispersed in water
- **Inside your lungs**: a surface-active substance called pulmonary surfactant keeps the lungs from collapsing

The ability to "work at the boundary between water and oil" is useful not only for washing but also for mixing. Mayonnaise doesn't separate for the same reason detergent keeps dirt afloat.

The idea that [erasers, adhesive bandages, and detergent all work on the same principle](/trivia/S8dCPnNWKlA/) can also be explained by this "like sticks to like" property.
