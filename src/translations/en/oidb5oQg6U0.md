### Light bends and bounces inside raindrops

For a rainbow to form, the sun has to be behind you and rain has to be falling in front of you. The UK Met Office and the U.S. National Weather Service both list this as the first condition.

When sunlight enters a raindrop, it bends as it goes in (refraction), bounces once off the back of the drop (reflection), and bends again on the way out before reaching your eyes. According to an explanation from the Fukuoka Regional Headquarters of the Japan Meteorological Agency, the outgoing light is concentrated at a specific angle measured from the direction it came from. Because each color of light bends by a slightly different amount, the angle where each color concentrates also shifts a little, so the light splits into bands of seven colors.

### "42 degrees" is measured from your own head's shadow

The video said "exactly 42 degrees," so let's add a small clarification. According to Arizona State University course material, red light arrives from about 42 degrees and blue light from about 40 degrees. That's why a rainbow isn't a single line but a wide band, bluish-violet on the inside and red on the outside.

This angle is measured from **the direction of your own head's shadow**. If you extend the line from the sun through yourself, the point it reaches is called the "antisolar point," and on a sunny day the shadow of your head on the ground marks it. An explanation from Georgia State University also says that if you can see your head's shadow in the rain, you're in a position to see a rainbow. The same principle is why you see a rainbow in the spray when you water with a hose with the sun at your back.

Raindrops at the same angle from the antisolar point send you the same color, whether they're to the right or the left. So light of the same color lines up on a circle centered on the antisolar point. A rainbow is really a circle; the part below the ground is just missing from view. The Met Office also explains that from high places, such as an airplane, you can sometimes see a full circular rainbow.

### The rainbow next to you comes from different raindrops

Raindrops are also at varying distances from you, so the drops sending light to your rainbow are arranged on the surface of a cone with your eye at its tip. The Arizona State University material explains that if you move left or right, you're looking at different raindrops, which makes it a new rainbow. It also notes that even when you're watching a rainbow with a friend, the two of you are seeing different rainbows.

What's more, raindrops keep falling, so even if you stand in the same spot, the drops sending light to your rainbow are constantly being replaced. The same material describes a rainbow as a cone of color that countless raindrops create "only for an instant." You can't get closer to a rainbow by walking toward it because when you move, the cone with your eye at its tip moves with you. The video's phrase "a view that belongs only to you, standing right there at that moment" fits this mechanism perfectly.

The artificial rainbow exhibit at the Nagoya City Science Museum shows that if you look with one eye at a time, the rainbow's position appears to shift between your left and right eye. Even a slight difference in eye position changes the light that reaches you.

That said, the video's opening line, "the person next to you can't see it," goes too far. The person next to you sees a rainbow in nearly the same direction and the same shape. What differs is the raindrops creating it. The accurate way to put it is: "It looks like the same view, but the light reaching each of you is different."

### No rainbow when the sun is high, and a double rainbow's outer bow is reversed

According to the U.S. National Weather Service, when the sun is more than 42 degrees above the horizon, the rainbow sinks below the horizon and can't be seen. That's why you're more likely to spot rainbows in the morning or evening than at midday in summer. The lower the sun, the closer the rainbow gets to a large semicircle.

The outer bow of the double rainbows you sometimes see is called the "secondary rainbow." According to the Fukuoka Regional Headquarters of the Japan Meteorological Agency, it comes from light that bounces twice inside the raindrop before exiting; it appears outside the regular (primary) rainbow, at about 50 degrees, is wider, and has its colors in reverse order. Because the extra bounce weakens the light, it looks dimmer than the primary rainbow.
