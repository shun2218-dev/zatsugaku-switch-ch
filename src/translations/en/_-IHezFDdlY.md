### Pencil lead is basically pottery

There's no actual lead in pencil lead. According to the Japan Writing Instruments Manufacturers Association, the core is made of **graphite** (a black mineral made of carbon) and clay.

Here's how it's made:

1. Water is added to graphite and clay, which are ground fine and mixed thoroughly
2. The mixture is extruded to the thickness of a pencil core and cut into pieces about 20 cm (8 in) long
3. After drying, the pieces are fired in a kiln at about 1,000–1,200°C (1,830–2,190°F) until hard
4. They're soaked in hot oil so they glide smoothly

Firing clay at high temperatures to harden it is the same thing that's done with teacups and plates. A pencil core is like a thin stick of pottery.

Pottery is hard, but in exchange it barely flexes. When it's bent, it snaps before it can bend.

### The thinner the tip, the less there is to hold it up

When you write with a pencil, the tip meets the paper at an angle. That creates a force that tries to bend the lead.

If you sharpen the lead long and sharp, the end gets very thin. Even if you write with the same pressure, a thin section has less area to take that force, so it can't hold up and breaks more easily.

It's just like how a thick tree branch is hard to break, while a thin twig snaps right away.

Mitsubishi Pencil advises that "sharpening the lead too much can cause it to break." That's the manufacturer's warning mentioned in the video.

### Colored pencils break especially easily

Mitsubishi Pencil goes on to say that colored pencils have softer cores, so they can break especially easily.

Colored pencil cores are made differently. According to the Japan Writing Instruments Manufacturers Association, they don't use clay. Instead, pigments or dyes that provide the color are mixed with wax, talc (a powder made from a soft mineral), and so on. Because of that, they're hardened without firing.

The same association also explains that because colored pencil cores aren't fired, they're weaker in terms of strength. Colored pencils need to be handled more gently than regular pencils.

### B is softer than H

Even among regular pencils, the strength of the lead varies by type.

According to the Japan Writing Instruments Manufacturers Association, the hardness of the lead is determined by the ratio of graphite to clay. An HB pencil is about 70% graphite and about 30% clay. More graphite makes the lead darker and softer; more clay makes it lighter and harder.

Under JIS (Japanese Industrial Standards), there are 17 grades of lead hardness, from 6B to 9H. H stands for "Hard" and B for "Black." The F that sits between H and HB comes from "Firm."

Even though they're all "pencils," the higher the number in front of the B, the softer the lead.

### Sharpening can break it too

Over-sharpening isn't the only thing that breaks lead.

According to Mitsubishi Pencil, small handheld sharpeners (pocket sharpeners) have a single blade and are hard to keep steady while sharpening, so they tend to break the lead more easily. Turning both the sharpener and the pencil at the same time, or pushing the pencil in hard, puts stress on the lead.

When sharpening, don't push; just turn gently. Once the tip is pointed, stop. That's the trick to keeping it from breaking.

### The researchers who studied broken pencil tips

Broken pencil tips have even become a subject of research in mechanics.

Professor Petroski, who studied how things fail at Duke University in the US, looked back on it in a 2000 essay. He had come across a short paper about broken pencil points in a 1983 mechanics journal and read it with fascination. He then did his own calculations on what shape and size a broken tip would be, and published a paper on it in 1987.

According to the professor, even though the pencil had been in use for hundreds of years, it seems the first documented analysis of how its tip breaks came in 1979. It was so familiar that nobody had seriously looked into it.

This research led the professor to dig into the history of the pencil, which he turned into a book.
