### "Mackerel clouds" is a nickname, not an official cloud name

The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas divides clouds into 10 genera based on their height and shape. "Mackerel clouds" isn't among them. Like "sardine clouds" and "scale clouds" (other Japanese nicknames), it's an everyday name.

In the video, we introduced it as "another name for cirrocumulus." Mackerel clouds usually do mean cirrocumulus, but a very similar pattern can also form in altocumulus, a lower mid-level cloud. The mackerel clouds you spot in the sky might actually be altocumulus, so for tips on telling them apart, see [our article on scale clouds](/trivia/E8Z-W7SqgKA/).

### "Mackerel" in English, too

According to the UK's Met Office, cirrocumulus can look like fish scales and is called a "mackerel sky" in English. Mackerel is the fish known as saba in Japanese. It's fun that Japan and the English-speaking world named the same cloud after the same fish.

The Met Office explains that the small cloudlets of cirrocumulus can line up like ripples or look like a honeycomb. The "stripes" of a mackerel sky are this wave-like arrangement.

### 5,000–13,000 meters is a guideline for the mid-latitudes

The video's "roughly 5,000 to 13,000 meters" matches the height the WMO gives for high clouds in the mid-latitudes (the temperate zone, which includes Japan). However, the WMO describes this as an "approximate" height that varies with latitude: about 3–8 km in the cold polar regions and about 6–18 km in the tropics.

The Met Office gives the cloud base of cirrocumulus as 20,000–40,000 feet (about 6–12 km). These clouds form far above the height of Mount Fuji (3,776 meters).

### The regular pattern comes from air waves and up-and-down currents

According to the Met Office, cirrocumulus forms when turbulent up-and-down air currents hit a layer of cirrus. That's what the video means by "up-and-down currents."

As for the other part, "waves in the air," the Met Office uses the example of air waves that form downwind of mountains. When air rises within a wave, it cools and water vapor turns into droplets, forming cloud. When the air sinks, it warms and the droplets evaporate. This is the same mechanism as the video's "where a wave lifts the air, it cools and clouds form; where the air sinks, the clouds disappear."

Because the crests and troughs of a wave repeat at regular intervals, the spots where clouds form and the gaps between them also line up at regular intervals. That's why a mackerel sky looks so orderly.

### It's a sign of worsening weather when it "spreads and thickens"

Harerun Land, the Japan Meteorological Agency's website for kids, says that when cirrostratus or cirrocumulus "spreads out and gradually thickens," the weather often takes a turn for the worse. The video's wording, "when it spreads and thickens," fits this explanation.

On the other hand, the Met Office writes that cirrocumulus is usually associated with fair weather, though it can also be a sign of stormy weather ahead. Simply seeing a mackerel sky doesn't mean the weather will turn bad.

The trick is to watch how the sky changes afterward. According to the Met Office, cirrus often forms ahead of a warm front, and cirrostratus forms at the very leading edge of frontal weather. If, after a mackerel sky, the sky becomes widely covered in thin cloud (cirrostratus) that gradually thickens, a low-pressure system or front may be approaching.
