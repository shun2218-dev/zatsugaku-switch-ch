### The rules say only "clear and obvious errors" count

VAR has seven principles, and this is number three:

> 3. The original decision given by the referee will not be changed unless the video review clearly shows that the decision was a 'clear and obvious error'.

In other words, the original decision is not changed unless the video review clearly shows it was a **clear and obvious error**.

This is the very design of the system. VAR is not "a system for making the correct call from scratch." It's "a system for fixing things only when they're clearly wrong."

That means borderline situations, where different people might see things differently, are **not subject to correction**. Since the original call can't be described as a clear and obvious error, it stands. That's what's really behind the complaint, "We have VAR, so why didn't the call change?"

The text drives this point home again:

> The referee`s original decision will not be changed unless there was a 'clear and obvious error' (this includes any decision made by the referee based on information from another match official e.g. offside).

### Subjective calls are hard to label as "errors" in the first place

The types of decisions that can be reviewed are also limited.

> The categories of decision/incident which may be reviewed in the event of a potential 'clear and obvious error' or 'serious missed incident' are:

They're narrowed down to the kinds of decisions that directly affect the result: goals, penalty kicks, red cards (sending-offs), and mistaken identity. Cautions (yellow cards) are not included.

And the procedure changes depending on the nature of the decision.

> For subjective decisions, e.g. intensity of a foul challenge, interference at offside, handball considerations, an 'on-field review' (OFR) is appropriate

The intensity of a foul, interference in an offside situation, handball judgments: these are explicitly called "subjective decisions." That's why an "on-field review," where the referee looks at a monitor beside the pitch with their own eyes, is considered appropriate.

By calling them subjective, the rules themselves admit that the answer can vary depending on who's looking. This is the fundamental reason refereeing mistakes never drop to zero.

### In the end, a human still decides

Let's look at the remaining principles.

> 2. The referee must always make a decision, i.e. the referee is not permitted to give 'no decision' and then use the VAR to make the decision; a decision to allow play to continue after an alleged offence can be reviewed.

The referee must always make a decision themselves. They can't "hold off and let VAR decide."

> 4. Only the referee can initiate a 'review'; the VAR (and other match officials) can only recommend a 'review' to the referee.

Only the referee can start a review. VAR can only recommend one.

> 5. The final decision is always taken by the referee, either based on information from the VAR or after the referee has undertaken an 'on-field review' (OFR).

The final decision is always made by the referee.

VAR's role is spelled out clearly too:

> The referee is the only person who can make the final decision; the VAR has the same status as the other match officials and can only assist the referee

VAR has the same status as the other match officials and can only assist the referee.

It's not a machine making the calls. A person watches the footage, a person gives advice, and a person decides. It's a system with three layers of human judgment.

### No time limit: accuracy comes first

Some principles may come as a surprise.

> 6. There is no time limit for the review process as accuracy is more important than speed.

There's no time limit on reviews, because accuracy matters more than speed.

This principle is why long stoppages happen. The rules acknowledge that the tempo of the game and the accuracy of decisions can't both be fully satisfied, and they choose accuracy.

### Most checks end without anyone noticing

In fact, VAR is working throughout the entire match.

> If the 'check' does not indicate a 'clear and obvious error' or 'serious missed incident', there is usually no need for the VAR to communicate with the referee – this is a 'silent check'

If no clear and obvious error is found, VAR doesn't need to contact the referee. This is called a "silent check."

> however, it sometimes helps the referee/assistant referee to manage the players/match if the VAR confirms that no 'clear and obvious error' or 'serious missed incident' occurred

That said, sometimes it's easier for the referee to manage the match if VAR confirms there was no problem.

> If the 'check' indicates a probable 'clear and obvious error' or 'serious missed incident', the VAR will communicate this information to the referee, who will then decide whether or not to initiate a 'review'

The reviews you actually see are only a tiny fraction of countless silent checks.

### Players are forbidden from surrounding the referee

The last principle covers players and team officials.

> 7. The players and team officials must not surround the referee or attempt to influence if a decision is reviewed, the review process or the final decision.

They must not surround the referee or try to influence whether a decision is reviewed, the review process, or the final decision.

When VAR was introduced, it created an expectation that "if we protest, maybe they'll review it." This rule exists to shut that down.

### Once play restarts, it's almost too late to fix

There's a timing constraint as well.

> If play has stopped and restarted, the referee may only undertake a 'review', and take the appropriate disciplinary sanction, for mistaken identity or for a potential sending-off offence relating to violent conduct, spitting, biting or extremely offensive, insulting and/or abusive action(s).

Once play has stopped and restarted, a review is only possible for mistaken identity or for potential red-card offenses involving violent conduct, spitting, biting, or extremely offensive, insulting, and/or abusive actions.

In other words, a missed foul or offside can no longer be corrected once play has resumed.

### Introduced in 2016, starting in Japan

According to the Japan Football Association's Q&A:

> VAR was first used at a FIFA (Fédération Internationale de Football Association) tournament at the 2016 FIFA Club World Cup, held in Japan, and was first used at a FIFA World Cup at the 2018 tournament in Russia.

It's a system that's only been around for about 10 years. And judging from how the principles are written, "eliminating refereeing mistakes" was never the goal from the start. The goal is to "prevent clear and obvious errors from changing the outcome of a match." Knowing where that line is drawn can help close the gap between what people expect from VAR and what it's actually meant to do.
