### It all comes down to one number: the dew point

In the video, we said that "the cooled air can't hold all its water vapor anymore." That "can't hold it anymore" temperature has a name. The Japan Meteorological Agency's forecast terminology defines it like this:

> **Dew point temperature**: The temperature at which relative humidity reaches 100% when air is cooled while its water vapor pressure stays constant. The temperature at which water vapor in the air condenses into dew.

Whether a glass gets wet is decided by comparing it to this single number. **If the glass's surface temperature is lower than the dew point of the surrounding air, it gets wet.** If it's higher, it doesn't.

### Look at the dew point, not the humidity

Here's the practical takeaway. "High humidity makes things wet more easily" is roughly true, but humidity is a number whose meaning changes with the air temperature.

As the Japan Meteorological Agency defines it, relative humidity is "the ratio of the amount of water vapor to the saturation amount of water vapor at the current temperature." Even at the same 60% humidity, air at 30°C (86°F) and air at 10°C (50°F) contain completely different amounts of water vapor.

The dew point converts that "amount of water vapor actually in the air" into a temperature, so it isn't swayed by the air temperature. That's why the dew point is better for predicting condensation.

Glasses get soaking wet so fast in summer because the dew point is high (the air is packed with water vapor). Set out the same glass of ice water in winter, and it won't get very wet, because the dew point is low.

### That's why lukewarm drinks stay dry

The same idea ties together a bunch of everyday observations:

- **A glass of ice water gets wet**: Its surface drops close to 0°C (32°F), lower than most dew points
- **Room-temperature drinks stay dry**: Their surface temperature stays above the dew point
- **Coasters help**: Not by preventing the wetness, but by catching the water that drips down
- **Pouring into a pre-chilled glass makes it extra wet**: The surface temperature has already been lowered

The misconception that "the water inside is seeping out" is easy to fall into because the amount of water is surprisingly large. Water vapor in the air is invisible, but whenever there's a surface below the dew point, it collects there and becomes visible.

### Below 0°C, you get frost instead of dew

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency's "Guide to Meteorological Observations," below 0°C the temperature at which air becomes saturated with respect to ice is called the **frost point**, because frost forms instead of dew.

That's why things you take out of the freezer get coated in white frost rather than water droplets. It's the same principle, but past that temperature threshold, both the name and the appearance change.
