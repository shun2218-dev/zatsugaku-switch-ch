### The ring is a "glory"; the shadow is the "Brocken"

In the video, we called the rainbow-colored ring around the shadow the "Brocken phenomenon." That name is common in Japan, but the World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas describes these as two separate things.

According to the WMO, the colored rings you see around your own shadow are a **glory**. Meanwhile, when your shadow is cast onto fog or clouds and looks hugely distorted because of perspective, it's called a "Brocken spectre." That term refers to the shadow itself, whether or not any colored rings appear.

The Japanese encyclopedia Nipponica says a glory is also called **goko** ("halo of light") and that it's most often seen from mountaintops. It also notes that on the Brocken, a mountain in Germany, your shadow can appear huge in the fog. In other words, the "giant shadow" and the "rainbow ring" are separate phenomena that often show up together.

### The ring is always exactly opposite the sun

There's a clear rule about where a glory appears.

According to the WMO, a glory is always centered on the point directly opposite the sun. This point is called the **antisolar point**. It's where the shadow of your head falls. Because the sun, you, and your shadow line up in a straight line, this point is below the horizon during the day while the sun is up in the sky.

That's why glories are seen when you look down on fog or clouds from somewhere high up, like a mountain or an airplane. The WMO notes that airplane passengers may see a glory around the shadow of the plane they're riding in.

According to NASA, passengers sitting on the side away from the sun can sometimes see the plane's shadow on the clouds below. Before air travel became common, glories were mostly seen by mountain climbers, and they're also called the "pilot's glory."

### Water droplets that send light back "where it came from"

Glories are the work of the tiny water droplets that make up clouds and fog.

NASA explains that a glory forms when water droplets scatter sunlight back toward its source. The Britannica International Encyclopedia (Japanese edition) likewise describes it as a **backscattering** phenomenon involving sunlight and water droplets such as fog particles. Backscattering means light scattering back toward the direction it came from, as if bouncing off.

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, the droplets that create glories are round particles with a radius of roughly 4 to 25 micrometers. One micrometer is one-thousandth of a millimeter. They're far too small to make out with the naked eye.

### The mechanism is much trickier than a rainbow's

In the video, we said light comes back by "traveling around the edges" of the droplets. That's close to what researchers think, but the question isn't fully settled yet.

The Hong Kong Observatory writes that, unlike rainbows, glories can't be explained by treating light as straight-line rays (geometric optics). The leading explanation combines **surface waves**, where light creeps along the surface of the droplet, with **diffraction** and **interference**, which come from light's nature as a wave. Diffraction is light bending around the edges of objects, and interference is waves overlapping to strengthen or cancel each other. Nipponica also says the mechanism of glories is "not yet clear."

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, rainbows, on the other hand, can be explained by treating light as straight-line rays. Glories involve much smaller droplets, and they're a phenomenon where light's wave nature shows up clearly.

### Red outside, blue inside, and smaller droplets mean bigger rings

The colors of a glory come in a set order. According to NASA, the outside is red, turning blue toward the center. The Hong Kong Observatory says the colors go red, orange, yellow, green, blue, and violet from outside to inside. Nipponica notes that this color order is the same as that of a **corona**, which forms right around the sun.

The size of the ring depends on the size of the droplets. According to the Hong Kong Observatory, the apparent size of the ring is roughly inversely proportional to the droplet size. As droplets get bigger, the ring gets smaller and less noticeable. With droplets 10 micrometers in radius, the innermost red ring appears about 2.4 degrees from the center of the shadow.

As the video said, uniform droplet size also matters. The Hong Kong Observatory explains that when droplet sizes are well matched, the glory gets brighter, has more rings, and shows clearer colors. Nipponica says the rings can sometimes be doubled, and in some cases there can be as many as five.

### Your glory is yours alone

Glories have one more interesting property.

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, even if several people climb a mountain together, each person sees only one glory, around the shadow of their own head. Nipponica also says a glory is visible only to you and can't be seen by the person standing next to you. That's because the center of the glory is the antisolar point for each viewer.

If you spot a glory from an airplane, what's at the center of the ring is the shadow of the plane you're on. Passengers on another plane looking at the same clouds would see a glory around their own plane's shadow. Try looking for one from a window on the side away from the sun when there are clouds spread out below.
