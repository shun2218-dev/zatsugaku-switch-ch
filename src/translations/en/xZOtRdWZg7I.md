### Break it again and again, and both poles still come along

If you break a bar magnet in the middle, it seems like you'd end up with a north half and a south half. But that's not what happens.

Physics lab materials from the University of Colorado Boulder explain that breaking a magnet always produces smaller magnets, and every piece keeps both a north and a south pole. A physics textbook from Michigan State University also says that no matter how finely you cut a magnet, you can't isolate a single pole; each new piece becomes a smaller magnet with its own north and south poles.

At the break, a pole opposite to the original one appears. The broken end of the north-side piece becomes a south pole, and the broken end of the south-side piece becomes a north pole. That's why, if you bring the pieces back together at the break, they try to stick to each other again.

### A magnet is a collection of tiny magnets

So why does a new pole appear every time you break it?

According to a physics textbook from the University of Central Florida, materials that can become magnets, like iron, are divided into regions called **magnetic domains**. A magnetic domain is a small cluster in which the atoms all point in the same direction. Each domain behaves like a tiny bar magnet, and each atom within it also behaves like a tiny bar magnet.

In other words, a magnet is "lots of tiny magnets lined up facing the same way." Wherever you break it, each piece still contains tiny magnets lined up in the same direction. The north poles of those tiny magnets show up at one end of the piece, and the south poles at the other. That's why a new pole appears at the broken end.

### Atoms are magnets thanks to electrons

Then why does an atom act like a tiny magnet?

The Michigan State University textbook explains that atoms behave like tiny magnets because of how the electrons inside them move, and because of a built-in property of electrons called **spin**. Spin is a property that makes an electron act as if it were spinning like a top, and it works like a tiny magnet on its own.

The University of Central Florida textbook also says that if you roughly picture an electron's spin as "spinning electric charge," it creates a magnetic field with a north and a south pole. However, it also notes that this spinning-top picture is just an analogy and doesn't fully match modern physics.

The electron itself is also a tiny magnet with a north and south pole as a set. Even at this small a scale, the poles don't separate.

### When iron becomes a magnet, and when it doesn't

Some iron is magnetized and some isn't. The difference lies in the direction of the magnetic domains.

According to the University of Central Florida textbook, in unmagnetized iron, the small domains point in random directions. Because they're scattered every which way, their magnetic forces cancel out overall. When you apply an outside magnetic force (a magnetic field), the domains line up, and some domains grow larger by absorbing others. The textbook says these grown domains can reach about a millimeter in size.

Magnets are also sensitive to heat. As temperature rises and the atoms jiggle more, the domains scatter into random directions again. According to the textbook, above a certain temperature a material can no longer be magnetized, and that temperature is called the **Curie temperature**. The Curie temperature of iron is 1,043 kelvins, or about 770°C (about 1,420°F).

### A north-only magnet hasn't been found yet

In the video, we said "you'll never get a lone pole." By breaking a magnet, that's certainly true. But to be more precise, the current position of science is that a particle with "only a north pole" or "only a south pole" **hasn't been found yet**.

A particle with only a north pole or only a south pole is called a **magnetic monopole**. The Michigan State University textbook says magnetic monopoles have never been observed in an experiment. The University of Central Florida textbook explains that there's no known reason why magnetic monopoles can't exist; they just haven't been found, so the search continues in the world far smaller than atoms.

Some experiments are actually hunting for them. At CERN (the European Organization for Nuclear Research), the Large Hadron Collider (LHC) has a detector called MoEDAL. According to CERN, MoEDAL is designed to directly search for magnetic monopoles, hypothetical particles that carry only a north pole or only a south pole.

### Electric charges can be separated, but magnetic poles can't

Magnetism has a lot in common with electricity. According to the Michigan State University textbook, like poles repel and opposite poles attract. That's just like the relationship between positive and negative electric charges.

The difference is whether they can be separated. Positive and negative charges can be pulled apart from each other. But according to the textbook, magnetic poles, unlike electric charges, can't be separated.

Break an everyday magnet and you won't get a north-only magnet. Even so, if a magnetic monopole were ever found, the story would change. The University of Central Florida textbook says that right now we think "all magnetism comes from the flow of electric charge," but if magnetic monopoles exist, that idea would have to be revised. Those broken pieces of a magnet connect to a question physics still hasn't answered.
