### The pupil is the eye's "aperture"

The **pupil** is the black part in the center of the eye. It's the opening that lets light in, much like the aperture of a camera.

In the dark, it opens wide to let in lots of light. In bright places, it closes down to cut the glare.

A human pupil stays round as it gets bigger or smaller. A cat's pupil opens into a full circle in the dark and narrows to a thin vertical line in bright light.

### A slit can open and close over a wider range

According to a 2015 paper, a round pupil closes using a ring-shaped muscle. A slit pupil has that plus two more muscles that squeeze the opening from either side. That lets it change its area much more.

Here are the figures the paper gives:

- Humans (round pupils): area changes about 15-fold
- Domestic cats (vertical pupils): about 135-fold
- Geckos (vertical pupils): about 300-fold

Animals that are active both day and night need pupils that open wide enough in the dark and close enough to avoid being dazzled by daylight. A slit pupil can cover that wide range. The 15-fold and 135-fold figures mentioned in the video come from this paper.

Together with the mechanism that [makes cats' and dogs' eyes glow in the dark](/trivia/5PQMhtb_khY/), a cat's eyes are built to handle darkness well.

### But why "vertical"?

That leaves one question. If all you need is a wide opening range, a horizontal or even diagonal slit should work just as well.

Professor Banks of the University of California, Berkeley, who led the research, put it this way in the university's announcement: previous explanations couldn't account for why slit pupils are either vertical or horizontal.

So the team looked at 214 species of land animals, comparing the shape of their pupils with how they live.

### Shape was linked to lifestyle

The results were clear:

- Vertical pupils: common in animals that hunt by ambush and are active both day and night
- Horizontal pupils: common in plant-eating prey animals with eyes on the sides of their heads
- Round pupils: common in animals that chase down their prey

An article in the Institute of Physics' magazine lists some foxes, cats, and snakes as examples with vertical pupils. Deer and zebras have horizontal ones, and cheetahs and coyotes have round ones.

The team checked horizontal pupils using computer models. A horizontally stretched pupil gives a wide view along the ground — ahead, behind, and to the sides. That's well suited to spotting approaching predators and seeing where to run.

The team also observed goats, sheep, and horses at zoos and farms. When the animals lowered their heads to graze, their eyes rotated so that the pupils stayed parallel to the ground.

### Vertical slits help judge distance

Ambush predators need to judge exactly how far they have to leap to reach their prey.

The team focused on two cues. One is the slight difference between what the left and right eyes see (**binocular disparity**). The other is the **blur** in areas that are out of focus.

According to the paper, a vertical pupil keeps vertical lines sharp, making it easier to pick up the difference between the two eyes. At the same time, horizontal lines in front of and behind the point of focus blur more easily, making it easier to judge distance from that blur. The team's idea is that vertical pupils let animals make use of both cues.

### Tigers and lions have round pupils

That said, not every ambush predator has vertical pupils.

According to the paper, of 65 ambush predator species with forward-facing eyes, 44 had vertical pupils and 19 had round ones. Of the vertical-pupil animals, 82% stood less than 42 cm (about 16.5 inches) tall at the shoulder. Among the round-pupil animals, only 17% were shorter than 42 cm.

Domestic cats have vertical pupils, but their big relatives in the cat family, tigers and lions, have round ones. The team uses calculations to explain why: using blur to judge distance works better the lower an animal's eyes are to the ground.

### Still at the "it's thought that" stage

Not every researcher is convinced by this explanation.

In the Institute of Physics magazine article, Kröger of Lund University in Sweden, who studies animal eyes, says there are countless exceptions. In his view, tigers are much bigger than cats and should need to judge long distances accurately, so it's unclear why they get by with round pupils.

The paper itself notes that it only studied land animals. Animals that live in water, in the air, or in trees are a subject for future research.

There's clearly a strong link between pupil shape and lifestyle. Exactly why is still being studied.
