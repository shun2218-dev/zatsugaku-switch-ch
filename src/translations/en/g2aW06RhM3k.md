### Same grass, different brightness depending on which way it leans

The stripes don't come from different types or colors of grass. Mowers used on match pitches have a roller that presses down the freshly cut grass, so the blades lean in the direction the mower traveled. When the mower goes up one row and comes back down the next, the direction the grass leans alternates from row to row, creating stripes.

According to turfgrass researchers, **grass bent away from the viewer looks lighter, while grass bent toward the viewer looks darker**. When the grass leans away from you, the long, flat surfaces of the blades face you and reflect plenty of light. When it leans toward you, you mostly see the tips of the blades, so there's less surface to reflect light.

### Light and dark rows swap depending on where you stand

The video said that grass "looks bright when bent one way and dark when bent the other way," but what actually determines brightness isn't the direction itself. It's **the direction relative to the viewer**. A row that looks light from one side will be leaning toward you if you view it from the opposite side of the pitch, so it looks dark. That's why the light and dark stripes can switch places when you change seats in a stadium or when the broadcast cuts to a different camera.

### The rules care about "lines," not "mowing"

The Laws of the Game (IFAB Law 1) state that the field of play must be natural grass, though artificial turf or a combination of both (hybrid) may be used if the competition allows it, that artificial surfaces must be green, and that **no markings other than those specified in the Laws may appear on the field**. However, there's no rule about how the grass is mowed or about stripe patterns.

The shape of the stripes is set by each competition's own regulations. For example, England's Premier League requires grass to be kept at 27 mm or shorter, and mowed in straight, parallel stripes running perpendicular to the touchlines across the full width of the pitch. Circles and diagonal patterns aren't allowed.

### About the "easier to follow the ball" and "offside guide" claims

You often hear that the stripes "make it easier to follow the ball" or "help referees judge offside." The video also mentioned this, framed as something that "is said to" be true. However, neither the Laws of the Game nor competition regulations say the stripes are used for these purposes, and we couldn't find any material that confirms such an effect.

The offside law (Law 11) says offside is judged by comparing the position of a player's head, body and feet to the second-to-last opponent or the ball, and the grass stripes aren't part of that standard. Besides, since the light and dark stripes change depending on where you look from, they wouldn't work well as a measuring guide anyway. It's probably best to think of the stripes first and foremost as a visual touch that comes from caring for the grass.
