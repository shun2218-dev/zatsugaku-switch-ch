### "Unstable" really means a two-story temperature gap

The Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) classifies thunderheads as cumulonimbus clouds. It states plainly that "the thunderheads we often see in summer are also cumulonimbus clouds," and defines them this way: "Cumulonimbus clouds are clouds that have developed markedly in the vertical direction due to strong updrafts."

The condition for their development is that "the atmosphere is unstable." You hear this phrase on the news all the time, but the JMA's definition is quite specific:

> "The atmosphere is unstable" refers to a state in which there is cold air aloft and a layer of warmed air near the ground.

In other words, it's a **vertical temperature difference**: cold on top, warm below. When the video says "in summer, the ground gets very hot, and when the air high above is cold," it's describing exactly this definition. Warm air tends to rise and cold air tends to sink, so convection kicks in easily.

### The other condition: moisture

A temperature difference alone isn't enough. The JMA adds:

> When the air near the ground is humid, the atmosphere becomes even more unstable, and cumulonimbus clouds develop more easily.

When water vapor condenses into water droplets, it releases heat, so humid air ends up warming itself as it rises. Thunderheads grow so readily in Japanese summers not just because of the heat, but because of the humidity.

### Winter cumulonimbus clouds don't grow even half as tall

As for height, the JMA says that "the cloud tops can exceed 10 kilometers, and sometimes even reach the stratosphere." That's what the video means by "nearly 10,000 meters."

What's interesting is that the very same type of cloud reaches completely different heights depending on the season. A JMA page explaining the climate of the Hokuriku region (on Japan's Sea of Japan coast) says:

> While summer cumulonimbus clouds develop up to the tropopause at heights of 10,000 meters or more, winter cumulonimbus clouds have a layer aloft where temperature does not drop much with height (a stable layer). As a result, they are low, ranging from 3,000 meters to 6,000 meters at most, and lightning also occurs at lower altitudes than in summer cumulonimbus clouds.

When there's a "lid" overhead, growth stops there. Thunderheads grow so tall because the summer sky has no lid, leaving them room to stretch all the way up to the tropopause.
