### A cloud's speed is the wind speed at its height

Clouds don't move on their own. They're carried by whatever wind is blowing at their height. So when we say "high clouds are fast," what we really mean is "the wind is strong up high."

Let's look at how high clouds actually are, using the World Meteorological Organization's (WMO) International Cloud Atlas. In the mid-latitudes, which include Japan, low clouds form from the ground up to 2 km, middle clouds at roughly 2–7 km, and high clouds at roughly 5–13 km. So the highest clouds sit several times higher than the low ones.

### Near the ground, friction acts like a brake

According to the U.S. National Weather Service (NWS), wind near the ground is slowed as it moves over the surface, making it weaker than it would otherwise be. Friction with hills, mountains, valleys, trees, buildings, and so on also creates eddies in the air. The video's line, "Wind near the ground gets slowed down by friction with buildings, trees, and the ground itself," is exactly right.

The NWS says the effects of friction usually don't reach much higher than about 2,000 feet (around 600 meters) above the ground. Above that, the wind blows harder than it does at the surface.

The NWS also explains that surface winds tend to pick up during the day because the ground heats strongly and stirs the air, bringing the momentum of faster winds aloft down to the surface. At night, once the ground stops being heated, surface winds die down again.

### Even higher, temperature differences make the wind stronger

When the video says "even higher up, temperature differences come into play too," it's talking about the **jet stream**. The jet stream is a band of especially strong wind flowing from west to east high in the atmosphere.

According to the satellite service of NOAA (the National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration), known as NESDIS, the jet stream sits 5–9 miles (about 8–14 km) above the ground and forms where masses of warm air and cold air meet. It averages about 110 mph (about 180 km/h), and when the temperature contrast is large it can exceed 250 mph (about 400 km/h); speeds like these are usually seen in winter.

The Japan Meteorological Agency's educational site "Harerun Land" also explains that around 10,000 meters up, a strong westerly wind of roughly 100 meters per second blows almost all year round. This wind pushes high- and low-pressure systems from west to east, which is why the weather moves from west to east too.

### High clouds sit right in the path of strong winds

The height where high clouds form (roughly 5–13 km) overlaps with the height where the jet stream flows (about 8–14 km). That's why high clouds are often riding strong winds and moving fast.

That said, the jet stream isn't always flowing right overhead. Its location and strength change with the season and from day to day. According to NESDIS, it's fastest in winter. You can think of that as the reason the video says high clouds are "often" moving fast.

### When you look up, distant clouds seem slower

The video ends by suggesting you notice "how cloud height and speed differ," but there's one thing to keep in mind when you do.

Just as a distant train seems to crawl along, clouds high in the sky can look like they're moving slowly from the ground even when they're actually fast. On some days, low clouds may be zipping by while the high clouds above them look almost still. How fast something looks and how fast it really is are two different things.

If you want to check how high clouds are moving, pick something that doesn't move—like a utility pole or the corner of a building—as a reference point, and watch for a while.
