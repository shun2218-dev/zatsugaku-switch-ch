### What blurs the view: "particles" in the air

Distant mountains look hazy because the air between you and the mountains gets in the way of the light.

According to the U.S. National Park Service (NPS), haze happens when sunlight hits tiny particles in the air. The particles scatter light out of the path to your eyes, scatter light into that path from the side, and absorb some of it. That's why the farther away the scenery is, the more it blurs, as if covered by a whitish veil.

The Japan Meteorological Agency also explains that whether the air looks murky or clear depends on how much water vapor, dust, and the like it contains.

The video named "less water vapor" as one of the causes. But the main direct culprit behind the haze isn't the water vapor gas itself — it's the particles floating in the air. Water vapor plays the role of "growing" those particles.

### In humid air, particles absorb water and scatter more light

According to the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency (EPA), when humidity is high, some particles in the air scatter light more efficiently, which reduces visibility. In particular, **ammonium sulfate** and **ammonium nitrate** (both water-attracting particles formed from things like air pollution) are said to take up water vapor from the air when humidity is high.

The EPA says this humidity effect is "especially strong in summer." It also offers a comparison: in the eastern United States, where average annual relative humidity is 70–80%, humidity is a major cause of reduced visibility, while in the West, at 50–60%, it isn't as much of a factor.

The video's explanation — "in summer, the humidity is high, so fine dust absorbs moisture, swells up, and scatters light" — refers to this mechanism.

### In winter, there's less water vapor and less dust

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, winter is colder than summer, and **convection** (the up-and-down exchange of air, as air warmed by the ground rises) is weaker. As a result, there's less water vapor and dust in the air, and the sky looks clear.

Cold air simply can't hold much water vapor to begin with. If there's little water vapor for the particles to take up, they don't swell, and they don't scatter much light.

### Dry seasonal winds sweeping down on the Pacific side

In Japan, winter on the Pacific side is a season of especially good visibility.

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency, in winter, dry and extremely cold northwesterly winds start blowing from Siberia and mainland China. As these winds cross the Sea of Japan, they pick up water vapor and drop snow on the regions along the Sea of Japan side. After dropping that snow, the air crosses the mountains and sweeps down the other side, so the Pacific side gets almost no snow and instead has clear skies and dry air.

In other words, the crisp winter days when you can see distant mountains are mainly a Pacific-side story. In the same season, things are different on the Sea of Japan side, where snow clouds tend to hang around. The Japan Meteorological Agency also notes that when a low-pressure system passes over the ocean south of Honshu (Japan's main island), it can snow even on the Pacific side.

### Even in winter, some mornings are hazy

It's not as if you can see far away anytime in winter.

According to the NPS, trailing plumes of smoke and layers of haze lying across the sky are common in the cold winter months, when the air doesn't move much. The flat layer of gray haze you sometimes see lying over a city on a calm winter morning is one example of this.

Distant mountains look sharpest on days when dry seasonal winds are blowing and the air has little water vapor and few particles. If you head up somewhere high on a clear winter morning, try looking for mountains you can't usually see.
