### Ice gives off water vapor, too

It sounds counterintuitive, but water molecules escape from the surface of ice, too.

The same thing that happens when water evaporates also happens with solid ice. Among the water molecules on the surface of ice, the ones that happen to have a lot of energy fly off directly as a gas, without passing through the liquid stage. This is called **sublimation**.

The amount is much smaller than with liquid evaporation, but it isn't zero. That's why ice gradually shrinks even below freezing.

Ice that's been in the freezer a long time getting smaller is the same phenomenon. It hasn't melted, yet its volume goes down. It's leaving as water vapor.

### Drying takes not just "releasing" but "carrying away"

This is the important part. Even if water vapor comes off, laundry won't dry if that vapor just lingers around it.

Once the air is saturated with water vapor, the amount leaving and the amount coming back balance out, and the laundry effectively stops drying. For drying to continue, the vapor that's produced has to be constantly carried away.

That's the wind's job. In the Japan Meteorological Agency's forecasting terminology, wind is defined as the movement of air. The reason [laundry dries faster on windy days](/trivia/4KEmTOiWH_M/) is exactly this "carrying away" effect.

On clear winter days, the temperature is low, but the air is dry. The drier the air, the more room it has to take in water vapor. That's how "it's cold, but things still dry" can happen.

### Staying stiff is the proof of sublimation

Here's the thing to watch for.

If the ice first melted into water and that water then evaporated, the laundry would pass through a **wet stage** along the way. It would go from stiff to limp, and then dry.

But when you actually bring in frozen laundry, it has gotten lighter while staying stiff. It never went through a wet stage. That means the ice shrank without turning into water.

Ice needs to be at 0°C (32°F) or above to turn into water. On a day that stays below freezing all day, that stage never comes.

### The same thing as freeze-drying

Freeze-drying puts this principle to industrial use.

Food is frozen first, then placed in a vacuum so the ice sublimates. Because it never passes through the liquid stage, the cell structure doesn't collapse, and the shape and flavor are preserved. The toppings in instant miso soup and freeze-dried coffee are made this way.

The vacuum is there to speed up sublimation. The lower the surrounding air pressure, the more easily the water vapor coming off the ice can escape.

In cold regions, there are also traditional preserved foods that use this principle: *shimi-dofu* (also called *koya-dofu*, a freeze-dried tofu), agar (kanten), and *shimi-daikon* (freeze-dried daikon radish). Methods like freezing by day and drying by night, or exposing food to cold winds so it dries while frozen, are natural freeze-drying.

### That said, it isn't efficient

To be fair, drying by sublimation takes time.

With air that's equally dry, moisture leaves faster at higher temperatures. Sublimation below freezing is a slow process compared with the evaporation of water.

So for frozen laundry to dry, you need conditions like clear skies, wind, and about a full day. It's true that laundry "dries even below freezing," but it doesn't "dry faster below freezing."

You're right to feel that laundry dries slowly in winter, and the reason it still dries if you give it several days is sublimation.

### Freezing puts strain on fabric

One practical caution: water expands when it freezes. When the water held in fibers freezes, it pushes the fibers apart from the inside.

Freezing delicate clothes isn't gentle on the fibers. If you force frozen items to bend or beat them, the fibers get damaged.

If you hang laundry outside on a cold day, it's safest not to force it into folds when you bring it in, and to wait until it warms back up indoors before handling it. That stiffness is a fun texture, but at that moment, ice is expanding inside the fibers.
