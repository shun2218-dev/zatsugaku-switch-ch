### The first line of the Law is the basis for everything

Here is the opening of Law 5 of the Laws of the Game:

> Each match is controlled by a referee who has full authority to enforce the Laws of the Game in connection with the match.

In other words, a match is controlled by a single referee, who has **full authority** to enforce the Laws of the Game.

Notice the words "full authority." When the referee blows the whistle and play stops, it isn't because the sound itself has any power. Play stops because the referee has decided to stop it. The whistle is simply the way that decision is announced to everyone.

### Decisions are written down as "final"

The next passage goes even further:

> The decisions of the referee regarding facts connected with play, including whether or not a goal is scored and the result of the match, are final. The decisions of the referee, and all other match officials, must always be respected.

The referee's decisions on facts connected with play are **final, including whether a goal was scored and the result of the match**. And the decisions of the referee and the other match officials must always be respected.

"Final." There's no system for appeals. Even if replays later show a call was clearly wrong, a decision made during the match isn't overturned. (Corrections through VAR are only possible during the match, before play restarts.)

This rule is what keeps the game moving. If there were a system that let teams halt play to challenge calls, soccer would never finish in 90 minutes.

### The whistle is blown "only when needed"

The Laws of the Game include a section called "Body language, communication and whistle" that lays out how the whistle should be used.

The key point is that **there are times when the whistle is not blown**. For restarts that are obvious to everyone, such as throw-ins, goal kicks and corner kicks, the referee doesn't whistle. Blowing it too often would dilute its power to grab attention.

The whistle is needed:

- To stop play (for a foul, an injury, a substitution and so on)
- To start or restart the match (kick-offs, penalty kicks, free kicks after the wall has been moved back, and so on)
- To end the match or each half

In fact, play is more often restarted with just a hand signal showing the direction, with no whistle at all. That's why you hear the whistle surprisingly rarely during a match: it's intentional by design.

### Play can even stop because of a fan's whistle

Here's an interesting provision:

> a spectator blows a whistle which interferes with play - play is stopped and restarted with a dropped ball

If a spectator blows a whistle and it interferes with play, play is stopped and restarted with a dropped ball.

Paradoxically, this shows that the "sound" of a whistle has no authority in itself. Players may stop even at a spectator's whistle. But since that wasn't the referee's decision, the stoppage is treated as invalid, and play is redone with a dropped ball.

> A player is carelessly fouled in the middle of the field. Their team retains possession of the ball and continues a promising attack, so the referee signals advantage but the attacking team players stop because someone in the crowd, not the referee or anyone on the team lists, has blown a whistle.

This is a case where the referee signaled advantage, but the players stopped because of a whistle from the crowd. It makes clear that the sound and the authority are two separate things.

### The equipment is set

A Q&A from the Japan Football Association lists what match officials carry:

> The referee carries a red card, a yellow card, a whistle, a coin, a watch, a pen and a record card. Assistant referees carry a flag, a watch, a whistle, a pen and a record card. In some competitions, the referee may also use a spray (vanishing spray) to mark positions for free kicks.

The whistle is just one of seven items. The cards, the coin, the watch: all of them are "tools for exercising authority in a visible way."

By the way, Japan has its own special card for youth soccer:

> The Japan Football Association (JFA) uses a green card in U-12 competitions. It was introduced to promote fair play and respect, and is shown to players who play or behave fairly and with great respect.

It's a card for praise, not punishment.

### The referee's authority runs from before the match to after it

The Laws also spell out when that authority begins and ends:

> has the authority to take disciplinary action from entering the field of play for the pre-match inspection until leaving the field of play after the match ends (including penalties (penalty shoot-out))

That's from the moment the referee steps onto the field for the pre-match inspection until leaving it after the match, including penalty shoot-outs.

> has the power to show yellow or red cards and, where competition rules permit, temporarily dismiss a player, from entering the field of play at the start of the match until after the match has ended, including during the half-time interval, extra time and penalties (penalty shoot-out)

This includes halftime, too. That's why players can get a card for something they do in the tunnel on the way to the locker room.

Even after the match is over:

> After the final whistle has been blown, a player argues with the referee on the field of play and clearly shows dissent verbally

A player who protests to the referee after the final whistle can still face disciplinary action. The last whistle marks the end of the match, but not the end of the referee's authority.

A single whistle can run 90 minutes of soccer because the rules concentrate all that authority in one person.
