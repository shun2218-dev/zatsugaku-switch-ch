### The real culprit: "soluble tannin"

The source of a persimmon's astringency is tannin, a large molecule made of many catechin-type units linked together. Around harvest time, the flesh of astringent persimmons contains about 2% by weight of a water-soluble type of tannin (soluble tannin).

When this dissolves into your saliva, it latches onto proteins in your tongue and the lining of your mouth, creating that intensely unpleasant, tightening sensation. The video's explanation, that "tannin that dissolves easily in water leaches out in your mouth, which makes it taste astringent," matches this mechanism exactly.

So to get rid of the astringency, you don't need to eliminate the tannin. **You just need to turn it into a form that won't dissolve in saliva.**

### Drying turns tannin into an "insoluble" form

To make dried persimmons, the surface of the peeled fruit is dried quickly, and then the fruit is left in the sun to slowly lose moisture. According to a research review, a dry film forms on the surface and the tissue of the flesh breaks down, which makes the fruit's respiration incomplete and produces a substance called acetaldehyde.

Acetaldehyde links soluble tannin molecules together into even larger clumps, turning them into a form that doesn't dissolve in water. In an experiment at Kagoshima University, adding acetaldehyde to persimmon tannin caused it to set into a gel (a jelly-like state) in a relatively short time. Methods that remove astringency with carbon dioxide or alcohol also rely on the same action of acetaldehyde.

One report found that with sun-drying, the soluble tannin and the astringency disappear in roughly 7 to 9 days. It's also thought that as the flesh softens, tannin sticks to fragments of broken-down cell walls and becomes insoluble that way as well.

Just as the video says, the astringent compound itself doesn't vanish. It stays in the flesh in an insoluble form.

### Does it "become sweeter" or just "taste sweeter"?

The phrase "turn sweet" in the title actually mixes two different meanings.

The first is that **you can now taste sweetness that was hidden by the astringency**. Converting the tannin to an insoluble form doesn't increase the sugar. Astringent persimmons contain sugar to begin with; the strong astringency just made it impossible to enjoy the sweetness.

The second is that **the sugar becomes more concentrated as water is lost**, and this isn't just a matter of perception: the concentration really does go up. According to the food composition tables published by Japan's Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, fresh persimmons that have had their astringency removed contain 82.2 g of water per 100 g, while dried persimmons contain 24.0 g. Carbohydrates come to 16.9 g and 71.3 g respectively, so by equal weight, dried persimmons have roughly 4 times as much as fresh ones (these figures are a rough guide, since they vary by variety and production method).

On the other hand, the total amount of sugar in a single persimmon doesn't increase when it's dried. In a research review, sugar consistently made up about 60% of the dry weight (everything other than water) throughout drying. The type of sugar does change, however: one report found that sucrose (the same sugar as table sugar) breaks down into glucose and fructose and almost completely disappears within about 10 days of drying.

### Black specks and white powder

The black, sesame-seed-like specks you see when you cut a sweet persimmon are, as the video says, clumped tannin. In some varieties that develop seeds, the seeds release acetaldehyde and ethanol, which make the surrounding tannin clump together; the clumps then oxidize and turn dark. Not every sweet persimmon gets these specks, though. In sweet varieties like Fuyu, which lose their astringency naturally while still growing on the tree, the astringency disappears without relying on this mechanism.

The white powder on the surface of dried persimmons is also, as the video says, not mold but sugar crystals. It forms when sugar crystallizes as water evaporates from the surface, and it consists of glucose and fructose. In every example studied, there was more glucose than fructose (about 1.7 to 6.5 times as much, depending on the variety and production method).
