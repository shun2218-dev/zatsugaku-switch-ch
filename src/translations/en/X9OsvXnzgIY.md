### It's the same as iron changing color when heated

The National Museum of Nature and Science's explainer starts with a familiar comparison.

> When you heat iron, it first begins to glow a dull, dark red, but gradually turns yellow, and eventually shines a dazzling white. This shows that color is related to temperature.

It goes on to explain why.

> At low temperatures, long-wavelength red light is strongest, and as the temperature rises, short-wavelength blue light becomes stronger.

Then it applies this directly to stars.

> The same is true for stars: the more a star's color shifts from red to yellow to bluish-white, the higher its surface temperature.

That's the exact opposite of our everyday sense that red is a "hot color" and blue is a "cool color."

### Lining up the actual numbers

Here are the star surface temperatures given in the same explainer, arranged from lowest to highest.

| Star | Color | Surface temperature |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Antares (Scorpius) | Red | About 3,500 degrees |
| Arcturus (Boötes) | Orange | 4,200 degrees |
| The Sun | Yellow | About 6,000 degrees |
| Vega (Lyra; the "Weaver Star" of Japan's Tanabata star festival) | White | About 9,500 degrees |
| Regulus (Leo) | Bluish-white | 13,000 degrees |

In Orion, Betelgeuse at the left shoulder is the classic red star, and Rigel at the lower right is the classic bluish-white one. So within a single constellation, you have stars whose temperatures differ by thousands of degrees.

The museum's explainer says this is exactly why such observations matter.

> Because observing a star's color lets us determine its temperature in this way, it is one of the most important kinds of observation.

### Differences in temperature mean differences in mass and age

So why does each star have a different temperature? The same page goes into more detail.

> We know that most stars that look bluish-white are massive, young stars. Red stars, on the other hand, are either low-mass stars or old stars.

The interior of a low-mass star is denser.

> When the density is this high, energy generated at the center has a hard time making its way to the surface. That's why low-mass stars have low surface temperatures.

Old stars are red for a different reason.

> As stars age, they swell to hundreds of times their original radius, becoming what are called red giants. This is why old stars have lower surface temperatures.

In other words, there are two kinds of red stars: ones that were small and dim to begin with, and giants that have swollen up as they near the end of their lives. Antares and Betelgeuse are the latter.

### The colors we see are only part of the true colors

The National Astronomical Observatory of Japan's "The Multi-Wavelength Universe" explains that hotter stars shine brightest at shorter wavelengths. Newborn, hot, massive stars are brightest in ultraviolet light, while cooler stars are easier to see in near-infrared light. That means the "red, yellow, and bluish-white" we distinguish with the naked eye are just a slice of the light stars emit, the range our eyes can detect.

On top of that, the colors we see from the ground are mixed with the effects of Earth's atmosphere. The Subaru Telescope's explainer puts it this way:

> The twinkling of stars is caused by fluctuations in Earth's atmosphere.

When stars low in the sky seem to flicker between red and blue, it's not because the star's own color is changing. It's because the direction the light travels gets disturbed as it passes through the atmosphere. If you want to see a star's true color, your best bet is to look at stars as high in the sky as possible.
