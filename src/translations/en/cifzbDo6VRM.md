### Seawater's freezing point depends on its salinity

The video's claim that "seawater starts to freeze at around minus 1.8 degrees" is roughly right. But the freezing temperature actually changes with how salty the seawater is.

In precise measurements made in 1974 by Fujino and colleagues at Hokkaido University's Institute of Low Temperature Science, the results at 1 atmosphere of pressure were as follows (‰ means parts per thousand, so 35‰ is about 3.5%):

- Salinity 35‰ (about the open-ocean average): −1.92°C
- Salinity 32.8‰: −1.79°C
- Salinity 24.7‰: −1.34°C
- Salinity 17.7‰: −0.95°C

The U.S. National Snow and Ice Data Center (NSIDC) likewise explains that for every increase of 5 in salinity (a unit almost the same as ‰), the freezing point drops by about 0.28°C, and that polar seawater with a salinity of around 32 starts to freeze at −1.8°C. For average open-ocean seawater, "about −1.9°C" is a good figure to keep in mind.

River water also has a little bit of stuff dissolved in it, but the amount is so tiny that its freezing point is almost exactly 0°C.

### Salt gets in the way of water "joining the ice"

In the video, we said that "salt makes it harder for water molecules to link up with each other, which lowers the freezing temperature." That's close as a mental picture, but the exact mechanism is a little different.

The drop in freezing temperature that happens when you dissolve something in water is called freezing point depression. According to encyclopedias, it happens when the dissolved substance can mix into liquid water but can hardly get into the ice crystal itself. How much the temperature drops depends not on what kind of substance it is, but on **the concentration of dissolved particles (ions or molecules)**. An easy way to think about it: when salt particles are around, water molecules have a harder time lining up into an ice crystal, so the water has to get colder before it freezes.

### When seawater freezes, the salt is pushed out

When seawater begins to freeze, tiny needle-like ice crystals form first. According to NSIDC, these crystals are made almost entirely of fresh water, and the salt gets pushed out. That expelled salt becomes extremely salty water (brine), which either drains back into the sea or gets trapped as tiny pockets in the gaps in the ice.

That's why newly formed sea ice is a little salty, but over time the brine gradually drains out. NSIDC explains that old sea ice that has survived for several years has so little salt that polar explorers have used it as drinking water.

Meanwhile, as salt builds up in the surrounding seawater, that water becomes even harder to freeze. Fujino and colleagues' paper also shows that as cooling continues, the ice grows, the remaining seawater gets saltier, and its freezing point keeps dropping.

### Another reason the ocean is slow to freeze

In lakes and ponds, water is heaviest at 4°C, so the colder water near 0°C floats on top, and the ice forms from the surface down.

Seawater with a salinity above 24.5‰, however, keeps getting heavier as it cools all the way to its freezing point. When surface water cools, it sinks, and warmer water rises to take its place, so seawater won't freeze until it has cooled down to a considerable depth. NSIDC says that typically the top 100 to 150 meters of water must cool to the freezing point.

In the Sea of Okhotsk, where drift ice reaches Hokkaido (Japan's northernmost main island), fresh water flowing in from the Amur River dilutes the surface seawater, so cooling just the surface layer is enough for it to freeze easily. Keiichiro Ohshima of Hokkaido University explains that saying "drift ice is frozen Amur River water" is a misconception: ice formed from frozen river water makes up only a tiny fraction of the total.

### Yes, you really do float more easily in the sea

Seawater has a density of about 1.02 to 1.03 grams per cubic centimeter, making it 2 to 3% heavier than fresh water (about 1.00 g). The buoyant force on an object in water is determined by the weight of the water it displaces, so the same body floats just a little more easily in seawater.

That said, the difference is only a few percent. The sea also has waves and currents, so don't assume "I'll float in the ocean, so I'm safe" — come prepared with a life jacket or similar gear.
