### Defined as "the area with weak winds and few clouds"

In the Japan Meteorological Agency's forecasting terminology, the eye of a typhoon is defined like this:

> **Eye of a typhoon**: The area near the center of a typhoon where winds are weak and there are few clouds.

The agency's page explaining typhoon wind characteristics also says that "a typhoon is a giant swirl of air, and near the ground, strong winds blow inward counterclockwise when viewed from above," and then adds that "the area immediately around the center (where air pressure is lowest) is called the 'eye,' and it is a region of relatively weak winds."

Notice the careful choice of words. It doesn't say "no wind"; it says "**relatively** weak winds." The eye is not a completely windless zone.

### Just outside the eye is the most dangerous part of the typhoon

It's dangerous to walk away remembering only how calm the eye is. The Japan Meteorological Agency's page on typhoon rainfall says:

> In a typhoon, vertically developed cumulonimbus clouds surround the eye like a wall, and there the wind and rain are ferocious.

This wall is called the "eyewall." In other words, to get into the eye, you always have to pass through this worst part. And once the eye has passed, the same wall is waiting for you again on the other side.

### "The wind stopped" doesn't mean it's over

The practical meaning of this structure is clear. If, in the middle of a typhoon, the wind suddenly stops and the sky brightens, it may be **not the end but the halfway point**.

What's more, the winds in the second half blow from the opposite direction. Because the center of the swirl passes right over you, the first half hits you from one side and the second half from the other. That's why things that held up against the first-half winds can easily be knocked over by winds coming from the opposite direction.

Staying indoors even after the wind dies down is a conclusion that follows directly from the structure of the eye.

### Only well-developed typhoons have an eye

A clearly visible eye in satellite images is limited to well-developed typhoons. In weak typhoons, or typhoons that have weakened after making landfall, the eye becomes blurry and fades away.

Put the other way around, a sharply defined eye is a sign of a powerful typhoon. If the hole at the center looks crisp in a satellite image on the news, that's a reason to raise your level of caution.
