### Kogarashi is a term officially defined by the Japan Meteorological Agency

First, let's pin down the term. It's listed right there in the Japan Meteorological Agency's glossary of forecast terms.

> Kogarashi: A (fairly) strong, northerly wind that blows from late autumn to early winter.

Season (late autumn to early winter), direction (from the north), and strength (fairly strong). Those are the three conditions.

By the way, the same page lists a similar term.

> Karakkaze

This one refers to a dry wind that blows down after crossing the mountains in winter. Both share one thing in common: they're dry winds.

### Wind from the north doesn't carry much water vapor to begin with

The first reason your skin dries out is the nature of the air the wind brings.

Northerly winds blow out from high-pressure systems over the continent. Air that has traveled over land carries less water vapor than air that has traveled over the ocean.

On top of that, the low temperature itself matters. There's a limit to how much water vapor air can hold, and that limit depends on temperature. As the temperature drops, so does the limit.

Here's how the Japan Meteorological Agency defines it.

> Dew point: The temperature at which relative humidity reaches 100% when the air is cooled while keeping the water vapor pressure constant.

Put the other way around, cold air hits 100% humidity with only a little water vapor. So even at the same relative humidity, winter air **actually holds a much smaller absolute amount of water vapor than summer air**.

Whether moisture leaves your skin depends on that difference in absolute amount. The more room the air has to take on water vapor, the more evaporation from your skin proceeds.

### Wind "carries it away," so evaporation never stops

The second reason is the wind itself.

When there's no wind, a thin layer of water vapor from your skin builds up just above its surface. If this layer is moist, further evaporation slows down.

But when wind hits, this layer gets blown away and replaced with dry air. That's why evaporation doesn't stop.

This is exactly the same mechanism as [why laundry dries faster on windy days](/trivia/4KEmTOiWH_M/). Laundry and skin behave the same way: remove the humid air sitting at the surface, and drying speeds up.

Cold winter winds bring both dry air and wind at the same time, so they hit you with a double whammy.

### Heating makes it worse

And it doesn't end with the conditions outdoors.

When you heat a room in winter, the relative humidity drops even further. Warming the air doesn't increase the amount of water vapor in it. But as the temperature rises, the limit on how much water vapor it can hold goes up, so relative humidity goes down.

Dry wind outside, heated dry air inside. There's nowhere to escape.

### Winter works against your skin, too

It's not just the air. Your skin has its own seasonal challenges.

Sebum production drops as temperatures fall. Sebum acts as a film on the skin's surface that holds back evaporation, so when there's less of it, moisture is lost more easily.

On top of that, when the substances that fill the spaces between cells in the stratum corneum decrease, the skin's ability to retain moisture also drops. When dryness and friction (from clothing, masks, towels) pile up, this structure gets damaged and the dryness gets worse.

In other words, in winter your skin faces four things at once:

1. The air has lots of room to take on water vapor (low absolute humidity)
2. Wind carries away the moist layer at the surface
3. Heating dries things out even more
4. Less sebum means evaporation can't be held back

"Skin gets dry when the cold winds start" really means that all of these line up at once as the seasons change.

### The fix is to "put a lid on it"

Based on how it works, there are two things you can do.

**Add moisture** (humidifiers, toner). But even the water you add will escape if there's dry air and wind.

**Put a lid on it** (cream, lotion, petroleum jelly). Create a film that holds back evaporation in place of the lost sebum. This is the more fundamental fix.

Physically covering the areas exposed to the wind also helps. Scarves and gloves don't just keep you warm; they also protect the moist layer at the surface from the wind. The reason your face in particular gets dry so easily is that it's the only skin that stays exposed all winter long.
