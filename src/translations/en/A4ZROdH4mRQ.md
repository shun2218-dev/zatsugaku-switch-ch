### Salt isn't the only special case

In fact, salt isn't the only food that's allowed to skip a best-before date. The Tokyo Metropolitan Government Bureau of Public Health lists these nine:

1. Starch
2. Chewing gum
3. Frozen desserts
4. Sugar
5. Ice cream products
6. Table salt and umami seasonings
7. Alcoholic beverages
8. Drinking water and soft drinks (limited to those in glass bottles, excluding those sealed with paper caps, or in polyethylene containers)
9. Ice

The reason is spelled out, too: because these foods "basically undergo very little change in quality, omitting the best-before date is permitted."

Sugar, ice, and ice cream being in the same group makes sense once you think about it. Salt isn't some "special food that never spoils" — it's "one of nine foods that the rules say don't need a date."

### The only thing that happens to salt is clumping

According to the Salt Industry Center of Japan, salt **does not deteriorate in quality except for clumping**, even when stored for a long time, so it isn't required to carry a use-by or best-before date.

The main cause of clumping is humidity. In Japan, magnesium carbonate is commonly used as an anti-caking agent to help keep salt from clumping.

### Storage tips: watch out for moisture and odors

The precautions the Salt Industry Center lists are very much grounded in real kitchens:

- **Where to keep it**: Avoid places with big temperature swings or lots of humidity. Specifically, **near the stove** and **under the sink** are spots to avoid
- **After opening**: Don't leave it open. Don't touch it with wet hands
- **Odors**: Salt easily absorbs smells, so don't keep it near detergents and the like

"Under the sink" is called out by name because moisture tends to build up there. Plenty of people probably keep their salt there, so it might be worth rethinking.

### Seasoned salts do have a date

Take a look at the bag you have at home. Seasoned salts mixed with herbs, spices, kombu (kelp), and so on should have a best-before date printed on them. That's because the exemption applies to "table salt" — once other ingredients are added, their own shelf-life issues come into play.

So even when they sit on the same shelf, pure salt and seasoned salt follow different rules.
