### Why does only the boundary change color?

Because that's the only place where the ingredients meet.

Sulfur-containing amino acids (cystine and methionine) are abundant in the **white**, while iron is abundant in the **yolk**. The hydrogen sulfide released from the white during heating is a gas, so it moves through the egg, and when it reaches the surface of the yolk, it meets the iron and becomes iron sulfide. That's why the color appears only at the boundary where the white and yolk touch.

### Is it "green" or "black"?

The video called it a "dark green substance," but sources describe it in slightly different ways. The Food Technology Research Center in Hiroshima Prefecture describes iron sulfide as "black," while an explanation from Kanto Chemical calls it "dark green."

What actually forms is a very thin film deposited on the surface of the yolk, so its appearance ranges from grayish green to nearly black, depending on how intensely the egg was heated. It seems fair to think of "greenish" and "blackish" as just different intensities of the same reaction.

### Older eggs discolor more easily

Even if you boil them the same way, freshness makes a difference. The Hiroshima Prefecture source explains that **older eggs produce hydrogen sulfide more easily than fresh ones, so they're more prone to discoloration**.

If you boiled your eggs for the usual time but they came out greenish today, before blaming the cooking time, think back to whether the eggs had been sitting around for a while since you bought them.

### Practical tips for preventing it

- **Don't overcook**: The Hiroshima Prefecture source gives guidelines of about 10 minutes in boiling water, or about 25 minutes at 80°C (176°F)
- **Put them in cold water right away**: Plunging the eggs into cold water as soon as they're done lowers the temperature, stopping the reaction and preventing discoloration. It also makes the shells easier to peel
- **Add a little vinegar for dishes like thick Japanese rolled omelets**: For dishes made with whole eggs, adding vinegar or something similar to lower the pH has also been suggested

### It's not a sign of spoilage

This discoloration is the result of a chemical reaction caused by heating; it doesn't mean the egg has gone bad or grown mold. It only affects the appearance, and it's more like a marker telling you that you "overdid" the cooking.

That said, if the egg smells clearly off or the white feels slimy, that's a separate issue from discoloration, so don't eat it; throw it out.
