### First off, it's a type of "echophenomenon"

Contagious yawning actually belongs to a named category. Here's how the University of Nottingham describes it:

> Contagious yawning is triggered involuntarily when we observe another person yawn — it is a common form of echophenomena — the automatic imitation of another’s words (echolalia) or actions (echopraxia).

In other words, it's set off involuntarily when we see someone yawn, and it's classed as a kind of echophenomenon, in the same family as echolalia (automatically repeating someone else's words) and echopraxia (automatically copying their movements).

And it's not just a human thing.

> And it’s not just humans who have a propensity for contagious yawning — chimpanzees and dogs do it too.

Chimpanzees and dogs catch yawns too.

### Resisting didn't change how contagious it was

In 2017, a University of Nottingham research team ran an experiment with 36 people.

> They recruited 36 adults to help with their study. These volunteers viewed video clips showing someone else yawning and were instructed to either resist yawning or to allow themselves to yawn.

Participants watched videos of people yawning under two conditions: "try not to yawn" and "go ahead and yawn." The researchers also kept detailed records.

> The participants were videoed throughout, and their yawns and stifled yawns were counted. In addition, the intensity of each participant’s perceived urge to yawn was continuously recorded.

They counted both full yawns and stifled ones, and continuously tracked how strong each person's urge to yawn felt.

Here's what they found:

> Their latest findings show that our ability to resist yawning when someone else near us yawns is limited. And our urge to yawn is increased if we are instructed to resist yawning. But, no matter how hard we try to stifle a yawn, it might change how we yawn but it won’t alter our propensity to yawn.

Our ability to resist is limited. On top of that, being told to resist makes the urge **stronger**. And no matter how hard we stifle it, all that changes is *how* we yawn, not how likely we are to catch the yawn in the first place.

So the feeling we all know, that holding back a yawn just makes you want to yawn more, was confirmed experimentally.

### The cause: "primitive reflexes in the motor cortex"

The team concluded that the explanation lies not in willpower or personality, but in the part of the brain that controls movement.

> Experts at the University of Nottingham have published research that suggests the human propensity for contagious yawning is triggered automatically by primitive reflexes in the primary motor cortex — an area of the brain responsible for motor function.

Contagious yawning is triggered automatically by primitive reflexes in the primary motor cortex. The idea is that the region that issues movement commands starts firing on its own the moment we see someone else's movement.

To investigate, they used magnetic stimulation of the brain.

> To test the link between motor excitability and the neural basis for contagious yawning the Nottingham research team used transcranial magnetic stimulation (TMS).

The researchers report that two measures from this testing, "how excitable the motor cortex is" and "how strong its inhibition is," explained roughly half of the individual differences in how contagious yawns were for each person. In other words:

> Importantly, they have discovered that the urge to yawn — our propensity for contagious yawning — is individual to each one of us.

Susceptibility to contagious yawning varies from person to person, almost like a constitutional trait, and much of it comes down to the properties of the motor cortex. It's not that people who don't catch yawns are "cold"; it's just that motor cortex excitability differs from person to person.

### Still, "closer people are more contagious" has been confirmed

On the other hand, there's also data pointing to a social side. An Italian research team spent a year observing yawns in everyday life.

> Only social bonding predicted the occurrence, frequency, and latency of yawn contagion.

Whether a yawn spread, how often it spread, and how long it took to spread were predicted only by social bonds. According to the paper, neither gender nor nationality made a difference.

They even found a ranking:

> As with other measures of empathy, the rate of contagion was greatest in response to kin, then friends, then acquaintances, and lastly strangers.

Yawns were most contagious in this order: **family → friends → acquaintances → strangers**. What's more:

> Strangers and acquaintances showed a longer delay in the yawn response (latency) compared to friends and kin.

When a yawn came from a stranger or acquaintance, the response took longer. Their conclusion:

> our results demonstrate that yawn contagion is primarily driven by the emotional closeness between individuals and not by other variables, such as gender and nationality.

### "Mirror neurons" are still just a hypothesis

In fairness, there's one thing worth saying about a popular explanation.

The idea that contagious yawning is explained by mirror neurons (a neural mechanism that prepares us to make the same movement when we see someone else make it) is widely repeated, but there's no solid evidence directly showing that human mirror neurons respond to yawning.

The University of Nottingham release is also careful about the neural basis of echophenomena:

> The neural basis for echophenomena is unknown.

That is, the neural basis of echophenomena hasn't been figured out. The study's contribution was showing that, even so, primary motor cortex excitability plays a role.

Other hypotheses, such as the "brain cooling" theory (that yawning serves to cool the brain), are also being explored. For now, three things are fairly certain: (1) catching a yawn is involuntary and hard to suppress, (2) susceptibility varies between individuals in ways rooted in the motor cortex, and (3) the closer you are to someone, the more likely you are to catch their yawn. *Why* we have this mechanism at all is still being researched.

### It grew out of clinical research

Finally, a word on where this research started.

> Echophenomena can also be seen in a wide range of clinical conditions linked to increased cortical excitability and/or decreased physiological inhibition such as epilespsy, dementia, autism and Tourette syndrome.

Stephen Jackson, who led the study, said:

> “We suggest that these findings may be particularly important in understanding further the association between motor excitability and the occurrence of echophenomena in a wide range of clinical conditions that have been linked to increased cortical excitability and/or decreased physiological inhibition such as epilepsy, dementia, autism, and Tourette syndrome.”

Contagious yawning may be on a continuum with the involuntary repetitive movements seen in conditions like Tourette syndrome. A trivial everyday thing like yawning is serving as a gateway to studying how the brain's inhibition works.
