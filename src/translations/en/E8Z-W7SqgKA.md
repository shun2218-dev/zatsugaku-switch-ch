### “Scale clouds” is a nickname, and it can also mean altocumulus

In its observations, the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA) sorts clouds into 10 types based on their height and shape (the ten cloud genera). Cirrocumulus is one of the “high clouds,” along with cirrus and cirrostratus.

The video said that “the official name for scale clouds is cirrocumulus.” It’s true that “scale clouds” (uroko-gumo) most often refers to cirrocumulus, but the term isn’t an official classification; it’s a traditional name. JMA’s kids’ site “Hareru Land” explains that “sardine clouds” (iwashi-gumo) is another name for either altocumulus or cirrocumulus, and that they also go by names such as “sheep clouds” (hitsuji-gumo) and “scale clouds.” In other words, **a cloud you spot in the sky and think “those are scale clouds” might actually be a mid-level altocumulus**.

To tell the two apart, look at the size of the individual cloudlets. The World Meteorological Organization (WMO) International Cloud Atlas distinguishes them by their size when viewed more than 30 degrees above the horizon:

- **Cirrocumulus**: Most cloudlets have an apparent width of less than 1 degree, and they have no shading (gray areas).
- **Altocumulus**: Cloudlets have an apparent width of roughly 1–5 degrees and are often shaded.

With your arm fully outstretched, the width of one finger is about 1 degree. If the cloudlets are small enough to be hidden behind one finger and are pure white, think cirrocumulus; if they’re bigger with gray shading, think altocumulus. That makes them easier to tell apart.

### “5,000 to 13,000 meters” is a rough guide

The video’s “5,000 to 13,000 meters above the ground” roughly matches the WMO’s height range for high clouds in the mid-latitudes (the temperate zone, which includes Japan). However, the WMO describes this as an “approximate height,” and it varies with latitude. High clouds are said to sit at roughly 3–8 km in the cold polar regions and about 6–18 km in the tropics.

### Mostly ice, but some particles stay liquid

The video said that “moisture quickly freezes into ice particles.” The WMO International Cloud Atlas explains that cirrocumulus is made almost entirely of ice crystals; it may contain strongly cooled water droplets, but these are usually quickly replaced by ice crystals.

Meanwhile, the UK's Met Office says that the small cloudlets of cirrocumulus are usually made of both ice and “supercooled” water. Supercooling is when water stays liquid without freezing even at temperatures well below 0°C (32°F). Water high in the sky doesn’t necessarily freeze the moment it gets cold.

### How the dappled pattern forms

JMA’s “Hareru Land” explains that sardine clouds (cirrocumulus and altocumulus) form when the air below the cloud is warmer and the air above is colder, causing “convection.” It’s similar to how, in a pot of heated miso soup, the miso looks like it’s bubbling up in little spots all over. The Met Office also writes that cirrocumulus forms when turbulent up-and-down air currents hit a layer of cirrus.

Where air rises, cloud forms; where it sinks, there’s a gap. The video’s line, “Inside the cloud layer, the air moves up and down in small motions, creating alternating spots where ice particles gather and gaps between them,” fits this mechanism.

The video also said the patches are even in size because “the air currents ripple at regular intervals.” The WMO International Cloud Atlas includes a variety called “cirrocumulus undulatus,” which is rippled in one or two directions, and the Met Office notes that cirrocumulus cloudlets are often arranged like ripples. Air waves are one mechanism that explains the regular arrangement. But since the convection described above also creates regular patterns, it’s best to think of it as **not being determined by just one cause**.

Also, according to the WMO, long-lasting contrails can be reshaped by strong winds aloft and turn into cirrocumulus.

### When it’s a sign of bad weather

The video’s closing line, “the weather tends to turn bad after scale clouds spread out,” goes a little too far.

JMA’s “Hareru Land” says that when cirrostratus or cirrocumulus **spread out and seem to be gradually thickening**, the weather often turns bad. The Met Office also writes that because nothing falling from cirrocumulus reaches the ground, it’s usually associated with fair weather, though it can be a sign of stormy weather to come.

You can’t say the weather will turn bad just because scale clouds appear. If you spot them, it’s a good idea to keep watching for a while to see whether the clouds gradually thicken and cover the sky.
