### "Mikan" is the name for easy-to-peel citrus

Oranges and grapefruits are hard to eat without a knife. Mikan, Japanese mandarin oranges, slip right off with just your fingers.

In "aff," the public relations magazine of Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, a researcher from NARO (the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization) describes the word "mikan" as "a general term for small citrus fruits that are easy to peel." In everyday use, it usually refers to the unshu mikan (known in English as the satsuma mandarin), which has the largest harvest of the group.

In other words, being easy to peel isn't just a bonus feature of mikan. It's the very reason they're called mikan. According to the same article, growing regions across Japan have bred them by selecting for "tastier, easier-to-eat mikan."

### The peel has a colored outer layer and a white inner layer

If you peel a mandarin and turn the peel over, the inside is white and fluffy.

Citrus peel is divided into several layers from the outside in. The colored outer layer is called the **flavedo**, and the white layer inside it is called the **albedo**. The flavedo is lined with tiny sacs filled with oil, which is where the fragrance comes from. The albedo is a soft, sponge-like layer.

A 2025 study published in the journal "Plants" breaks peelability down into two forces: the "peel firmness" you push against when you dig a finger into the peel, and the "adhesion strength" you work against when you pull the peel away from the fruit. According to this study, adhesion strength is mainly determined by the structure of the white, spongy layer.

### The white layer fills with gaps as the fruit grows

So what happens to the white layer in a satsuma mandarin?

A study published in 1975 in the Journal of the Japanese Society for Horticultural Science tracked satsuma mandarin peel under a microscope from the time the fruit formed until harvest. It found that the cells in the white layer stop increasing in number in June, while the fruit is still small. After that, the peel grows as each individual cell gets bigger.

During this time, a substance called pectin builds up at the points where neighboring cells touch, and the cells take on a star-like shape with protrusions sticking out. As the fruit swells and the surface area of the peel expands, those protrusions get stretched longer and longer, and the spaces between cells become air-filled gaps. The paper describes this as "albedo tissue with many intercellular spaces."

The white part looks like a sponge not as a figure of speech, but because it really is full of gaps. And the more gaps there are, the fewer places there are holding the peel and fruit together.

### Ripening also changes the "glue" between cells

Cells are held together by the **pectin** mentioned above. It works like glue that sticks one cell to the next.

The same research group also studied the pectin in satsuma mandarin peel. The amount of pectin in the peel increased until around late July, then declined. Its makeup changed, too. When the fruit was young, the most common type of pectin was the kind that wouldn't dissolve out without hydrochloric acid. But as the fruit grew, water-soluble pectin increased sharply, and by harvest time it made up a very large share.

In other words, a glue that was hard to dissolve turns into a form that dissolves easily. The "Plants" study also presents the idea that the breakdown of pectin between cells is directly involved in how easily the peel comes off. However, within the sources checked for this article, we couldn't find any that directly measured how much changes in pectin affect peelability in satsuma mandarins.

### The riper the fruit, the more easily the peel comes away

There's also research that confirms with numbers that ripening makes fruit easier to peel.

The "Plants" study used machines to measure peel firmness and the adhesion strength between peel and fruit in three late-ripening citrus varieties grown in China. Both decreased as the fruit developed and were lowest at full ripeness. All three varieties were easiest to peel when fully ripe.

Differences between varieties showed up as well. In the hardest-to-peel variety, even when ripe, the cells in the white layer stayed neatly shaped and tightly packed, with only small gaps. In the two easier-to-peel varieties, the cells became irregular in shape as the fruit developed, their arrangement loosened, and the gaps widened, to the point where you could see them with the naked eye on a cut surface.

This study didn't look at satsuma mandarins. Even so, its finding that "the more gaps in the white layer, the easier the peeling" lines up well with the microscope observations of satsuma mandarins.

### When the gaps get too big, you get "puffing"

Easy peeling can also go too far.

NARO's Institute of Fruit Tree Science describes **puffing (ukikawa)** as "a condition in satsuma mandarins in which the peel and flesh have become markedly separated." It's a mandarin whose peel has lifted off the fruit and become loose and baggy. Fruit in this state is said to rot more easily and lose flavor.

According to NARO's materials, puffing is more likely the higher the temperature and humidity. The Wakayama Prefecture Fruit Tree Experiment Station also explains that it increases with high temperatures and humidity during the period when the fruit is coloring. NARO's materials also cite the observation that puffing has become widespread in recent years due to global warming.

In the video, we described puffing as "the peel alone overgrows and lifts away from the fruit." The sources we checked state that puffing increases in hot, humid conditions, but they don't go so far as to describe a mechanism where only the peel overgrows. So here, we'll stick to describing it as "a condition where the peel and fruit have separated significantly."

Growing regions use a method of spraying a mix of two agents that act like plant hormones in late summer. However, it can delay coloring, and the Wakayama Prefecture Fruit Tree Experiment Station notes that it is "not an agent that can completely prevent puffing." A mandarin that slips easily out of its peel is, in part, something growers work hard to maintain, balanced between separating easily and separating too much.
