### "Volante" is a word with many meanings

Look up volante in a Portuguese dictionary and you will indeed find "the wheel used to steer a car," in other words, a steering wheel. The Japanese dictionary *Digital Daijisen* also explains boranchi (the Japanese word for this position) as meaning "the steering wheel of a car or similar vehicle."

But that's not the only thing volante means. The word goes back to the Latin volans ("flying"), and as an adjective it can mean "flying," "portable," or "not staying in one place." As a noun, it refers to all sorts of things, including a badminton shuttlecock, the heavy **flywheel** that smooths out a machine's rotation, and a part of a clock. It's more accurate to think of "steering wheel" as just one of its many meanings.

### "Volante" in Brazil

Portuguese dictionaries also list volante as a soccer term, as a separate meaning. In Brazilian Portuguese, it refers to a player positioned **between the defensive line and the midfield**, a player who takes on a defensive role in midfield (also called médio-volante). In Portugal, a player in the same position is called a trinco.

In Japanese, boranchi likewise refers to a player who snuffs out the opponent's attacks in midfield and builds play from deep positions. The role overlaps almost entirely with what's known as a "defensive midfielder" or "defensive half."

### How true is "steering wheel, so they steer the team"?

In the video, we said that "just as a steering wheel controls direction, the volante steers the whole team." It's an easy image to grasp, but Portuguese dictionaries list the soccer sense of volante as a separate meaning from "steering wheel," and they don't say the position was named after one.

As for why this position came to be called volante, **there are several theories, and no clear record has been found**. Also, Brazilian dictionary definitions put "defense" at the center of the role. It's probably best to enjoy the "helmsman" idea as one way of looking at it, shaped partly by how the role is pictured in Japan.

### Free from what, exactly?

The Italian word libero does indeed mean "free." According to the Italian dictionary Treccani, a libero in soccer is a **defensive player** who has no assignment to watch a specific opponent (man-to-man marking) and instead organizes and directs the defense as a whole. Rather than "free to go anywhere," the sense is "free from marking duties."
