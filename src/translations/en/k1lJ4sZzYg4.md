### The source of the sweetness: maltose

As the video explains, much of a roasted sweet potato's sweetness comes from maltose, which forms when starch is broken down during cooking. Raw sweet potatoes contain almost no maltose, but heating produces a lot of it.

The job of breaking down the starch falls to an enzyme called β-amylase, which sweet potatoes naturally contain. However, this enzyme can barely act on raw starch. Only once the starch turns into a paste-like state with heat and water (that is, once it **gelatinizes**) can the enzyme break it down.

### "The temperature where the enzyme works best" and "the temperature where sweetness forms" are different

The video said that "the enzyme works best at around 70 degrees," but strictly speaking, that's not quite right.

According to a research report from Niigata Seiryo University and others, the temperature at which sweet potato β-amylase works best (its optimal temperature) is around 40–50°C (104–122°F). Its activity starts dropping at around 60°C (140°F), and at 75°C (167°F) only about 30% of its peak activity remains.

Meanwhile, the temperature at which starch begins to gelatinize is said to be around 75°C in ordinary varieties (though it ranges from 50 to 80°C depending on the variety and growing conditions). Materials from NARO (the National Agriculture and Food Research Organization of Japan) also state that ordinary sweet potato starch gelatinizes at "around 70°C." The video's line that starch "softens above 65 degrees" is best taken as a rough guideline that may hold for some varieties.

In other words, the two are out of sync:

- At lower temperatures, the enzyme is active, but the starch hasn't gelatinized yet, so it can't be broken down.
- By the time the temperature rises and the starch gelatinizes, the enzyme is gradually weakening.

Lots of maltose forms only in a limited temperature range around 70°C, where **the starch has gelatinized and the enzyme can still work**. So around 70°C isn't "the temperature where the enzyme is most active"—it's "the temperature where the conditions for both the starch and the enzyme overlap just right."

The research report concludes that for making roasted sweet potatoes, methods like stone roasting or pot roasting, which raise the potato's temperature gradually and hold it in the 70–80°C range for a long time, make good sense. Notably, in the same report's experiments, potatoes simply held at exactly 70°C for 35 hours had a considerably lower sugar content than potatoes roasted at 150°C for 3 hours. It seems the important thing is to "pass slowly through" the temperature range and then finish by cooking the potato thoroughly.

### Microwaving makes them less sweet

As the video explains, heating a sweet potato quickly in the microwave makes it pass through this temperature range fast, shortening the time the enzyme has to work. A Brazilian study (2023) compared sweet potatoes cooked in a pressure cooker, an oven, a microwave, and other methods, and found that the microwaved ones had the least sugar.

An interesting case is Quick Sweet, a variety developed by NARO. Its starch gelatinizes at a temperature about 20°C lower than ordinary varieties—around 50°C. Because the starch becomes breakable while the enzyme is still highly active, it tends to turn sweet even with short cooking times and is said to be well suited to microwave cooking.

### Temperature isn't the only thing that decides sweetness

At the start of the video, we flatly stated that "what decides how sweet a roasted sweet potato gets is the temperature inside the potato while it's cooking." But temperature is just one important factor—other things affect sweetness too.

- **Variety**: In a study published in the Journal of the Japanese Society for Food Science and Technology, enzyme strength and starch gelatinization temperature differed by variety, and the stronger the enzyme and the lower the gelatinization temperature, the more maltose formed when the potatoes were steamed. There were even cases where the same variety, grown in cool Hokkaido, had a lower gelatinization temperature and produced more maltose.
- **Post-harvest storage**: Sweet potatoes get sweeter after being stored for a while than they are right after harvest. In trials in Chiba Prefecture, both Silk Sweet and Beniharuka varieties became sweeter during storage as saccharification (the conversion of starch and other substances into sugar) progressed, and developed a moist, sticky texture.

The sweetness of roasted sweet potatoes from specialty stone-roasting shops likely has to do not only with how they're roasted, but also with choosing varieties that sweeten easily and using potatoes that have been stored for just the right amount of time.
