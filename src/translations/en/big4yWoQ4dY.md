### The stars themselves aren't twinkling

As the video explains, stars don't twinkle because they're getting brighter and dimmer.

James Lattis, director of UW Space Place at the University of Wisconsin–Madison, says stars twinkle "because we're looking at them through the atmosphere." The cause isn't the stars, but the air between them and us. (We cover the Subaru Telescope's explanation in [our article on why stars twinkle at night](/trivia/QknT5fmfLrA/).)

### Air at different temperatures bends light

When light crosses a boundary between air with different properties, its direction bends slightly. This is called **refraction**.

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, what distorts starlight is differences in air temperature. Cold air bends light more strongly than warm air, because in warm air the molecules are spaced farther apart. Air temperature typically drops by about 6.5°C (about 11.7°F) for every kilometer of altitude, so layers of air at different temperatures are stacked up in the sky.

Lattis also explains that pockets of warm and cold air are constantly rising, sinking, and swapping places, and each time, the path of the light changes. That's what the video means by "uneven temperature and density."

The Hong Kong Observatory also cautions that it isn't accurate to say twinkling is "caused by air turbulence." Its point is that wind itself doesn't make stars twinkle; what bends the light is unevenness in the air's temperature.

### They flicker because they look like "points"

Stars are extremely far away. According to the Hong Kong Observatory, effectively only a single ray of light reaches us from a distant star. When that ray gets bent away from your eye, the star seems to vanish for an instant, and when it comes back into your eye, the star reappears.

Lattis likewise explains that because stars appear only as points, their light arrives as a thin beam, and we can see that beam wobble. That's what's really behind the flickering sparkle of stars.

### Stars near the horizon twinkle more, and even change color

In an article for Scientific American, astronomer Phil Plait writes that when you look straight up, starlight passes through roughly 100 kilometers of air, but toward the horizon that becomes more than 1,000 kilometers. With that many more chances for the light to be bent, stars low in the sky twinkle more.

The video says the light "passes through thicker atmosphere." It's not that the atmospheric layer itself gets thicker; it's that, because you're looking through it at a slant, the light's path through the air gets longer.

According to Plait, different colors of light bend by slightly different amounts. So at one moment only red light might get bent toward you, making the star look reddish, and the next moment it might look bluish. He says this is especially noticeable with bright white stars like Sirius when they're near the horizon. (We also cover star colors in [our article on why stars look different colors](/trivia/X9OsvXnzgIY/).)

### Why planets look steady

Planets are much closer than stars. The Hong Kong Observatory writes that planets have a larger apparent size than stars and can't really be called "points," something you can confirm even with binoculars.

Lattis explains that a much wider beam of light reaches us from a planet, and the wobbles in light coming from different parts of its disk average out, so planets don't twinkle as much as stars. Plait also notes that Jupiter usually appears several tens of arcseconds wide (one arcsecond is 1/3600 of a degree), so even when its light twinkles, its position barely shifts and it looks steady.

According to the Hong Kong Observatory, the Sun and Moon don't twinkle for the same reason: so many rays of light reach us that even if some are deflected, how they look hardly changes. If you spot a bright "star" in the night sky that shines more steadily than the others, it might be a planet.
