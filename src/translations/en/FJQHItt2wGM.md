### Only the top of the water warms up

First, here's why the bottom actually is colder.

What mainly warms a pool is sunlight. Water absorbs sunlight, but the light doesn't reach very deep. Because water absorbs light, most of the energy gets used up in the top few dozen centimeters (roughly the top foot or so).

And since warmed water becomes lighter, it stays on top. Cold water is heavier, so it sinks. This arrangement is stable and doesn't mix on its own.

The result is water temperature in layers: warm on top, cold below. The boundary is surprisingly sharp, so as you lower your feet, you feel it suddenly get cold partway down.

This boundary is called a **thermocline**, and the same structure forms in lakes and oceans. A deep pool behaves just like a small lake.

### Wind doesn't reach the bottom either

In a shallow pool, people moving around and wind blowing across the surface mix the water. The whole pool tends to end up at the same temperature.

In a deep pool, stirring at the surface only affects the upper layer. The water at the bottom doesn't move and stays cold.

For the same reason, crowded pools mix easily, while pools with few people tend to keep their layers. The bottom of the pool is especially cold first thing in the morning because nobody stirred it up overnight.

### Water draws heat away 24 times faster than air

Now for how it feels. Let's compare thermal conductivity (W/m·K) using material from Osaka Kyoiku University.

| Substance | Thermal conductivity |
| --- | --- |
| Water (10°C) | 0.582 |
| Dry air (0°C) | 0.0241 |

That's **24 times**.

Your skin doesn't measure temperature; it senses how fast heat is leaving your body. That's why, even at the same 25°C, being in water feels much colder than being in air.

A 25°C (77°F) room is comfortable, but a 25°C pool is cold. That's not strange at all: the rate at which heat is drawn away differs by a factor of 24.

Convection also plays a role in water. Water warmed around your body moves upward, and cold water flows in to replace it. The same thing happens in air, but water carries away more heat.

### At the bottom, you get hit twice

To sum up, at the bottom of a deep pool, two effects overlap:

1. **The water itself is colder** (it's layered, and the bottom hasn't warmed up)
2. **Water draws heat away quickly** (24 times faster than air)

So the feeling that "the bottom is cold" isn't an illusion. It's an accurate observation.

### Staying still makes you even colder

One more thing: if you stay still in the water, your body cools down quickly.

While you're swimming, your muscles produce heat, so things balance out. But once you stop, the heat supply drops while heat loss keeps going.

And when you rest at the edge of the pool and start feeling cold, that's because water evaporating from your wet body takes heat with it (the heat of vaporization). That evaporation is why it can feel colder out of the water than in it.

### Oceans and reservoirs are even more extreme

The same structure occurs in natural bodies of water. In a summer lake, the surface might be 25°C, but dive a few meters and it suddenly turns cold. At the beach, you might feel a cold current just around your feet. These are all thermoclines.

River fog forms downstream of dams even in winter because warm water from deep in the reservoir is released. The story from [the river fog article](/trivia/S11MFGRYF2Y/) is the flip side of these temperature layers. Deep water is cold in summer and warm in winter. Since seasonal changes have a hard time reaching it, its temperature differs from the air temperature above ground.

### The fix: get up, or keep moving

In practice, it's simple:

- **Move to where your feet can touch**: you get to stay in the warm layer
- **Keep moving**: your muscles produce heat and can keep up even as the water around your body is replaced
- **Get out and dry off**: staying wet is what chills you the most

Wetsuits work precisely because of thermal conductivity. Air bubbles trapped in the material create an insulating layer between the water and your body. It's the same principle as [a wool sweater](/trivia/MKfS0AIVDhM/), just used underwater.
