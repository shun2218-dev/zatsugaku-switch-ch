### 1. The hat trick was a cricket hat

The Japan Football Association's Q&A explains the origin like this:

> There are various theories about the origin, but it is said to come from cricket, a sport that originated in England.

In cricket, a hat was presented for the feat of getting three batters out in a row.

> Getting three batters out in a row is said to be even harder than in baseball, and hats came to be presented to honor the players who achieved this feat.

The idea of "a hat for three feats" carried over into soccer. The fact that six goals is called a double and nine goals a triple is also a holdover from this way of counting in units of three.

### 2. Stoppage time isn't a rough guess

That number shown near the end of a match is calculated by the referee, working with the fourth official, by adding up the time play was stopped. Injury treatment, stoppages after goals, time taken for substitutions—each one gets added together.

The final decision rests with the referee alone. That's why the number shown can differ even when matches play out similarly.

→ For more, see [Did You Know Stoppage Time Is Actually Calculated "Down to the Second"?](/trivia/APzWBxviFd4/)

### 3. The goal net was born to end arguments

In the 19th century, soccer goals had only posts and a crossbar. Neither the referee nor the crowd could tell whether the ball had passed inside the frame or gone wide. Arguments broke out almost every game.

In 1891, the net was invented, and the ball could now be physically stopped. It was an invention for making calls, not for looks.

→ For more, see [The Surprising Reason Soccer Goal Nets Were Invented](/trivia/s49Zv1Ob0Ok/)

### 4. The corner flag is equipment you can't pull out

Here's the text of Law 1 of the Laws of the Game:

> A flagpost, at least 1.5 m (5 ft) high, with a non-pointed top and a flag must be placed at each corner.

"Must be placed"—it **has to be placed** at each corner. Like the goalposts, it's specified as equipment required to play the match.

What's more, the corner arc is drawn using this flagpost as its reference point.

> The corner area is defined by a quarter circle with a radius of 1 m (1 yd) from each corner flagpost drawn inside the field of play.

The flag is the reference point for that 1 m radius. Pull it out, and the basis for determining the position disappears.

→ For more, see [Did You Know Pulling Out the Corner Flag During a Match Is an Offense?](/trivia/-yKVxCVlvC0/)

### 5. With VAR, a computer draws the offside line

Dedicated cameras installed around the stadium record the positions of players' bodies at high frequency and convert them into skeletal line data. The offside line isn't based on the referee's eyes; it's a virtual line drawn by a computer from that data.

However, VAR only corrects "clear and obvious errors." On close calls, the original decision stands. This design is why refereeing mistakes never drop to zero.

### 6. The real World Cup trophy is in Zurich

Here's the announcement from the FIFA Museum:

> The FIFA World Cup™ Original Trophy is back on display in our iconic FIFA World Cup™ Gallery at the FIFA Museum in Zurich, Switzerland – now featuring an updated base plate.

The real trophy is on display at the FIFA Museum in Zurich, Switzerland. The names of every winning country since 1974 are engraved on its base, and a new base plate is made for each tournament.

> The name of 2026 FIFA World Cup™ winners Spain has been added to the existing engravings, joining all previous champions since 1974, when the FIFA World Cup Trophy was first used.

Since it's an object that keeps a running record of history, the winning country can't take it home. Its predecessor, the Jules Rimet Trophy, was awarded permanently to Brazil after it won three times, and it was later stolen and never recovered.

→ For more, see [Why Can't the Winners Take the World Cup Trophy Home?](/trivia/heQnzxTcog0/)

### 7. Number 10 became the ace's number later on

Shirt numbers were originally just assigned mechanically by position. Number 10 wasn't special.

The leading theory is that in the formations of the time, it was close to the center of the attack, and as one player with outstanding skill after another wore it, its meaning built up over time.

→ For more, see [Why Did the Number 10 Shirt Become "the Mark of the Ace" in Soccer?](/trivia/gV7613zxE7k/)

### What you see when you line up all seven

The origins of words (hat trick, number 10), systems for fairness (net, flag, VAR, stoppage time), and an agreement for preservation (the trophy).

Four of these are measures to "make sure everyone reaches the same answer." The net makes it visible whether the ball went in, the flag shows the reference point for position in three dimensions, VAR makes up for the limits of the human eye, and stoppage time turns stopped time into a number.

The other three are things that history gave meaning to after the fact: the cricket hat, the position numbers, and the lesson of the lost original trophy.

Rules are designed, and words build up over time. The mix of these two may be part of what makes watching soccer so enjoyable.
