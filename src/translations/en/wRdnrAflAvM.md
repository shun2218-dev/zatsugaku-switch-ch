### The cap's real name: the "cupule"

That little bowl-shaped part on top of an acorn—most people call it a "cap."

Its proper name is the **cupule**. Dictionaries describe the cupule as a bowl- or ball-shaped structure that covers part or all of the fruit in plants of the beech family.

A researcher at the Museum of Nature and Human Activities, Hyogo, asked people in a survey at an acorn seminar what they call this part. Among 175 third- and fourth-graders, 88.6 percent answered "cap." Several older people reportedly called it a *hakama* (a traditional pleated Japanese garment worn over the lower body). The report also notes that small children sometimes answer "underpants."

This researcher is apparently running a "don't call it a cap" campaign. When the parts of an acorn are compared to the human body, the end where the cupule attaches is called the "bottom" or the "belly button." Calling something that covers the bottom a "cap" really is a bit odd.

### What it really is: the flower's "bracts"

So what is a cupule made of?

According to dictionaries, a cupule forms when the **bracts** of the female flower gather and fuse together. A bract is a modified leaf that grows at the base of a flower. Another dictionary explains it as many bracts gathered and fused into one.

In the video, we described the cupule as "made of many bracts—leaf-like parts at the base of the female flower—gathered together." That matches the dictionary explanations.

If you look closely at the surface of the bowl, you'll see small scales packed tightly together. Materials from the Ministry of the Environment (Japan) call these scales "involucral bracts."

### An acorn is a "fruit," not a "seed"

Then what is the part wearing the cap?

An explanation from the University of Shizuoka states clearly that an acorn is not a seed. An acorn is a type of fruit called a **nut**—a fruit with a hard shell.

Inside that hard shell is the seed. According to the explanation, most of the seed consists of cotyledons, which are packed with starch. Cotyledons are the first leaves, storing nutrients for when the sprout emerges. They make a feast for forest animals such as bears, deer, squirrels, and mice.

In the video, we said the cap is "not part of the fruit—it's a separate structure that wraps around it." The fruit is the main body of the acorn, while the cupule is a separate part that develops from the bracts at the base of the flower.

### The cap's pattern tells you the type of tree

The shape and pattern of the cupule vary depending on the kind of tree.

According to a survey guide from the Ministry of the Environment (Japan), there are 18 representative species of acorn-bearing trees found in Japan. You can roughly tell them apart by examining the acorn and its cupule. Does the cupule wrap only the bottom of the fruit, or the whole thing? Are the scales on its surface needle-like, scale-like, or arranged in stripes? By working through differences like these in order, you can identify the tree.

Dictionaries give examples too. In Japanese beech and daimyo oak, the tips of the bracts are spine-like. In Japanese evergreen oak and Japanese white oak, the bracts fuse together to form ring-shaped stripes.

According to the University of Shizuoka's explanation, the sawtooth oak's cupule is bowl-shaped, with many long, thin scales around it. Chinquapin (*shii*) nuts ripen while completely enclosed in their cupules, which then split open to let the nut out.

A chestnut's spiny bur is also a type of cupule. A bowl and a bur look completely different, but both are the same part in different forms.

### What is it for?

In the video, we said the cupule "is thought to support and protect the developing fruit."

There's research on this. A paper published in a botany journal in 2020 observed how fruits develop—from when they first form until they ripen—in 20 species of *Lithocarpus* (stone oaks). It reported that the cupule served to give the developing seed additional protection.

One of the things it guards against is insects. The paper notes that the fruits it studied and the acorns of oaks (*Quercus*) are eaten by similar insects. The University of Shizuoka's explanation also mentions insects that eat acorns. The acorn-cutting weevil *Mechoris ursulus* lays its eggs in young acorns still on the branch, then snips off the tip of the twig. Larvae of acorn weevils and others also grow by eating acorns.

However, this paper looked at *Lithocarpus*. For this article, we couldn't find any source that directly confirmed the same thing for the cupules of konara oak or sawtooth oak, which are common in parks. It's probably best to stick with saying the cupule "is thought to have a protective role."

### What happens to the cap when an acorn falls?

In the second half of the video, we introduced an observation record about Japanese white oak acorns: "they often fall with their caps on, but on the ground they're usually separated from them," and "only about 1 in 100 still has its cap."

While writing this article, we tried to reopen the original source, but the page wouldn't load and we couldn't verify its contents. So in this article, we won't present those numbers as fact. Please understand that what the video introduced was an observation record from one particular place, not something confirmed as a general trait of Japanese white oaks.

Some things have been confirmed, though. There are trees, like chinquapins, whose cupules split open to release the nut. How the cap and the fruit part ways differs from tree to tree.
