### Not "smoke," but a tiny fog

Dry ice is carbon dioxide in solid form. At minus 78.5°C (about -109°F), it turns directly into a gas without becoming a liquid first (sublimation). The "minus 79 degrees" in the video is this temperature rounded off. One kilogram of dry ice (about 0.64 L in volume) turns into roughly 500 L of gas.

Carbon dioxide gas is colorless and odorless, so you can't see it. Materials from Japan's Consumer Affairs Agency also explain that the white fog seen around dry ice is "moisture in the air that has turned into droplets of water or ice as the temperature drops." The video's explanation that "the white fog isn't carbon dioxide" and that "it's the same thing as fog" is exactly right.

Your breath turning white on a cold day and a chilled windowpane fogging up belong to the same family of phenomena: water vapor in the air cools down and becomes tiny droplets of water.

### It flows down by riding on "heavy air"

Carbon dioxide gas is about 1.5 times as heavy as air. Air chilled by dry ice also becomes denser (heavier) than the warmer air around it. The white droplets themselves are very small and light, so they aren't falling under their own weight. **They ride along on the flow of cold, heavy gas and travel downward with it.**

As the flow mixes with the surrounding air and warms up, the water and ice droplets turn back into water vapor, and the white fog quietly vanishes.

### The fog may be gone, but the carbon dioxide remains

The key point here is that "white fog = carbon dioxide" is not true. As the video's hook says, what looks white is water droplets. Flip that around, and **even when the white fog is no longer visible, the carbon dioxide hasn't gone away**. The invisible carbon dioxide spreads out, but it also flows to low places and accumulates there.

Carbon dioxide normally makes up about 0.04% of the air, but according to the Consumer Affairs Agency, at 3% it causes difficulty breathing, headaches, dizziness, and nausea, and at 30% people lose consciousness almost immediately. In a test by the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan, the carbon dioxide concentration inside a closed coffin containing about 10 kg of dry ice exceeded 30% after 20 minutes. There have in fact been reports of people dying after putting their faces inside a coffin at a funeral.

The video's closing warning that "using a lot of it in a small room or a car can let carbon dioxide build up to dangerous levels" is also accurate. The Consumer Affairs Agency says it is dangerous to use or store dry ice in poorly ventilated places such as basements or vehicles, and cites a workplace accident in which a worker transporting dry ice in a van died.

### Tips for handling dry ice

Based on materials from the Consumer Affairs Agency and the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan, here's what to keep in mind at home.

- **Don't touch it with bare hands**: At minus 78.5°C, it can cause frostbite if it touches your skin, for example if you pick it up barehanded. There have been reports of people getting hurt after grabbing a white lump in a delivery bag thinking it was ice, or of children touching it. Use thick gloves when handling it, and keep it out of children's reach.
- **Don't put it in a sealed container**: If you put it in a plastic bottle or jar and close the lid, it turns into gas and expands inside, and the container may burst. In a test by the National Consumer Affairs Center of Japan, a 500 mL plastic bottle filled with 350 mL of water and 30 g of dry ice, then capped, burst with a loud bang after 55 seconds.
- **Ventilate**: Carbon dioxide collects in low places. Avoid handling or leaving dry ice in places that are hard to ventilate, such as basements or cars. Don't put your face into places where carbon dioxide can build up, like a coffin.
- **If you have leftovers**: Leave it in a well-ventilated spot and wait for it to turn into gas and disappear on its own.
