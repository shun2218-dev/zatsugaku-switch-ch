### Not a star, but space dust

Despite the name "shooting star," as the video explains, it isn't actually a star moving across the sky.

According to NASA, the rocks and dust drifting through space are called **meteoroids**, and they range in size from dust grains to small asteroids. When a meteoroid plunges into Earth's atmosphere at high speed and glows, that's a **meteor** (a shooting star), and one that survives all the way to the ground is a **meteorite**. The same object gets a different name depending on whether it's out in space, glowing in the sky, or lying on the ground.

The American Meteor Society explains that meteor showers happen when Earth passes through a trail of debris left behind by a comet (or occasionally an asteroid). According to the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan (NAOJ), there are also shooting stars that have nothing to do with meteor showers; these are called **sporadic meteors**.

### Grains the size of sand, hitting at tens of kilometers per second

In the video, we described the source dust as "about a millimeter to a few centimeters." The American Meteor Society says most visible meteors are caused by particles ranging from the size of a grain of sand to a pebble. It also notes that a particle doesn't need to be large to produce a striking streak of light.

It's surprising that a bright flash crossing the night sky comes from a particle small enough to sit on your fingertip. What makes such a tiny particle light up is its incredible speed.

According to the American Meteor Society, meteoroids typically enter Earth's atmosphere at roughly 11 to 72 kilometers per second. That works out to about 40,000 to 260,000 kilometers per hour. At 72 kilometers per second, it covers 72 kilometers in a single second.

The Society also notes that there are more meteors in the early morning hours than in the evening, because the dawn side of Earth runs into more incoming meteoroids. If you're hunting for shooting stars, the hours before dawn are your best bet.

### What's glowing: vaporized dust and air

NAOJ explains that a meteor glows from the energy released when its source material plunges into the atmosphere. NASA also writes that most of the material vaporizes in the atmosphere, leaving a bright streak of light. That's what the video means by "the dust vaporizes and glows along with the air."

According to NAOJ, a shooting star's color depends on which molecules and atoms are glowing. For example, magnesium or oxygen can show up as green, and nitrogen as red. A shooting star's color reflects both what the dust is made of and what the air is made of.

### How high they glow, and for how long

According to the American Meteor Society, meteors mostly glow at altitudes of about 80 to 120 kilometers above the ground. NAOJ also explains that meteors glow several tens of kilometers above the ground. Even when one looks like it streaked by right nearby, it's actually glowing far up in the sky.

In the video, we said it takes "only about a second" to burn out. NAOJ says shooting stars are visible for anywhere from "less than a second" up to "about 5 seconds" at most, with some rare ones visible for around 10 seconds. To be precise, some last less than a second and some last a bit longer.

According to NAOJ, especially bright shooting stars are called **fireballs**, a term often used for those brighter than about magnitude −4. Across Japan, a few are spotted per month on average.

### They almost never reach the ground

According to NAOJ, shooting stars usually vaporize in the atmosphere and almost never reach the ground. The American Meteor Society likewise says most of the particles that produce meteor showers can't survive the trip through the atmosphere.

Even so, material from space is steadily piling up on Earth. NASA estimates that about 44 tons of meteoritic material from space falls to Earth every day. A shooting star that flashes for an instant and vanishes in the night sky is the visible part of that.
