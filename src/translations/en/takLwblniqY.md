### That wind has a name

The cold wind that comes before a summer evening downpour has a proper name in meteorology: the **gust front**.

According to the Japan Meteorological Agency (JMA), a gust front "occurs when a mass of cold (heavy) air formed beneath a cumulonimbus cloud flows out, under its own weight, toward the side of warm (light) air."

What's worth noting is the scale. According to the same explanation, "its horizontal extent is larger than that of tornadoes or downbursts, and can reach several tens of kilometers or more." In other words, the cool wind you felt may be blowing at the same moment in places dozens of kilometers away.

### The video's explanation vs. the JMA's explanation

In the video, we said that "raindrops absorb heat from their surroundings as they evaporate, so the air gets chilled quite a bit." This is a commonly used explanation for how air cools inside a cloud.

The JMA's definition, on the other hand, focuses on **what happens after** the air has been cooled. The resulting mass of cold air "flows out toward lower ground because it's heavy"—that's what a gust front is. It all makes sense once you think of it this way: why the air cools and why it flows outward are two separate steps.

### A cold wind isn't just a "sign of rain"

The JMA groups the violent gusts that occur with cumulonimbus clouds into three types—tornadoes, downbursts, and gust fronts—and calls them collectively "**tornadoes and other violent gusts**." In other words, gust fronts are one of the things people are warned to watch out for.

A downburst, a member of the same family, is "a violent flow of air that blows out horizontally when a downdraft descending from a cumulonimbus cloud strikes the ground," with the outflow spreading roughly several hundred meters to about 10 kilometers. Its defining feature is that the damage spreads out over a wide area.

They matter for aircraft, too. The JMA's aviation weather explanation notes that both downbursts and gust fronts are born from the downdrafts of cumulonimbus clouds, and that a sudden increase in headwind can push a plane off its landing approach path.

### If you feel that wind, there's something to do before worrying about getting wet

If a cold wind suddenly blows on a summer evening, the next thing to think of after "the laundry" is **lightning**. That wind is evidence that a cumulonimbus cloud is nearby, so lightning strikes are possible even before the rain starts. If you're outdoors, the safest move is to get inside a building or a car first.
