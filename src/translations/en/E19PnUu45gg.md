### Steering toward the fall

When you ride a bike, you're constantly making small steering adjustments without even noticing.

If your body leans to the right, you turn the handlebars slightly to the right. The wheels then head right and end up back directly underneath you. A 2011 paper in the journal *Science* describes bicycle balance as "steering toward the fall to bring the wheels back under the rider."

It's just like the game of balancing a broom upright on your palm. If you move your hand toward the side the broom is falling, you can keep it standing.

### Righting itself with no rider

Here's where it gets interesting.

According to the paper, many bicycles will steer and recover on their own as long as they're moving—even with nobody on them. This is called **self-stability** (the ability to keep itself upright).

As a Delft University of Technology press release puts it: a bicycle moving fast enough won't fall over, even if you push it from the side.

As shown in the video, a stationary bike doesn't have this property. It only shows up when the bike is moving.

### The classic explanations: "spinning wheels" and "front-wheel offset"

So why does the bike steer itself? According to the press release, scientists have been puzzling over this since the 19th century. Two explanations were long accepted.

The first is the **gyroscopic effect** (the tendency of a spinning object to hold its orientation). When the spinning front wheel tilts, it creates a force that turns the handlebars toward the lean. It's fundamentally the same property that keeps a spinning top from falling over.

The second is **trail**. The point where the front wheel touches the ground sits slightly behind the point where an imaginary line extended from the steering axis hits the ground. That gap is the trail. It works much like the little wheels on a shopping cart, which naturally swivel to line up with the direction you're pushing.

The "spinning wheel" and "front-wheel design" mentioned in the video correspond to these two ideas.

### A bike with neither still didn't fall

But in 2011, a team including Delft University of Technology and Cornell University overturned this view.

The team built a special bicycle. They paired its small wheels with additional wheels spinning in the opposite direction, canceling out the gyroscopic effect. They also placed the front wheel's contact point slightly *ahead* of the steering axis line—giving the bike negative trail.

When they gave this bike a push, it stayed upright as long as it was going faster than 2.3 meters per second (about 8 km/h, or roughly 5 mph). Even when knocked from the side, it returned to an upright position.

The paper concluded that neither the gyroscopic effect nor trail is "necessary" for self-stability. It also says they're not "sufficient" either, because there are many designs that have both yet aren't stable at any speed.

Researcher Dr. Schwab said in the press release that "gyroscopic effects and trail help, but they are not essential."

### Weight distribution is also key

So why did that special bike steer toward its fall?

Here's the paper's explanation. On this bike, the mass around the handlebars sits low and ahead of the steering axis. A weight that's low down falls faster than one that's high up—the same way a short pencil topples faster than a tall broom.

Because the front assembly tries to fall before the rest of the frame does, the front wheel steers toward the side the bike is falling.

That said, the paper is candid about this too: no simple explanation has been found for why it steers by just the right amount at just the right time. Many design factors are tangled together in complicated ways.

### It's only stable within a "just right" range of speeds

Self-stability doesn't happen at every speed.

According to a 2007 report by the research team, the benchmark bicycle model used for calculations is stable between roughly 4.3 and 6.0 meters per second. That works out to about 15 to 22 km/h (roughly 10 to 14 mph). In one set of measurements on a real bicycle, the range was about 4.0 to 7.9 meters per second.

Go slower than that, and the side-to-side wobble gradually grows until the bike falls. At the high-speed end, the instability is said to be mild enough that a little steering can correct it.

The reason we don't fall over at slow speeds in everyday riding is that we're balancing with the handlebars and our bodies. In other words, there's a range of speeds where the bike helps you out, and a range where the rider has to do the work.
