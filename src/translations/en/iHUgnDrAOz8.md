### The rule restricts three things at once

Law 15 of the Laws of the Game doesn't set just one condition for a throw-in.

First, the feet.

> have part of each foot on the touchline or on the ground outside the touchline

**Each foot**: part of both feet, each one, must be on the touchline or on the ground outside the line. Just one foot isn't enough.

Next, the hands and how the ball is thrown.

> throw the ball with both hands from behind and over the head from the point where it left the field of play

With both hands, from behind the head and over the top of it. And from the spot where the ball went out.

Only when all three of these come together is it a correct throw-in.

### "Feet on the ground" means no run-up and no takeoff

Why make players keep both feet on the ground? If you think about it, this effectively **bans jumping and pushing off**.

If one foot were allowed to leave the ground, a player could take a run-up, push off on one foot, and use their whole body to throw the ball far. Something like a long-jump motion would become possible. At that point a throw-in would be less "throwing" and more "launching," and the ball could travel close to the center of the pitch.

Keeping both feet on the ground and throwing with both hands from behind the head limits the muscles you can use to your arms and upper body. That puts a natural cap on how far the ball can go.

The ball crosses the touchline dozens of times in a single match. If every one of those gave a team a long-range attacking weapon, it would change the nature of the game. The idea that a throw-in is "a way to restart play," not "an attacking weapon," is reflected in these restrictions on posture.

### A violation doesn't always mean a redo

In real matches, throw-ins with a foot off the ground happen fairly often. How they're handled is interesting.

> The throw-in is retaken by the opposing team because the Laws of the Game require the thrower to have part of both feet on/touching the ground (on or behind the touchline) at the moment of delivering the ball. However, if the opponents gain possession of the ball, the referee may apply the advantage if this is of greater benefit than awarding them the throw-in.

As a rule, the throw-in goes to the other team. But if the opponents have already won the ball, letting play continue is better for them than giving them a throw-in, so the referee can let it go.

This is the idea of "advantage" that runs through all of the Laws of the Game: if calling a foul would hurt the team that was fouled, don't call it.

> Although the throw-in was not taken correctly, as the ball came into play, the referee may apply advantage and allow play to continue if this benefits the opposing team.

### "From behind the head" is checked strictly too

There's also an official interpretation for violations in how the ball is thrown.

> Law 15 clearly states that the thrower must throw the ball 'from behind and over the head'. If the movement of the ball starts in front of or above the head (not behind it), the throw-in is not taken correctly. In this case, the opposing team will take the throw-in.

If the ball's movement starts **in front of** the head or **directly above** it, it's not a correct throw-in. It has to start from behind the head.

This also ties into limiting distance. Pulling the ball back behind your head before throwing restricts your arm swing. A throw that pushes the ball out from in front of your chest can actually generate more power, but that's not allowed.

### When does a throw-in happen in the first place?

While we're at it, let's check the standard for when the ball is considered out.

> A throw-in is awarded to the opponents of the player who last touched the ball when the whole of the ball passes over the touchline, on the ground or in the air

**Whole of the ball**: when the entire ball has crossed the touchline. If any part of the ball is still over the line, it's still in play.

The reason you sometimes see plays on TV where "it looked like it went out, but play continued" is this "whole of the ball" condition. If the ball is touching the line even a little, it's still in.

### The referee's whistle outranks the assistant referee's call

One more practical point from the official interpretations.

> The ball goes out of play while two players are challenging for it near the touchline. The assistant referee indicates that the throw-in is awarded to Team A but a Team B player quickly picks up the ball and throws it onto the field before the referee intervenes.

The assistant referee signals a throw-in for Team A, but a Team B player throws it in first. Situations like this really do happen.

Throw-in decisions are made quickly, and players often move without waiting for the officials' signal. That's why, by the numbers, throw-in errors are one of the most common fouls in a match. The game still keeps flowing because the idea of advantage is applied so widely.

If referees called every throw-in where a foot came off the ground, the game would be stopping constantly. What referees are watching isn't just the wording of the rule, but whether "the design that the restriction is meant to protect" has been broken.
