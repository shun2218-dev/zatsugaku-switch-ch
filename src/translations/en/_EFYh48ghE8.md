### Tea "fermentation" is the work of the leaves' own enzymes

In the video, we described tea's color as coming from "pigments that form while the tea leaves are fermented." But this kind of "fermentation" isn't like natto (fermented soybeans) or yogurt, where microbes do the work.

Black tea and green tea both start out as leaves from the same plant, the tea plant (Camellia sinensis). According to an explainer from Saitama Prefecture, green tea (an unfermented tea) is made by steaming or otherwise treating freshly picked leaves right away to stop the **oxidizing enzymes** in the leaves, while black tea (a fermented tea) is made by letting those enzymes work as fully as possible. In the tea world, this enzyme-driven change has long been called "fermentation."

When the leaves are rolled, their cells break open, and the enzymes meet the catechins in the leaves (a family of compounds responsible for astringency). Catechins themselves are nearly colorless, but once the enzymes oxidize them and they link together, they turn into colored substances. It's a lot like how cut apples or vegetables turn brown.

### The pigments behind black tea's color

An explainer by a researcher at Japan's Tea Research Station (then part of the Ministry of Agriculture and Forestry) names two main pigments that give black tea its color:

- **Theaflavins**: a vivid, reddish orange. The more there are, the brighter and more beautiful the tea's color.
- **Thearubigins**: reddish-brown to dark brown. They give the color depth and richness. Their makeup is complex, and much about their detailed structure is still unknown.

The color of brewed tea (called *suishoku*, literally "water color," in Japanese) is determined by the mix and proportions of these pigments.

### Lemon makes tea paler because it makes it more acidic

In a study from Shukugawa Gakuin College, researchers added lemon slices to store-bought black tea and examined how the color changed.

- Plain tea had a pH (a measure of how acidic or alkaline something is) of about 5.2, which dropped to about 3.5 after the lemon was added.
- To the eye, the color shifted from reddish-brown to a pale yellowish-brown.

When they used hydrochloric acid or acetic acid instead of lemon, the color became paler in the same way. Even when they boiled the lemon first to stop its enzymes from working, the result was the same. Since any acid, not just lemon, changes the color the same way, the main reason the color gets paler appears to be, just as the video says, **the shift toward acidity**.

The study also observed that in acidic conditions, the color doesn't just get a little paler: **the hue shifts from red toward yellow**. That's why lemon tea looks a bit yellowish if you look closely.

That said, tea's pigments don't work like litmus paper, where a single substance flips color between acid and alkali. The same study notes that the color changes from acidic to alkaline can't be explained by one simple reaction. It's best to think of the "paler" look as the combined result of many different pigments each changing in their own way.

### Making it alkaline darkens the color

The video's point that "add something alkaline like baking soda, and the color gets even deeper and darker" was also confirmed in the same study. Tea brought to a pH of about 8.2 with baking soda was darker than plain tea, and at even stronger alkalinity, it went from a deep reddish-brown to a blackish reddish-brown.

Another study (Food Chemistry, 2001) also reported that under alkaline conditions, some theaflavins and catechins break down or change form, making the tea darker.
