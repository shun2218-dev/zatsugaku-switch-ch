### What they share: "engineered compatibility"

Whether things stick together depends on the attractive forces between molecules. And how strong that attraction is changes depending on the partner.

An explanation from Toyo University sums up this "difference depending on the partner" neatly.

> Also, even with the same water, the shape of a droplet changes depending on what it's placed on, and it may either be repelled or spread out and wet the surface.

The same water behaves differently depending on the surface it sits on. Some surfaces repel water, and others let it spread out.

> The change in a droplet's shape is expressed by the "contact angle." (omitted) With "water repellency," where water is repelled, the contact angle is large; with "hydrophilicity," where water spreads out, the contact angle is small.

If you deliberately design for this "compatibility," you can make things stick or make them come apart. Detergent, erasers, and bandages are all products of this kind of design.

### Detergent: using molecules that reach out to both sides

Here's the Japan Soap and Detergent Association's explanation.

> Detergent delivers its strong cleaning power because the molecules of its main ingredient, surfactants, disperse in water and form clusters called "micelles." When the force of the washing machine is applied, these micelles surround the dirt on fibers even more effectively and pull it away.

One end of a surfactant molecule gets along with water, and the other end gets along with oil. A single molecule has two kinds of compatibility.

That lets it act as a go-between for water and oil, a "poorly matched pair." It grabs greasy dirt with its oil-loving side and floats it in the water with its water-loving side facing out.

And it also keeps the dirt from coming back.

> Also, it surrounds the dirt that has come loose in the water, preventing "redeposition," where dirt reattaches to the fibers.

### Erasers: stick more strongly than paper, then carry it away

Pencil writing is just graphite particles caught on the bumps and grooves of paper fibers. There's no chemical bond.

The material in an eraser (PVC resin + plasticizer) has a stronger affinity for graphite than paper fibers do. So when you rub, the graphite moves onto the eraser.

Then **the eraser itself wears away**. The surface layer holding the graphite breaks off as eraser crumbs.

This does the same job as detergent's "redeposition prevention." Detergent floats dirt in the water and washes it away; an eraser traps graphite in crumbs and drops it. Both have a second-half job: "don't put back what you removed."

→ For more, see [Why does an eraser remove pencil marks?](/trivia/-on9IHsYkgU/)

### Bandages: keeping close contact, breaking down with water

A bandage's adhesive works its way into the bumps and grooves of your skin's surface and attracts it at molecular-scale distances. It isn't hardened in place; it's simply in close contact.

That close contact breaks when another substance gets in between. If water seeps in, there's less area where the adhesive and skin touch directly, and it peels off.

What's more, soap is a substance made precisely for "removing oily things from skin." Bandages are weak in the bath because their adhesive has the same properties as the stuff soap targets.

→ For more, see [Why do bandages peel off more easily in the bath?](/trivia/DyTCmxmX4nE/)

### Put the three side by side and the same blueprint appears

| | What sticks to what | Force used | How to remove it |
| --- | --- | --- | --- |
| **Detergent** | Dirt to surfactant | Compatibility of hydrophilic and lipophilic groups | Rinse with water |
| **Eraser** | Graphite to resin | Compatibility of plasticizer and graphite | Drop it along with the crumbs |
| **Bandage** | Adhesive to skin | Close contact via intermolecular forces | Pull it off, let water in |

**Each one takes advantage of a difference in compatibility: "sticks more strongly to B than to A."**

Detergent makes dirt stick more strongly to surfactants than to fibers. An eraser makes graphite stick more strongly to resin than to paper. A bandage makes adhesive stick more strongly to skin than to air.

Removing things works on the same idea, too. Either bring in a partner that sticks even more strongly, or add a substance that wedges in between.

### This idea is used even more widely

If you look for things that run on the same principle, you'll find plenty around you.

- **Lint rollers (sticky rollers)**: Move dust from the floor onto the sticky surface, then peel off and toss the sheet. Same structure as an eraser
- **Dry dusting sheets**: Attract dust with static electricity and trap it in the fibers
- **Chromatography**: A chemistry technique that separates mixtures based on differences in how strongly each substance adsorbs
- **Silica gel**: Adsorbs water molecules onto its porous surface. [Heating releases them, so it can be regenerated](/trivia/WI2xa5RaFpc/)
- **Activated carbon in water filters**: Adsorbs odors and impurities onto the surface of the carbon

**"Differences in how strongly things stick" are a universal tool for separating and removing.**

Chromatography, used in chemical analysis, works on almost the same principle as an eraser. A mixture is moved across a material that each substance adsorbs to with different strength, which separates them. The difference in sticking power is used to pull out only what you want.

It's a fun thought that eraser crumbs balling up and falling off and separating substances in a lab rest on the same idea.
