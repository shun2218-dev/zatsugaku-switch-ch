### They get sweeter because the potato is still alive

Harvested potatoes are still alive. Even after leaving the field, they keep respiring, and when exposed to cold, they start reacting to protect themselves.

One of those reactions is turning starch into sugar. More sugar inside the cells makes them harder to freeze, which helps the potato withstand the cold. This phenomenon is called **cold-induced sweetening**, and it works in the same direction as the way plants get through the winter.

So potatoes kept in the fridge really do get sweeter. It's true that when you use them in simmered dishes or potage, you may notice more sweetness than with potatoes stored at room temperature.

One thing worth keeping in mind, though: this happens through a different mechanism than the sweetness of sweet potatoes. The sweetness of a roasted sweet potato comes from enzymes converting starch into maltose during cooking. Cold-induced sweetening in potatoes happens during storage, before any cooking.

### The extra sugar turns into acrylamide when fried

This is the most important part of the story. NARO's research report sums it up in a few short sentences:

> When raw potatoes are stored at low temperatures, their sugar content increases, and when they are then fried, they not only brown heavily but also produce large amounts of acrylamide. Potatoes stored in the refrigerator are best not deep-fried plain.

Acrylamide is a substance that forms unintentionally when carbohydrate-rich foods are cooked at high temperatures. Materials from the Food Safety Commission of Japan also cover this.

The extra sugar reacts with amino acids to create that golden-brown color and toasty aroma (the Maillard reaction). Acrylamide is produced as a byproduct. The more sugar there is, the further the reaction goes.

In other words:

- **Refrigerate** → starch turns into sugar (sweeter)
- **Fry that sugar at high heat** → darker browning + more acrylamide

The same cause produces both a desirable result (sweetness) and an undesirable one (acrylamide) at the same time.

### Boiling or steaming isn't a problem

The key point is that the outcome depends on how you cook them. Since acrylamide forms at high cooking temperatures, it's less likely to form with water-based cooking.

Simmered dishes, steamed potatoes, miso soup, potage: none of these go above the boiling point of water (around 100°C / 212°F), so acrylamide isn't an issue. You just get the bonus of extra sweetness.

What you want to avoid instead is plain deep-frying, French fries, potato chips, and high-temperature oven roasting. Frying oil reaches 160–180°C (320–356°F), so sugar-rich potatoes brown more easily and produce more acrylamide.

### Choosing where to store them

Here's the practical takeaway:

| Planned use | Where to store |
| --- | --- |
| Simmered, steamed, or in soups | The fridge is fine (they get sweeter) |
| Fried dishes, French fries | Avoid the fridge; store at room temperature in a cool, dark place |

The usual way to store potatoes is not in the fridge but in a cool, dark, well-ventilated place. It's tempting to put them in the fridge to keep them from sprouting, but if you're going to fry them, that backfires.

Also, soaking potatoes in water reduces the reducing sugars on their surface, so soaking before frying helps lower acrylamide. Materials from the Food Safety Commission of Japan also show that soaking in water before cooking can reduce the amount of reducing sugars and similar compounds.

### It doesn't happen with processed or frozen products

One more note: all of this applies only to **raw potatoes**.

Starch turns into sugar because the potato is alive and its enzymes are working. In processed products that have already been cooked, or in frozen French fries, the enzymes have stopped working. That's why sugar doesn't build up even if they sit in the freezer for a long time.

The only thing to watch out for at home is the combination of "putting raw potatoes in the fridge, then frying them."

### "They get sweeter" has a catch

To sum up, this bit of trivia comes with a catch.

It's true that potatoes get sweeter in the fridge. But that sweetness comes from extra sugar, and frying that sugar at high heat increases browning and acrylamide. You can't simply say, "Sweeter means better."

That's why NARO's conclusion takes the form of concrete advice: "Potatoes stored in the refrigerator are best not deep-fried plain."
