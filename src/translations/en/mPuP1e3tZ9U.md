### The rule says "the whole of the ball"

The definition of a goal is a surprisingly short sentence.

> A goal is scored when the whole of the ball passes over the goal line, between the goalposts and under the crossbar, provided that no offence has been committed by the team scoring the goal.

**whole of the ball**. When the entire ball has crossed the goal line.

This is where people often get it wrong. "Crossed the line" doesn't mean "the front of the ball crossed." Unless the ball, right down to its back edge, has completely passed the line (its outer edge), it's not a goal.

In other words, if any part of the ball is still directly over the line, it isn't a goal yet. Forget "one ball's width": if just a few millimeters remain, it's no goal.

The same condition applies to the touchline. The throw-in rule also says "the whole of the ball passes over the touchline." Soccer's lines are consistently designed so that "as long as it's on the line, it's still in."

### Some moments are impossible for the human eye

Here's the problem. The ball is about 22 cm (roughly 8.7 inches) in diameter, and shots can exceed 100 km/h (about 62 mph). The goal line is no more than 12 cm (about 4.7 inches) wide.

Under these conditions, an assistant referee standing away from the goal line has to judge with the naked eye whether "the whole ball crossed." And the moment that matters is only the instant the ball goes deepest. If the keeper scoops it out, the whole in-and-out takes a few tens of milliseconds.

There are situations that are physically impossible to judge. That's why technology was brought in.

### Goal-line technology is required to work "within one second"

Law 1 of the Laws of the Game contains the provisions for goal-line technology (GLT).

> GLT systems may be used to verify whether a goal has been scored to support the referee's decision.

It can be used to **support** the referee's decision. It doesn't make the decision in place of the referee.

Its scope is also limited.

> GLT applies solely to the goal line and is only used to determine whether a goal has been scored.

Only the goal line. Only to decide whether a goal was scored. It's not used for offside or fouls.

And the conditions for communicating the result are specific.

> The indication of whether a goal has been scored must be immediate and automatically confirmed within one second by the GLT system only to the match officials (via the referee's watch, by vibration and visual signal, and/or via the referee's earpiece/headset); it may also be sent to the video operation room (VOR).

**Within one second**, **automatically**, **only to the officials**. It arrives on the referee's watch as a vibration and a display.

What matters is that all three conditions are met. If it took longer than a second, play would stall. If it weren't automatic, human judgment would creep in. And because it goes only to the officials, spectators and players don't find out first and cause an uproar.

There are also conditions for using it.

> The use of GLT must be stipulated in the competition rules.

If it isn't written into the competition rules, it can't be used. That's why it isn't available in every match.

> If GLT is used in competition matches, the competition organisers must ensure that the system (including any potentially permitted modifications to the goal frame or technology in the ball) meets the requirements of the FIFA Quality Programme for GLT.

Including any modifications to the goal frame or technology inside the ball, the system must meet the requirements of FIFA's quality program. Whether it's camera-based or magnetic-field-based, only certified systems can be used.

### A separate system from VAR

They often get mixed up, but GLT and VAR are different things.

| | GLT | VAR |
| --- | --- | --- |
| Covers | Goal line only | Goals, penalties, red cards, mistaken identity, etc. |
| Decision | Automatic | Humans (video referees) review |
| Speed | Within one second | No time limit |
| How the referee is told | Watch vibration and display | Radio conversation |

GLT is a system where a machine answers instantly; VAR is a system where people review footage and advise. Here's how the Japan Football Association explains it:

> It's short for Video Assistant Referee. Their role is to support the referee while watching footage in the Video Operation Room (VOR).

The timing of its introduction is also noted:

> It was first introduced at a tournament organized by FIFA (Fédération Internationale de Football Association) at the 2016 FIFA Club World Cup held in Japan, and at the FIFA World Cup it was first introduced at the 2018 tournament in Russia.

### The final say always belongs to the referee

Even with technology involved, the principle of the rules hasn't changed. Here's Law 5 of the Laws of the Game:

> The decisions of the referee regarding facts connected with play, including whether or not a goal is scored and the result of the match, are final.

The referee's decisions, including whether a goal was scored and the result of the match, are final.

Both GLT and VAR are positioned as tools that help with this final decision. Even if the watch buzzes, it's the referee who declares the goal. It may look like a formality, but a design that concentrates "who is responsible" in a single person is quite important for how the rules work in practice.
