### 140 = "14 countries × 10 names"

Why 140? The Japan Meteorological Agency's explanation shows where the number comes from.

> 14 Asian countries that cooperate on things like typhoon monitoring each submit 10 names, and a list of 140 names is prepared in advance

So 140 isn't just a nice round number. It's **the result of 14 countries each contributing 10 names**. This system started in 2000. Before that, typhoons were referred to by number only.

Because each country submits names in its own language, the list is full of words that reflect each country's culture, such as animals, plants, and constellations. Japan submits names of constellations.

### They get reused

The 140 names are used in order, and once they're used up, the list goes back to the beginning. Roughly 25 typhoons form each year, so it takes about five years to go all the way around.

If you look at the JMA's "Table of past typhoon numbers and names," you can see the same names actually appearing again and again. For example, "Pabuk" was given to typhoons in 2001, 2019, and 2024.

So if you feel like you've heard a news report with the same name before, you're not misremembering.

### The list isn't fixed; names get replaced

This is where the "only 140 names" story continues. The list isn't made once and left alone; it gets revised.

In a notice the JMA issued on June 25, 2026, four names were changed.

| Old name | New name |
|---|---|
| Haikui | Tianma |
| Ewiniar | Tirou |
| Toraji | Gaeguri |
| Saola | Saobien |

The names of typhoons that caused major disasters may be retired at the request of the country that submitted them. That same country then submits a new name to fill the empty slot. So the total stays at 140, while the names themselves keep changing.

"There are only 140 names" is true, but "the same 140 names forever" is not.

### Numbers and names are separate

In Japan, typhoons are also given numbers like "Typhoon No. X of Reiwa Year X" (Reiwa is the current Japanese era name). This is a serial number assigned in the order typhoons form each year. The Asian name, on the other hand, comes in order from the list of 140. The two run on separate systems.

On top of that, Japan sometimes gives a special name to a typhoon that caused a particularly major disaster, such as "Typhoon No. 15 of Showa 34 (the Isewan Typhoon)." That means a single typhoon can end up with three labels: a number, an Asian name, and a special Japanese name.
