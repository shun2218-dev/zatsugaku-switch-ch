### The hexagon comes from "how molecules are arranged inside ice"

Snow is made of ice crystals that form when water vapor in clouds freezes directly into ice. In ordinary ice, each water molecule's oxygen atom is surrounded by four other oxygen atoms arranged in a regular tetrahedron (a triangular pyramid), linked by hydrogen bonds (the attraction between water molecules). Viewed from above, this network forms a hexagonal mesh.

In the video, we said that "molecules link up at fixed angles, so they become hexagonal." That's the right idea. To be a bit more precise, **each molecule reaches out in four directions at fixed angles to connect with others**, and as a result the whole ice crystal takes on a hexagonal order (the hexagonal crystal system).

Encyclopedias also explain that the sixfold symmetry of snow crystals "comes from the hexagonally symmetric arrangement of the water molecules that make up the ice crystal." A crystal that grows slowly takes the basic shape of a "hexagonal prism," with two faces on the top and bottom and six faces around the sides.

### Whether it becomes a plate or a column depends on temperature

Which direction the hexagonal prism grows in makes a huge difference in how it looks. If it spreads sideways (parallel to the hexagonal faces), it becomes a hexagonal plate or a star-shaped crystal; if it grows lengthwise, it becomes a hexagonal column or a needle-like crystal.

The person who uncovered this relationship was Ukichiro Nakaya of Hokkaido University. In 1936, he succeeded in making artificial snow crystals in a low-temperature laboratory, and showed that a crystal's shape is determined by "air temperature" and "how much water vapor is available (supersaturation)." Nakaya's famous saying, "Snow crystals are letters sent from the sky," means that you can tell what conditions were like high above from the shape of the crystals that fall.

Later, B. J. Mason in the UK and Teisaku Kobayashi in Japan, among others, repeated the experiments with more precise equipment. Because Nakaya's diagram had a problem with how water vapor was measured, the version corrected by Kobayashi is now the standard, and it's sometimes called the "Nakaya–Mason–Kobayashi diagram." According to the Encyclopedia Nipponica, the shape switches at around −4°C, −10°C, and −22°C.

- About 0 to −4°C: plates
- About −4 to −10°C: columns and needles
- About −10 to −22°C: plates (with lots of water vapor, the familiar tree-branch shape)
- Below −22°C: columns

Kenneth Libbrecht of Caltech likewise explains that slender columns and needles grow well around −6°C, and large, thin plates grow well around −15°C. Columns and needles are hexagonal if you slice them crosswise, but they look nothing like a "hexagonal star."

### "Always hexagonal" goes too far

In the video, we said "under a microscope, snow crystals are always hexagonal" and "every snow crystal always has a hexagonal basic shape," but that was a bit of an overstatement. To be accurate: "the arrangement of molecules in ice follows a hexagonal order, but the crystals that fall don't necessarily look hexagonal."

- Misshapen crystals are the most common: Libbrecht writes that neatly symmetrical crystals are favored by photographers but aren't that numerous, and that irregular crystals are "the most common type."
- Triangular crystals: Crystals that look like triangles with their corners cut off have been recorded since around 1820. In a 2009 experiment by Libbrecht and colleagues, about 5% of plate-shaped crystals took this shape at −10°C with relatively little water vapor. They look as if every other side of a hexagon grew much larger, and the surrounding airflow is thought to play a role.
- Crystals with 12 branches: When two small crystals collide in midair, stick together, and keep growing, they can become a crystal with 12 branches.
- Stuck-together snow: What usually falls as a single "snowflake" is often a cluster of dozens to hundreds of crystals stuck together, which makes the shape of any one crystal hard to make out.

### Try observing them yourself

According to the Fukuoka Regional Headquarters of the Japan Meteorological Agency, the larger snow crystals can be 1 mm or more across, so you can sometimes see them right on your clothes. Using a magnifying glass, or taking a photo and zooming in, makes their shapes easy to see. Not just stars, but columns, needles, and misshapen crystals too are each a kind of letter telling you about "the temperature and water vapor high above."
