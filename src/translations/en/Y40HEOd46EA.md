### Dry sand can't even support its own weight

Grab a handful of dry sand and it trickles away the moment you open your hand. Try to make a pile, and all you get is a gentle slope.

A 1997 report in the science journal Nature opens like this: any child playing on a beach knows that wet sand and dry sand behave completely differently. With wet sand you can build a sandcastle with sharp corners, but with dry sand it falls apart.

A 2012 paper likewise explains that when sand is shaped into something like a sandcastle, dry sand can barely support its own weight.

It's the same sand, so why does adding just a little water make such a big difference?

### "Water bridges" linking grain to grain

The answer lies in tiny bridges of water that form between the grains of sand.

The 2012 paper explains that what makes wet sand firm is the **capillary bridges** that form between grains. A capillary bridge is a very small blob of water gathered where two grains touch. It connects the two grains like a bridge.

The water surface of the bridge isn't flat; it's curved. According to the paper, this curvature creates a pressure difference, which becomes a force pulling the grains toward each other.

The source of this is water's **surface tension**. Surface tension is the force by which water tries to make its surface as small as possible. It's also why water droplets bead up into balls on leaves (see "[Why does morning dew form round drops on leaves?](/trivia/9oQSCVzdRd8/)").

Each individual bridge exerts only a very small force. But sand contains countless grains, and bridges form between them. The paper explains that the resulting "network of grains connected by bridges" is what holds up shapes like a sandcastle.

### Just a tiny bit of water will do

So how much water should you add?

In the 2012 paper, the researchers measured how firm wet sand was while varying the amount of water. It was strongest when water made up about 1 percent by volume. That's so little that the paper itself calls it "very small."

The 1997 report looked at an even smaller scale. It found that when grains about a millimeter in size were coated with a liquid layer just nanometers thick, the angle at which they could be piled up changed dramatically. A nanometer is one-millionth of a millimeter.

In the video, we said that "just the right amount of moisture is what firms up the sand." Looking at the research numbers, that "just right" amount is much smaller than you might think.

### A bit too much water is fine

Does that mean you have to measure the water precisely or the sand won't hold together?

Here's where there's an interesting finding. According to a study published in Nature Materials in 2008, properties of wet sand such as its firmness hardly change even when the amount of water varies over a wide range.

The research team used X-rays to examine, in 3D, what shape the water takes inside a lump of sand. The shape of the water in sand is very complex and changes greatly depending on how much water there is. Even so, the water didn't fill up the gaps completely; it settled into shapes that left open spaces. The team explains that firmness isn't strongly affected by the amount of water because the water settles into shapes like these.

That's why you can build a sandcastle at the beach without carefully measuring out the water.

### Soak it, and the bridges disappear

Add too much water, though, and it's a different story.

The 2012 paper explains that when ordinary sand is submerged in water, the water bridges between the grains break down and the sand loses its strength.

The bridges can exert their force because there's a boundary between water and air, in other words, a water surface. When the gaps are completely filled with water, the surface itself disappears. The source of the pulling force is gone.

The same reason likely explains why waterlogged sand at the bottom of a bucket turns sloppy and runs.

The same paper also describes an experiment that turns this idea on its head. With special water-repellent sand, the roles of water and air are swapped underwater. "Air bridges" form between the grains, so apparently you can build a sandcastle even underwater.

### Tips for building a taller sandcastle

The 2012 paper is titled "How to construct the perfect sandcastle." The research team examined at what height a column made of wet sand would collapse.

A column buckles and collapses under its own weight. The taller it can stand without collapsing, the wider its base. The paper shows a relationship in which the height is proportional to the base radius raised to the "two-thirds power." A wider base lets you build higher, but doubling the width doesn't quite double the height.

By the paper's calculations, a column made of ordinary beach sand with a base radius of 20 centimeters could stand about 2.5 meters tall.

Another tip is to pack the sand down firmly. The paper says that compacting sand makes it stronger, and notes that sandcastle builders always do this.

Add just a little water, pack it down tight, and make the base wide. What kids naturally do on the beach matches the research findings quite well.
