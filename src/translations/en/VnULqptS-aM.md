### 1. Why mountain weather "turns bad in the afternoon"

On a sunny morning, mountain slopes in the sun heat up quickly, and so does the air touching them. According to the U.S. National Park Service, on sunny days this creates air currents that creep up the slopes, and especially in summer, these become the source of afternoon showers and thunderstorms. Air cools as it rises, so the water vapor it carries turns into clouds.

The Japan Meteorological Agency also explains that towering cumulus clouds (cumulonimbus) form when strong sunshine sends moist air near the ground rushing upward, and that they often appear from early afternoon into evening on hot, humid, calm summer days. The video's explanation matches this mechanism, and the hiking rule of "get moving in the morning" has a real basis.

That said, this tends to happen on days with strong sun and moist air. It doesn't mean mountain weather always turns bad every afternoon. The National Park Service also notes that it's most noticeable in summer.

### 2. Contrails that vanish and contrails that linger

According to a fact sheet put together by the U.S. Environmental Protection Agency, Federal Aviation Administration, and National Oceanic and Atmospheric Administration, contrails form when hot, humid engine exhaust mixes with the cold air high up. The water vapor in the exhaust first condenses into droplets around tiny particles, which then quickly freeze into ice particles. The video said "it's below freezing up there," but at the altitudes where planes fly, it can get as cold as the minus 50s °C (around −60 °F or colder).

As the video says, whether a contrail disappears quickly or lingers depends on the moisture up high.

- **When the air aloft is dry**: The ice particles disappear quickly, and the contrail is gone within seconds to minutes.
- **When the air aloft is very humid**: The ice particles grow by taking in moisture from the surrounding air, and the contrail can last for hours, sometimes even more than a day.

In other words, long-lasting contrails grow by taking in not just the water vapor from the exhaust, but also moisture that was already in the upper air. Also, humidity at ground level can be very different from humidity up high. What a contrail tells you about is only the moisture at the altitude where the plane is flying.

The idea that "a lingering contrail means the weather is going downhill" is one of the **traditional sayings for predicting weather by watching the sky (known in Japan as *kanten bōki*)**. It is a sign that the upper air is moist, but you can't predict the weather from contrails alone, so it's best enjoyed alongside the weather forecast.

### 3. Why the sky looks high and clear

The Japan Meteorological Agency says that in autumn, sunny days when a migratory high-pressure system covers the area, the air is dry, and temperatures are comfortable are often called "refreshing weather." The crisp look of a clear autumn sky comes from this dry air.

The video said that "summer air holds a lot of water vapor, and light hits those particles and scatters," but here's a small clarification. Water vapor itself is an invisible gas. What makes the sky look whitish and hazy is the **tiny water droplets and fine particles that have absorbed moisture** floating in the air. In the Japan Meteorological Agency's terminology, a state where visibility is reduced by such particles is called *moya* (mist). These particles are much larger than air molecules and scatter light of all colors equally, so the sky looks whitish and hazy.

The blue color of the sky itself comes from air molecules strongly scattering blue light (→ [Why is the sky blue?](/trivia/tYn7fs759Hs/)). In dry air, less of that whitish light gets mixed in, so the blue looks deeper. Also note that the sky "feeling higher" is just an impression; the sky itself doesn't actually get any higher.

### 4. Why fewer stars are visible on full-moon nights

According to the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, as the Moon waxes, the lit portion grows and the Moon as a whole gets brighter. A full moon rises around sunset and sets around sunrise, so **there's a bright moon in the sky all night long**.

When that bright light faintly brightens the whole night sky, the light of faint stars gets lost in the background and becomes hard to see. As the video says, if you want to see a starry sky, it's better to aim for times when the Moon isn't up rather than full-moon nights. By the way, for about a day on either side of the new moon, the Moon is near the Sun and dark, so you can't see it at all.

### 5. Why the cycle of phases is about 29.5 days

The numbers in the video are accurate. According to the National Astronomical Observatory of Japan, the time it takes the Moon to go around Earth once (the sidereal month) is about 27.3 days, and the cycle of phases (the synodic month) is about 29.5 days. While the Moon makes one trip around Earth, Earth also moves along its path around the Sun, so the Moon has to travel that much extra before it lines up with the Sun the same way again. That "extra" takes about two days.

Note that 29.5 days is an average. The actual length of each cycle of phases varies slightly every time.

### 6. Why stars twinkle

According to NASA, most stars shine at a steady brightness. They appear to twinkle because air moves around in Earth's atmosphere, and starlight reaches us while being slightly bent along the way. The video's explanation matches this.

The closer a star is to the horizon, the thicker the layer of air its light passes through before reaching your eyes, so it twinkles more. This is also just as the video described.
