### The blue of the sky is the color of "scattered light"

Sunlight looks whitish, but it contains all seven colors of the rainbow mixed together. When this light enters the atmosphere, it hits nitrogen and oxygen molecules and scatters in all directions. Because the molecules are much smaller than the wavelength of light, the scattering that happens here, called "Rayleigh scattering," means that **the shorter the wavelength, the more strongly the light is scattered**. The strength is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength, so by this calculation, blue light (about 450 nm) scatters roughly 5 to 6 times more readily than red light (about 700 nm).

The scattered blue light reaches our eyes from every direction in the sky, so the whole sky looks blue. The sky near the horizon looks whitish because light traveling such a long distance gets scattered many times over, and the colors mix back together.

### Why isn't the sky violet?

Violet scatters even more strongly than blue. The reason the sky still doesn't look violet is mainly explained by two things: "sunlight contains little violet to begin with" and "the human eye is more sensitive to blue than to violet" (the UK Met Office's explanation also cites these two points).

In the video, we introduced a third reason: "violet light is absorbed in the upper atmosphere." However, what ozone high in the sky strongly absorbs is mainly invisible ultraviolet light; visible violet light isn't significantly reduced there. It's more accurate to think of **the two main reasons as the amount of light and the eye's sensitivity**.

### Sunsets, clouds, and rainbows each involve other mechanisms too

In the evening, sunlight passes through a long stretch of atmosphere, so blue light is scattered and reduced along the way, leaving a larger share of red and orange light. Rather than "only red is left," think of it as "the proportion of red light increases." Dust and fine particles in the air can intensify the red of a sunset or, if there are too many, dull its colors, depending on their amount, size, and altitude (→ [Why do sunrises and sunsets turn red?](/trivia/0uRqMKmXTPU/)).

The water droplets in clouds are much larger than the wavelength of light, so "Mie scattering" occurs, scattering all colors about equally, which is why clouds look white. Rainbows, on the other hand, are produced not by scattering but by **refraction and reflection** inside raindrops, and they appear at roughly 42 degrees from the point directly opposite the sun. When light reflects twice inside the raindrops, a secondary rainbow with the reverse order of colors forms outside the primary one.

The video summed everything up as "just one principle," but rainbows involve refraction and reflection, and the blue of the sea comes from water absorbing red light, so those are somewhat separate stories from scattering.

### The sky on Mars: reddish-brown by day, bluish at sunset

Mars's atmosphere is much thinner than Earth's, and its main component is carbon dioxide. Fine dust, reddened by rusted iron in the ground, gets kicked up and drifts in the atmosphere, so according to NASA, the daytime sky would look like a hazy reddish color to the human eye.

In the evening, the sky around the sun turns bluish. NASA's Curiosity rover captured this sunset in color at Gale Crater on April 15, 2015. According to NASA's explanation, the fine dust on Mars lets blue light through relatively easily, so blue light stays in the part of the sky near the sun, while yellow and red light are scattered more widely. This effect is most noticeable around sunset, when light travels a long way through the atmosphere.

Note that it's the sky around the sun that turns blue; the whole sky doesn't change to blue. NASA's explanation for kids describes it as "blue-gray."
