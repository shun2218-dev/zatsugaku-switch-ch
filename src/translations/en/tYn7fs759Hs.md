### The blue of the sky is the color of "scattered light"

Sunlight looks whitish, but it contains all seven colors of the rainbow mixed together. When this light enters the atmosphere, it hits nitrogen and oxygen molecules and scatters in all directions. Because the molecules are much smaller than the wavelength of light, the "Rayleigh scattering" that occurs here **scatters shorter wavelengths more strongly**. The strength is inversely proportional to the fourth power of the wavelength, so by the numbers, blue light (about 450 nm) scatters roughly 5 to 6 times more readily than red light (about 700 nm).

The scattered blue light reaches our eyes from every direction in the sky, so the whole sky looks blue. The sky near the horizon looks whitish because the light travels a long distance and scatters many times along the way, mixing the colors back together.

### Why isn't the sky violet?

Violet scatters even more strongly than blue. The reason the sky still doesn't look violet is mainly explained by two things: "sunlight contains relatively little violet to begin with" and "the human eye is more sensitive to blue than to violet" (the UK Met Office's explanation also gives these two points).

In the video, we introduced a third reason: "violet light is absorbed in the upper atmosphere." However, what ozone high in the sky strongly absorbs is mainly invisible ultraviolet light, and visible violet light isn't greatly reduced there. **It's more accurate to think of the main reasons as two: the amount of light and the sensitivity of our eyes.**

### Sunsets, clouds, and rainbows each involve other mechanisms too

In the evening, sunlight travels a long way through the atmosphere, so blue light gets scattered and reduced along the way, leaving more red and orange light. Rather than "only red is left," think of it as "the proportion of red light increases." Dust and fine particles in the air also scatter blue light and deepen the red of the sunset.

Cloud droplets are much larger than the wavelength of light, so "Mie scattering," which scatters every color about equally, occurs, and clouds look white. Rainbows, on the other hand, aren't caused by scattering but by **refraction and reflection** inside raindrops, and they appear at about 42 degrees from the point directly opposite the sun. When light reflects twice inside the raindrops, a secondary rainbow with its colors in reverse order forms on the outside.

The video summed it all up as "just one principle," but rainbows involve refraction and reflection, and the blue of the sea comes from water absorbing red light, which is a slightly different story from scattering.

### The Martian sky: reddish-brown by day, bluish at dusk

Mars's atmosphere is much thinner than Earth's, and its main component is carbon dioxide. Fine dust, reddened by rusted iron in the ground, is kicked up and drifts in the atmosphere, so according to NASA, the daytime sky would look like a hazy, reddish color to the human eye.

In the evening, the sky around the sun turns bluish. NASA's Curiosity rover captured this sunset in color at Gale Crater on April 15, 2015. According to NASA's explanation, the fine dust on Mars lets blue light through relatively easily, so blue light stays in the part of the sky near the sun, while yellow and red light gets scattered more widely. This effect is most noticeable around sunset, when light travels a long way through the atmosphere.

Note that it's the sky around the sun that turns blue; the whole sky doesn't change to blue. NASA's explanation for kids describes it as "blue-gray."
