### It started as a "trial" in 1993

Under the golden goal, the first team to score in extra time wins on the spot. At the time, it was also called "sudden death."

Records from the IFAB (International Football Association Board) meeting in February 1993 show that FIFA and UEFA asked to trial it at youth tournaments they organized, and the request was approved. The procedure was: "The first team to score in extra time is the winner; if no goal is scored, the match is decided by penalty kicks."

It was later used at major senior tournaments too. In the final of the 1996 European Championship (EURO 96), Germany's Oliver Bierhoff scored four minutes into extra time, and the title was decided by the tournament's first-ever golden goal. The 2002 edition of the Laws of the Game lists both the golden goal and penalty kicks as ways to determine a winner.

The video's description of it as something "from the 1990s" is roughly right, but as a rule, it stayed on the books until 2004.

### UEFA switched to the "silver goal" first

One of the earliest critics of the golden goal was UEFA, European soccer's governing body. In 2003, UEFA trialed the "silver goal" in the finals of its club competitions. If a team was ahead when the first 15-minute period of extra time ended, that team won. If the score was level, play continued into the second period, and if that still didn't settle it, the match went to penalties. A goal didn't end the game; play continued until that 15-minute period was over.

A UEFA article cited comments like these as reasons for the switch:

- It put too much pressure on referees
- The team that conceded had no chance to come back
- Extra time tended to become **cautious (defensive) play**
- It also raised stadium safety concerns

A UEFA official explained that the silver goal would make for "a fairer conclusion." The silver goal was also used at the 2004 European Championship, and the Greece vs. Czech Republic semifinal was decided by a goal from Greece's Traianos Dellas at the end of the first period of extra time.

### In 2004, IFAB removed it from the Laws of the Game

At its Annual General Meeting on February 28, 2004, IFAB discussed how the winner of a match should be determined. FIFA proposed that "only methods approved by IFAB may be used," listing four: away goals, extra time, golden goal and penalty kicks. IFAB adopted the proposal **after removing the golden goal from the list**. Because the previous year's meeting had already decided that "only methods approved by IFAB may be used," the silver goal, which had never been in the Laws of the Game, could no longer be used either.

In fact, the 2004 edition of the Laws of the Game lists only three ways to determine a winner: away goals, extra time and penalty kicks. Today's Law 10 still describes extra time as "two equal periods, not exceeding 15 minutes each," and play continues to the end even if a goal is scored.

### The reason for scrapping it isn't clearly stated

The video said that "teams packed in on defense so much that games got boring, so IFAB abolished it." However, while IFAB's 2004 minutes record the decision to remove the golden goal, they don't give the reason.

"Games tending to become cautious" was one of the reasons UEFA gave when it switched to the silver goal in 2003. IFAB may well have shared that view, but it can't be confirmed from the official record. It's probably best to treat problems like "teams going defensive" and "no chance to come back" as the likely reasons, rather than established fact.
