### The white and the yolk set at different temperatures

Eggs set when heat changes the shape of their proteins (a process called denaturation). The white and the yolk contain different kinds of proteins, so they set at different temperatures.

According to a research summary from Kagoshima University, the yolk sets at 65–70°C (149–158°F), while the white needs to be heated to 75–80°C (167–176°F) to set. The numbers in the video—"the white starts to set at around 60°C and sets completely near 80°C" and "the yolk starts to set at around 65°C and is firmly set at 70°C"—fall roughly within these ranges.

Egg white is a mix of several proteins. One of them, ovotransferrin, is sensitive to heat and turns into a soft, whitish gel at around 65°C (149°F). Meanwhile, ovalbumin, which makes up more than half of the protein in egg white, is heat-resistant and won't set until the temperature gets high. That's why egg white can "turn a little cloudy but never quite set."

### What "the yolk sets first" really means

If you look closely at the numbers in the video, it's actually the white (at about 60°C) that **starts** to set first. The reason the yolk sets "first" in an onsen egg is that the yolk **sets completely at a lower temperature**.

If you leave an egg in water at around 65–68°C (149–154°F), the whole egg eventually reaches that temperature. That's hot enough for the yolk to set, but not hot enough for the heat-resistant proteins in the white. So the yolk sets thick and fudgy, while the white stays soft and half-cooked.

### "30 minutes" is how long it takes to heat the center

In a study published in the Journal of Cookery Science of Japan, researchers soaked eggs in water at 65°C, 68°C, and 70°C and examined the internal temperature and how the eggs set.

- It took 30 minutes at 65°C, 28 minutes at 68°C, and 27 minutes at 70°C for the center of the egg to get close to the water temperature.
- At 65°C, the white was still runny even after 210 minutes (3.5 hours).
- At 70°C, the white was no longer runny after 30 minutes.

The video's "about 30 minutes in water at 65 to 70°C" makes sense as the time needed to heat the egg to its center. But since the white stops being runny after about 30 minutes at 70°C, **if you want to keep the white soft and runny, aim for about 65–68°C (149–154°F)**. The results also vary depending on the size of the egg, its starting temperature, and the amount of water.

### Ordinary boiled eggs set "from the outside in"

As the end of the video explains, when you cook an egg in boiling water, the heat travels in from the outside, so the white on the outside gets hot first and sets. If you take the egg out before the heat reaches the yolk inside, you get a soft-boiled egg; cook it longer and you get a hard-boiled egg.

You could say the difference between an onsen egg and a boiled egg comes down to "the water temperature" and "whether you wait until the temperature inside the egg evens out."

### What to know about food safety

The shell or contents of an egg can, on rare occasions, carry Salmonella, a bacterium that causes food poisoning. According to materials from the Food Safety Commission of Japan, most Salmonella is killed by heating at 60°C (140°F) for 15 minutes, but heat resistance varies depending on the strain and the food.

When restaurants and other food businesses cook with eggs, they are required to heat them at 70°C (158°F) for at least 1 minute (or use a method with an equivalent effect), except when using eggs meant to be eaten raw that are within their best-before date. The temperature used for onsen eggs is lower than this guideline.

If you make onsen eggs at home, keep these points in mind:

- **Use eggs that are within their best-before date and have no cracks**: In Japan, the best-before date on eggs meant to be eaten raw is the deadline for eating them raw. Labels say that eggs past that date should be cooked thoroughly before eating.
- **Put eggs in the fridge right after buying them**: The storage guideline is 10°C (50°F) or below.
- **Eat them soon after making them**: A notice from Japan's Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare advises that egg dishes that aren't fully cooked should be eaten within 2 hours of starting to cook.
- **Take care with vulnerable people**: The Ministry of Health, Labour and Welfare's notice asks that raw eggs be avoided for older adults, infants and children 2 years old and under, pregnant people, and people with weakened immune systems, and that they be served thoroughly cooked egg dishes whenever possible. The guideline for thorough cooking is "until both the yolk and the white are firm," and onsen eggs don't meet it.
