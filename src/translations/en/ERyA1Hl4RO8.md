### The dullness on a 10-yen coin is a thin film on the copper

The 10-yen coin is made of an alloy called bronze. According to the Bank of Japan, it's 95% copper, with the rest being zinc and tin. When copper, the main ingredient, is exposed to air, a very thin film of copper oxide gradually forms on its surface.

A document from the Industrial Research Institute of Niigata Prefecture lists several causes of copper discoloration: oxidation (bonding with oxygen), sulfidation (bonding with sulfur), and coloring caused by light interference in an extremely thin copper oxide film, just tens to hundreds of nanometers thick. It explains that in ordinary air, a film of copper(I) oxide forms first, and once it gets thick enough, black copper(II) oxide also starts to form.

The video said the copper "bonds with oxygen in the air," but oxygen isn't necessarily the only cause of the dullness; sulfur compounds in the air and other substances can also play a role. The Japan Mint also notes that, given the nature of the materials, discoloration and corrosion of coins over the years is hard to avoid, and it cautions that **touching coins with your hands speeds up discoloration because of skin oils and tiny scratches**.

### Silver turns black because of sulfur

According to materials from the Canadian Conservation Institute (CCI), the black tarnish on silver jewelry and spoons is mainly a layer of **black silver sulfide**. It forms when silver reacts with sulfur-containing substances such as hydrogen sulfide in the air. Just as the video explained, with silver the main culprit is sulfur, not oxygen.

The CCI points out that every time you polish off tarnish, a little of the silver underneath gets worn away too. So it recommends prioritizing prevention, for example by storing silver in a sealable container along with anti-tarnish products, so it doesn't tarnish in the first place.

### Stainless steel is "iron with chromium"

Stainless steel is an alloy of iron mixed with about 10.5% or more chromium (and no more than 1.2% carbon). According to the Japan Stainless Steel Association, the chromium in stainless steel bonds with oxygen in the air to form a **"passive film" about 1 to 3 nanometers thick (1 nanometer is one millionth of a millimeter)** on the surface. It's an extremely dense, hard-to-peel film made of chromium bonded with oxygen and hydroxyl groups (pairs of oxygen and hydrogen).

Even if part of this film gets scraped away by a scratch or the like, it's quickly rebuilt as long as oxygen is present. The association's materials explain that it can repair itself "under ordinary conditions," and this is the mechanism behind what the video described as "repairs itself naturally." The association's materials also explain that the name Stainless Steel means steel that "doesn't rust" or "rarely rusts."

### It doesn't stay rust-free "forever"

The video said stainless steel "stays rust-resistant for as long as it lasts," but that depends on conditions. The Japan Stainless Steel Association says that, depending on the environment, the passive film can break down and corrosion can occur. Factors include the type of acid, pH (how acidic or alkaline things are), the presence of halogens such as chlorine, and temperature.

- **Salt (chloride ions)**: The film can be broken in spots, causing "pitting corrosion," where small dot-like or moth-eaten-looking holes form
- **Gaps**: "Crevice corrosion" is more likely in places like tight gaps between pieces of metal
- **Rust from other iron**: The association notes that if you leave a wet iron nail on a stainless steel sink overnight, a nail-shaped spot of red rust can appear. If this kind of "transferred rust" is left alone for a long time, it can progress into rust on the stainless steel itself

The association's care guide recommends wiping off everyday dirt with a neutral detergent or soapy water, and removing rust stains with a commercial stainless steel cleaner while they're still in the very early stages. **Not letting dirt or salt build up** is the best way to make stainless steel last.
