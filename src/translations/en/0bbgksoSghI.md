### Konjac is almost all water

Konjac (a firm, jelly-like food made from the konjac plant's corm, common in Japanese cooking) is far more watery than it looks.

According to the food composition database of Japan's Ministry of Education, Culture, Sports, Science and Technology, 100 grams of block konjac (made from refined konjac flour) contains 97.3 grams of water. Only a tiny amount is anything else.

So why doesn't it run like water instead of holding its shape? According to materials from Gunma University, the main component of konjac is a substance called **glucomannan**. It's a long, string-like substance made of many sugars linked together. These strings connect to form a fine mesh. The materials call this a "gel network."

Lots of water is held in the gaps of that mesh. That's why konjac is springy and jiggly even though it's mostly water.

### Freezing creates large ice grains inside

When you put konjac in the freezer, the water inside starts to freeze.

The Gunma University materials use diagrams to explain what happens at this point. The water that was scattered throughout the mesh gathers together and grows into large ice grains. In the materials' words, it's "coarsened ice." The ice grains merge with one another and keep getting bigger.

Glucomannan gets pushed out of the places that turn to ice. The displaced glucomannan is gathered around the ice and becomes tightly concentrated.

When water freezes, the same weight takes up more space. That's why ice floats on water. We cover this in more detail in "[Why do ponds and lakes usually freeze from the surface?](/trivia/mUS8cBfXQQI/)"

### The ice damages the mesh, and it doesn't recover

The Gunma University materials explain that the large ice grains "damage" the mesh. And even after thawing, it doesn't return to its original state.

The materials state that with regular konjac, freezing and thawing is "said to reduce its elasticity and make it spongy." They also show photos of konjac before freezing and after freezing and thawing side by side, and even by appearance alone they look like different things.

When the ice melts, the spots where it was become gaps filled with water. Around them, the mesh that was gathered and concentrated remains. This is thought to be what creates the sponge-like texture.

### It's not necessarily "it gets firm because the water drains out"

In the video, we said, "When it thaws, the ice melts and the water drains out, but the holes stay." Let's add a bit more detail here.

In a Gunma University study, researchers froze a gel made from glucomannan and measured its firmness and water content after thawing. Here's what they found:

- After freezing and thawing, it was firmer than before freezing
- Extending the freezing time from 1 hour to 1 day didn't change the firmness much
- The proportion of water was almost the same before and after freezing

In other words, in this experiment the gel got firmer even though not much water was lost. The main reason for the change in firmness seems to be that the ice alters the structure of the mesh, rather than a loss of water.

Of course, if you freeze and thaw konjac at home and squeeze it by hand, water will come out. And squeezing out the water makes it even chewier. The point is just that "the water draining out" isn't the only reason the texture changes.

Also note that this experiment was done on a gel made for research, not on the konjac sold in stores.

### Shimi konnyaku: konjac made by freezing

There's a food that has made use of this property for a long time: **shimi konnyaku** (frozen konjac) from Ibaraki Prefecture.

According to "Japan's Traditional Foods Encyclopedia" from Japan's Ministry of Agriculture, Forestry and Fisheries, shimi konnyaku is a food handed down in places like Daigo Town and Hitachiota City in northern Ibaraki. Here's how it's made:

- It's made during the coldest part of the year, from about mid-December to February
- Straw is spread across a field, and thin slices of konjac about the size of a postcard are laid out by hand, one at a time
- Water is poured over them, and they freeze overnight
- During the day, they slowly thaw in the sunlight
- This is repeated for about 20 days
- Finally, they're dried thoroughly over about a week

As the konjac is frozen and thawed again and again, its water drains out and it becomes spongy. It keeps so well that it's said to last for years when dry.

According to the same encyclopedia, shimi konnyaku soaks up broth like a sponge. That's why it absorbs flavor well in dishes like nishime (a Japanese simmered dish).

Today, only a handful of farms still make it. The encyclopedia explains that the number of producers dropped sharply starting around 1960, due to factors such as the harshness of working in the cold season and the aging of farmers.

### If you try it at home

The same change happens in a home freezer.

Some of you may have accidentally frozen konjac and found that it turned crumbly and chewy. That doesn't mean it went bad; it's the result of the mesh structure inside changing.

It won't go back to its original jiggly state, but because it has lots of gaps, it soaks up flavor more easily. Use it in simmered dishes or stir-fries to enjoy a texture different from regular konjac.
