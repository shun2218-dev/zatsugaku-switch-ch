### Snow on a slope stays put because of a "balance"

An avalanche is when snow piled on a slope flows down at a speed you can see.

According to an explainer from the Civil Engineering Research Institute for Cold Region, three forces act on snow on a slope:

- The force of its weight pulling it downward
- **Friction** with the ground (the force that resists sliding)
- **Bonding** between snow grains (the force that holds them together)

Normally, these three are in balance, so the snow stays where it is. When heavy snow falls, the snow gets heavier, the downward force increases, and the balance breaks. This is the kind of avalanche most people know from winter.

### Meltwater upsets the balance

The balance doesn't only break when weight increases.

The same explainer says avalanches also happen when snow melts from sunshine, rising temperatures, or rain, because the bonds between snow grains and the friction with the ground get weaker. Even if no snow is added, the snow starts to slide if the forces holding it in place weaken.

The Colorado Avalanche Information Center also explains that rising temperatures, strong sunshine, and rain falling on snow make the surface snow wet, and the more water there is, the more easily the snow falls apart. The Swiss WSL Institute for Snow and Avalanche Research SLF describes this as "a loss of cohesion between snow crystals."

### Adding a bit to what the video said

The video said that "meltwater seeps between the layers, the bonds between snow grains weaken, and the snow collapses." That isn't wrong, but more precisely, the type of avalanche depends on where the water goes.

- **When the snow near the surface loosens**: Wet snow starts moving from a single point and spreads out in a fan shape. The Colorado Avalanche Information Center says this type happens at or just below the snow surface. Rather than between layers, it's the surface snow itself that weakens.
- **When water pools at a boundary between layers**: According to the Swiss institute, a slab-like block of snow can start sliding because of an already weak layer or water that has collected at the boundary between layers.
- **When water reaches the ground**: The Civil Engineering Research Institute for Cold Region explains that in early spring, meltwater or rain reduces the friction between the snowpack and the ground, causing a **full-depth avalanche** (an avalanche that slides on the ground surface), in which the entire snowpack on the slope slides down.

In Japan, the full-depth avalanche is the type that's often in the news in spring. When the sliding surface is inside the snowpack, it's called a **surface avalanche**, and that type is more common after heavy snowfall in the depths of winter.

### "Temperature" is part of official warning criteria too

Looking at the criteria for the Japan Meteorological Agency's "avalanche advisories" shows how spring avalanches are viewed.

For example, under the criteria for Yamanashi Prefecture, a surface avalanche advisory applies when "there is 30 cm or more of snowfall in 24 hours and the weather is changing sharply." A full-depth avalanche advisory, on the other hand, applies when there is "a snow depth of 50 cm or more, a high temperature of 15°C or above (at the Kofu Local Meteorological Office), and 24-hour precipitation of 20 mm or more."

So it's not just heavy snow: warmth and rain are part of the official guidelines, too. Note that these criteria vary a lot from region to region and can't be applied directly to other areas.

### Why is the "afternoon" dangerous?

Avalanche Canada calls the change that happens in the mountains on sunny spring days a **melt-freeze cycle**: a daily cycle in which the snow melts during the day and refreezes as it cools at night.

In the morning, a hard surface crust frozen overnight (often 10 cm or more) holds the snow in place, so avalanches are unlikely. As the sun rises and things warm up, this crust softens and the upper snow takes on more water. Avalanche Canada notes the change can be fast, with major shifts in anywhere from a few minutes to an hour or two.

That's why experts recommend starting early. The Colorado Avalanche Information Center advises planning your route so you don't travel on or beneath very steep slopes in the afternoon, and moving while the snow surface is still cold and hard.

However, it isn't always "only in the afternoon."

- The timing depends on which way a slope faces the sun. According to Avalanche Canada, southeast-facing slopes become unstable first, and west-facing slopes later
- The danger can last into the evening
- According to the Swiss institute, after a cloudy, warm night the snow doesn't refreeze, so it can already be dangerous in the morning. When it rains, the effects show up regardless of which way a slope faces

### Know the warning signs

Avalanches sometimes give warning signs.

According to the Civil Engineering Research Institute for Cold Region, full-depth avalanches can be preceded by signs such as **snow wrinkles** (creases that form on the snow surface) or chunks of snow falling. Agencies in Colorado and Switzerland list signs to watch for such as small snowballs rolling down slopes or off trees and "pinwheels," clumps of snow that roll like wheels.

A snowy mountain on a sunny day may look inviting, but inside the snowpack, the forces holding it in place may be gradually weakening. Check the avalanche information and wrap up your activities before it warms up. That's the basic rule for spring mountains.
